# Captree dock talk: ocean's got some shoulder to it, so we're playing the inside game
*With the Bight rolling 2 to 3 feet and warm, the real action this week is happening inside the inlet and up in the bay, not out front.*

**CAPTREE, NY — July 13** — _by Mariana "Mari" Reyes, Zone Writer_

Guy pulls up to the fuel dock Thursday morning, boat still dripping, and he says to me, "Mari, I ran out to the reef and it was lumpy as hell, didn't even bother dropping a line." I told him — good, don't. That's basically been my answer to everybody this week. The Bight's been carrying a 2 to 3 foot swell on a long period, water out there sitting around 73 degrees, and while that's not dangerous by any stretch, it's enough push that the bass and the bait have both tucked themselves inside where the water's calmer and, frankly, more comfortable. When the ocean gets a little shoulder to it like this in July, I stop fighting it and start fishing the inlet and the bay instead. That's where the week's been made.

Fire Island Inlet itself has been the standout. On the last two hours of the outgoing, with that warm marsh water dumping out of the back bay creeks around Oak Beach and into the main channel, you get this current seam that sets up right off the Democrat Point side, and the bass have been parking on it like they've got a lease. I've been working 1.5-ounce bucktails, white or that chartreuse-white combo, casting up-current and letting it sink clean before I start a slow bottom retrieve back through the seam. Most of what's coming through is schoolie to low-slot, 22 to 26 inches, but we've picked a few overslot fish in the 34 to 36 inch range right at the bottom of the tide when the current's really ripping through the throat of the inlet. If you're throwing eels, same idea — dead-drift them through that seam and let the current do the work, don't retrieve too fast.

Fluke have been the more honest story this week — good, not great, and you have to work for it. The Robert Moses bridges, both the buoy channel side and the flats off the Coast Guard station, have been giving up fluke in the 3 to 5 pound range on the outgoing, with the odd 6 or 7 pound fish mixed in if you're covering ground. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow along the bottom in 25 to 35 feet — that's been the winning combo. What I'm noticing is the bigger fish are sliding a little deeper than they were in June, which tells me they're feeling that warmer water up top and getting comfortable a bit lower in the column. If you're marking bait on the sounder around 28 to 32 feet, that's where I'd put the bait down first.

Bluefish have shown up with some teeth behind them the last ten days, mostly working the rips off Democrat Point on the first two hours of the incoming, chopping through peanut bunker that are starting to push into the bay in real numbers now. If you see the birds working and the water nervous, that's your cue — a wire leader with a diamond jig or a bunker spoon will get bit fast, and these aren't small blues, we've had several in the 8 to 10 pound class that will absolutely test your drag. Good problem to have, and it's a nice change of pace when the bass get finicky.

Bay-side, porgies have been steady over the reef structure off Nicoll Bay and around the Sayville flats, small hooks, sandworms or clam, standard high-low rigs, and they'll keep you bent while you wait out a tide change. Weakfish talk has been quieter than I'd like this year — I've heard of a few being taken incidentally on bucktails worked slow along the deeper edges of the bay near Patchogue, but it's not the kind of numbers that make me tell you to go target them specifically. If you stumble into one, count your blessings and don't tell anybody exactly where.

The bigger pattern here, and why I keep steering people inside instead of out front, comes down to that ocean-to-bay temperature gap. The open Atlantic's holding warm and rough, which is uncomfortable water for bait to sit in, so everything — bunker, peanut bunker, spearing — is sliding into the calmer, structured water of the inlet and the bay mouth to get out of it. Bass and blues are following that bait right to the door. It's a pretty simple equation once you see it: rough ocean pushes forage inside, predators follow forage, and suddenly your best water isn't the water everybody's staring at from the beach, it's twenty feet off the jetty rocks at Democrat Point on a dropping tide.

Looking ahead, if that ocean swell settles down even a little by the weekend, I'd expect some of that bass activity to spread back out toward the surf line at Fire Island and Robert Moses beach, especially at first light when the water's calmest. Until then, I'd keep my plans flexible — fish the inlet seam on the outgoing for bass, hit the bridges for fluke on the same tide, and keep an eye out for bird activity for the blues. The moon's working toward the new phase later this month, which should juice up the current a bit more and tighten these bite windows even further. I'll be out there most mornings out of Captree chasing it — come find me at the dock if you want to compare notes.
