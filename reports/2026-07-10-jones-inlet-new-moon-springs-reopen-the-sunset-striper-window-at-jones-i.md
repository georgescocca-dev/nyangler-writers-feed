# New moon springs reopen the sunset striper window at Jones Inlet
*Bay water hit 79 degrees and pushed the fluke out to the channels — here's where they landed and why the bass bite comes back to life this weekend.*

**JONES BEACH, NY — July 10** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay cooked. Great South Bay pushed into the high 70s, touching 78.8 in the shallows off Wantagh and Freeport, and that kind of heat does exactly what you'd expect — it clears everybody out of the flats and pushes them toward moving water. We had a stretch of light southwest wind that kept the surface glassy through most of the week, which is pretty on the water but it lets the sun bake the bay even harder. Tides were still ramping down off the June full moon, so current was soft, bait got lazy, and so did the fish that eat it. It wasn't a bad week so much as a sleepy one — the kind where you had to go find them instead of waiting for them to come to you.

That changes fast. We're building into a new moon this weekend, and that means spring tides — bigger swings, harder current, more water moving through the inlet on both ends of the cycle. The piece that's got my attention is the timing: the PM flood is now lining up with sunset, which is the first time that's happened since the full moon back in June. That's not a small thing at Jones Inlet. When the high stand hits right as the light drops, bass push up out of the deeper inlet water and work the bar edges and the sod banks on Short Beach and the West End on the last of the light. Friday through Monday is the sweet spot for that alignment, with Saturday and Sunday looking like the peak. If you've only got one evening to fish this week, that's the window I'd take.

Fluke have already voted with their fins. With the bay pushing 79, the doormats and even a lot of the smaller fish have abandoned the flats around Middle Bay and Sloop Channel and dropped into the channels and the inlet proper, where the water's moving and staying cooler. The ocean side has actually been upwelling — cooler water, somewhere around 71, getting pulled up along the beach and into the inlet mouth on the outgoing. That's a nice setup: warm, slow bay water pushing bait out, cooler clean ocean water pulling fluke in to intercept it. I've been working the deep bend in the inlet channel just inside the Coast Guard station, 18 to 24 feet, dragging 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 5-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets on the outgoing tide. Slow drag, short hops, let it sit dead for a second at the bottom of each hop — that's when they've been eating. Mixed results, but decent ones: a few fish in the 4 to 6 pound range came out of there Tuesday and Wednesday, with plenty of shorts mixed in that make you work for your keepers. Sloop Channel itself has been quieter than usual for July — I think most of the better fish slid toward the inlet with the heat, so if you're used to drifting the flats there, shift your search toward the deeper cuts.

Stripers have been a grind but not a skunk. Bunker schools are scattered through the inlet and along the beach off Short Beach and Point Lookout side, and where you find a nervous pod getting pushed up on the surface, there are bass under it — just not every day, and not for long stretches. Best luck has come working topwater and darters over those pods early, before full light, then switching to bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig once the sun's up and the blitz shuts down. A few fish in the 15 to 20 pound class came off the jetty rocks at the inlet on the last hour of the incoming, and one angler I talked to at the ramp had a mid-20s fish on a fresh bunker chunk fished tight to the rocks on Short Beach at first light. That PM flood-sunset window this weekend should wake this bite up — bass that have been holding deep in the inlet on the slow tides should push up onto the bars and into casting range once that spring current starts moving real water again.

Bluefish have filled in the gaps nicely, and if you're just looking to bend a rod, this is your fish right now. Cocktail blues and some bigger ones are chopping up the same bunker pods the stripers are working, and they'll hit almost anything with flash — Kastmasters, Deadly Dicks, tin squids worked fast through the froth. They don't care much about tide stage, though the tail end of the outgoing at the inlet mouth has been the most consistent.

Weakfish talk keeps coming up at the dock, and I want to be straight with you — I haven't seen the numbers yet to call it a real comeback. A few have shown in the deeper holes of the bay off Freeport on chartreuse Gravity Tackle sand eels fished slow near bottom, and that's a good sign, but it's scattered, not a pattern. Worth a drift if you're already out there for fluke, not worth building a whole trip around yet.

Porgies have been steady and honest work off the Jones Inlet rocks and around the Short Beach rubble — bloodworms or clam on a standard high-low rig, incoming tide, nothing flashy but reliable if you want fish for the pan.

Looking ahead: the new moon springs are the story. Bigger tides mean more water moving through the inlet, which should reset the whole system — push bait around, wake up the stripers that went quiet on the slack cycle, and probably tighten up the fluke bite in the channel edges. If the wind stays out of the south and light like it's been, I'd fish the Friday and Saturday evening flood at the inlet first, with a fallback drift through the deep bend for fluke if the bass don't show. Either way, this is the best tide setup we've had in three weeks — worth building your weekend around.
