# Where Did the Bass Go? A Lesson in Warm-Water Behavior
*Daytime stripers have gone to school after dark, but fluke, bluefish, and weakfish are keeping the classroom open on the back bays this week.*

**OCEAN CITY, NJ — July 21** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get the same question every July, usually from a guy leaning on the rail at the Ninth Street bridge with a rod he hasn't bent since June: "Professor, where'd all the bass go?" It's a fair question, and it's got a fair answer. Water temp off the beach is sitting around 71 to 72 degrees, and in the shallow back bay guts around Brigantine and the Rainbow Channel behind Ocean City, it's running warmer than that by midday. Striped bass don't like working hard in warm, low-oxygen water under a July sun. They go nocturnal. That's not a mystery, that's biology — same lesson every summer, different year.

So if you want stripers this week, you fish the edges of the day. Dusk into dark around the Route 30 bridge pilings in Absecon Inlet, working a white paddletail on a 3/4-ounce jighead tight to the structure on the last two hours of the outgoing. Same story at the Great Egg Inlet railroad bridge — that current funnel holds fish after sundown, and a bunker chunk fished on the bottom with just enough weight to hold in the tide has been the more consistent producer than artificials there. Daytime stripers are there, but they're stacked deep and sluggish, and you're better off not wasting your best hours chasing them.

Which brings me to what daytime hours are actually for right now — fluke. This remains the most dependable program on this whole stretch of coast, and it hasn't slowed a bit with the heat. The sod banks off Absecon Inlet, in that 12 to 18 foot stretch along the edges of the marsh channels, are holding fish through the last of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing, when the current is strong enough to keep a 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a bucktail moving naturally along bottom without you having to add weight. Great Egg's channel edges, particularly the drop from the ICW into the inlet proper, have been giving up keeper fluke to 4 and 5 pounds on the same rig, sometimes better on a chartreuse/white combo when the water's got a little color to it after a tide change. Nothing extraordinary in numbers — you're working for your five fish, not filling a cooler in an hour — but it's steady enough that I'll take clients there with confidence most days this month.

Now, the bluefish. We flagged this last week — said the blitz pattern working the bunker schools up around LBI and Belmar would keep pushing south, and it has. Birds have been diving on bait pods off Ocean City and down toward Sea Isle City the last several mornings, and the blues chasing those bunker schools have been chopping bait right at the surf line in daylight, which is unusual enough to notice. If you see the gulls working tight to the beach at first light, get a metal lip swimmer or a Hopkins spoon out there fast — that window can close in twenty minutes once the bunker push through. It's not a guarantee every morning, but three mornings out of the last five have produced fish, and that's a good number for late July blues on the beach.

Weakfish are the quiet story right now, and I mean that literally — the bite's been a nighttime affair in the back channels around Stone Harbor and Townsends Inlet, calm evenings with little wind producing the best results. We noted last week that warm, settled evenings would rebuild that weakfish bite, and the calm stretch we've had has done exactly that. A 3-inch chartreuse Gulp shrimp on a light jighead, fished slow along the drop-offs where Townsends Inlet channel meets the marsh flats, has been picking up weakfish in the 16 to 19 inch range on the last two hours of the outgoing after dark. Not a blitz, but a real, repeatable pattern if you're willing to fish it at 9 or 10 at night.

Kingfish have been steady in the surf at Stone Harbor and the south end of Ocean City on bloodworm and Fishbites combos fished on a simple high-low rig in the second and third sandbar troughs — small fish, good eating, easy on a summer evening when the ocean swell (we've had a bumpy stretch, 3 to 4 feet at short period off the coast) makes bigger surf targets tough. Triggerfish continue to show around the Great Egg reef structure and the rock piles at the Absecon Inlet jetties, taking small pieces of clam fished tight to the rocks — a bonus fish more than a target, but worth knowing they're there if the fluke bite goes quiet on you.

Looking ahead, we're sliding into the first-quarter neap, which means softer current for the next several days. That's going to widen the productive fishing window at the inlets — less brutal peak-tide current means you can fish fluke and stripers comfortably through more of the tide cycle instead of just the last hard hour. If the bunker keep pushing south and the calm weather holds, I'd expect the bluefish blitz pattern to keep showing at Ocean City and work its way toward Great Egg by the weekend. Worth having a rod rigged and ready before the coffee's done.
