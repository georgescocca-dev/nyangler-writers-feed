# Biddeford Pool's Incoming Hands Up a 30-Pound Bass, Blues Rip Through Higgins
*Cooler Gulf of Maine water is keeping stripers feeding in daylight while the south chokes on heat — this is our window and it's open now.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — July 20** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Tuesday evening, half-tide and building, I was standing on the bar at Biddeford Pool with a Gibbs pencil in my hand and green water sliding past my knees. Third cast, the plug got walloped right at the edge of the channel where the current pinches down before it dumps into the Pool proper — a bass that pulled drag like it meant it and measured out at 30 pounds when I finally got a tape on her in the wash. That's the fish of the week for me, and it came exactly where it should have: the last two hours of the incoming, when bait gets pushed up off the flats and pinned against that gut running along the eastern shore. If you've fished the Pool long enough you know that window — it's the best hour of the Maine striper day, full stop, and it hasn't let me down yet this July.

Here's the thing that's making this stretch of coast worth fishing right now while the water south of the Cape turns into a bathtub: our water hasn't caught up to that heat yet. The Gulf of Maine is still running cool enough that these bass haven't gone nocturnal on us. Down in Jersey and the Sound they're feeding at dawn, dusk, and after dark because the shallows are too warm to hold fish through the middle of the day. Up here, I'm still getting bites at eleven in the morning off the rocks at Prouts Neck. That's a real edge for us, and I don't expect it to close up anytime soon — cooler water tends to hold that daytime pattern well into August around here, and I said as much last week. Nothing's changed my mind.

Pine Point has been the more consistent producer for schoolie-to-mid-slot bass, fished on the dropping tide out of the river mouth where the Scarborough River dumps into the marsh channel. Bucktails, half-ounce and three-quarter, white and chartreuse, bounced along the bottom in eight to twelve feet as the current picks up speed past the jetty rocks. Nothing huge — most of what's coming over the rail is 24 to 28 inches — but it's steady, and on a good outgoing you can pick away at a dozen fish in a couple hours without much competition on the water.

Bluefish are the story at Higgins Beach right now, and it's the kind of blitz you smell before you see — oily, fishy, and the gulls are already working before the first blue busts bait on the surface. Peanut bunker have pushed in tight to the beach on the higher stages of the tide, and the blues have been all over them mid-morning and again in the last light before dark. Nothing subtle needed — a bone or chrome Kastmaster, a Hopkins spoon, or a topwater popper thrown right into the melee will get bit almost every cast. Bluefish season is following the bunker exactly the way it's supposed to, and I've been telling folks for two weeks that as long as this bait stays put along the beach, the blues will keep coming with it. That's held up. I don't see any reason for it to break unless a hard blow scatters the peanut schools off the beach.

Prouts Neck fished well on the southwest wind we had over the weekend — that wind pushes warm surface water and bait up against the rocks on the ocean side, and the bass tuck in behind the boulders waiting for it to get shoved past them. I worked a black Danny plug along the edge there Saturday morning on a rising tide and connected with two fish in the high 20s before the wind swung and killed the bite around midday. If you get another southwest blow this week, don't sleep on that spot — just get there before the wind direction flips, because once it does the current along those rocks goes dead and so does the bite.

Mackerel are still thick off Wells Harbor and out toward the Cape Elizabeth ledges, and that's no small thing — that bait is what's keeping both the stripers and whatever cod are still lurking on the deeper structure happy. If you want fresh mackerel for bait or the table, small diamond jigs or a mackerel tree worked in 20 to 40 feet off the harbor mouth will fill a cooler fast, usually first thing in the morning before the boat traffic picks up. Flounder guys have been doing quiet, unglamorous work in the Kennebunk River and around Wells Harbor on sandworms and clam bellies, fished on the bottom on the last of the ebb — not exciting fishing, but if you want a mess of flounder for dinner, that's still a reliable play.

We're heading into a neap tide the next few days off Thursday's first quarter moon, which means less current push through the Pool and around Prouts Neck — the water will move slower, and that generally widens the bite window instead of compressing it into one hard hour. I'd use that to my advantage and fish the Pool an hour either side of what's normally the sweet spot, because with softer current the fish don't have to commit to the same tight window. My money this week is on the incoming at Biddeford Pool early, Higgins Beach for the blues midday if the bunker hold, and Prouts Neck again if we get one more southwest wind before the weekend. Bring the big plugs. This bite's earned them.
