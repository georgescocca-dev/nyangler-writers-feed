# Why are the bluefish only biting before 7 a.m.? Read the bunker, not the clock
*Peanut bunker pinned to the surf line are running the whole show this week — from Brigantine blues to Absecon fluke to the first real weakfish of the summer.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — August 14** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get some version of this question every August, usually from a reader standing on the Ocean City boardwalk at nine in the morning wondering why the blues that tore up the wash at six have vanished. The answer is always the same lesson: bluefish don't punch a clock, bait does. Peanut bunker have been pinned tight to the beachfront from Brigantine down through Sea Isle City, and those pods move with light and water temperature, not convenience. First light and the last hour of the outgoing tide is when the bunker get squeezed against the bar, and that's when the blues show themselves. By mid-morning the sun pushes the bait off the bar into deeper, cooler water, and the blitz you saw at sunrise just melts away. If you want consistency, you fish the tide, not the wristwatch.

We flagged this pattern last week — said to watch for surface blitzes on scattered peanut schools in the early window — and that's exactly what's playing out along this stretch of beach. Absecon Inlet's north jetty and the Brigantine surf have both given up solid runs of cocktail blues in the 2 to 4 pound class on white bucktails and small Got-Cha plugs worked fast through the wash. If you're metal-fishing, don't overthink the retrieve — these fish are eating on reaction, and a jerky, erratic presentation outproduces a slow swim every time right now.

Striped bass are riding the same bait wave, though down here they're more of a supporting character than the headline they've become up in the Sound and Buzzards Bay. We're seeing them stage on the same peanut bunker pushes, particularly around the Great Egg Inlet rips and the deeper water off the Ocean City Music Pier, feeding hard on the last two hours of the outgoing when current stacks bait against the pilings. Bucktails in the 1 to 1.5-ounce range, white or chartreuse, dropped to the bottom and hopped slow through the current seam, have been drawing slot fish in the 26 to 30-inch range. It's not a blitz down here — it's a tide-window bite, twenty, thirty minutes of good activity around the turn, and then it shuts off until the next cycle. The new moon on the 12th put some extra push behind these tides, and that stronger flow has sharpened the bite exactly the way I expected it would — more current means more bait gets funneled into a tighter lane, and the bass know it.

Fluke remain the bread-and-butter story in the back bays, and the program hasn't changed much from what I've been teaching all summer. The sod banks off Absecon and the channel edges inside Great Egg continue to hold the better grade of keeper, especially on the last two hours of the outgoing when bait gets pulled off the marsh and funneled through the deeper guts. I'm still running 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets in white or pink shad, paired with a 1/2 to 3/4-ounce bucktail depending on how hard the water's moving, drifted slow along the drop from 8 feet down to 14. Townsends Inlet and the Stone Harbor side channels have also been kicking out keepers in that 18 to 20-inch range, with the occasional better fish mixed in — nothing like the 12-pound doormat that got weighed up in North Jersey this week, but a 5 or 6-pounder off the Sea Isle City bridge structure isn't out of the question if you're covering ground and staying on fresh bait.

Weakfish have started showing again in modest numbers, mostly incidental catches on the same fluke drifts along the channel edges in Absecon and Great Egg — nothing you'd plan a trip around yet, but worth noting because a few years back this time of season was when the pattern started to build. If you connect with one, it's usually on the same Gulp/bucktail combo, just a little higher in the water column than where the fluke are holding.

Surf fishing for kingfish has been a quiet, honest bite in the cooler morning hours before the sun gets up over the dunes — bloodworm and Fishbites on a high-low rig, fished right in the first trough off Ocean City and Sea Isle City beaches, has been steady enough to fill a bucket if you're patient and willing to be out there by 6 a.m. It's not exciting fishing, but it's reliable, and on a week where the bigger bite windows are short and tide-dependent, reliable counts for something.

Looking ahead, I don't expect this pattern to shift much through the weekend. The bait is staying pinned to the beach on the current tide cycle, and as long as that holds, the bluefish and striper windows should stay tight but productive around first light and the outgoing turn. If the wind stays out of the southwest and doesn't stir things up too much, I'd put my time on the Great Egg rips for stripers on the last two hours of the outgoing, and the Absecon sod banks for fluke on that same water. Watch the moon phase easing off the new-moon push over the next week — as the tidal range narrows back down, expect these bite windows to stretch out a little longer, which is good news for anyone who can only get out for a couple hours at a time.
