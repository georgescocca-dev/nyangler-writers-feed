# Midnight Ebb at the Inlet: Bass Deep, Weakfish Working the Bridge Pilings
*The tide's building into the new moon and I'm choosing my hours accordingly — outgoing after dark at the inlet, incoming at dusk under Ponquogue.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — August 8** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

I made a decision on Tuesday night that cost me sleep and paid me back in stripers: skip the first two hours of the outgoing and wait for the back half, after midnight, when the current at the inlet really starts to move water off the flats. That's when the deep hole on the ocean side of the east jetty turns from a maybe into a sure bet. Two bass in the mid-20s, one push past 30 inches, all on a live eel drifted down along the rock face where the current cuts hardest against the wall. If you're fishing this spot on the front of the tide, in my experience you're just soaking bait in slack water and wondering why nothing's happening. Wait for the push.

That's the whole game at Shinnecock right now — timing, not just location. We flagged this last week, said the building tide into the August 12 new moon would sharpen the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's playing out at the inlet. The tidal range is stacking up night over night, and with it the flow through that gut between the jetties is getting stronger, pulling more bait and holding more fish tight to structure on the last two hours of the ebb. If you've got one night to burn between now and the new moon, burn it late — 11 PM to 2 AM outgoing, working bunker chunks or eels along the rocks, has been the most consistent window I've fished all month.

Ponquogue Bridge is running its own clock. That spot wants the opposite tide — incoming, right at dusk into full dark, when the current sets up along the pilings on the Hampton Bays side and the weakfish move up out of the deeper bay water to feed. I had a mixed night out there Thursday: three weakfish in the 18 to 20 inch range, all on bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, bounced slow off bottom in maybe 15 to 18 feet right where the shadow line from the bridge lights meets open water. Lost two more to short strikes that felt like blues, not weakfish — and sure enough, the bluefish have started showing up under those lights too, which tells me the bait's thick enough to draw both species into the same water. If you're throwing bucktails at the bridge right now, downsize a little — half-ounce to three-quarter-ounce heads move slower and stay in that strike zone longer, which matters more than color right now given how stained the bay's running after the last few days of onshore wind.

Fluke fishing in the bay has been honest work, not a gimme. The drift from the Tiana Beach flats down toward the canal mouth has produced keepers, but you're covering ground to find them — the fish are scattered, not schooled up tight the way they were in June. Four-inch white Gulp on a bucktail, dragged slow over the sand-to-mud transitions in 8 to 12 feet, has out-produced bare bait two to one for me this week. Best window has been the last two hours of the incoming, when clean ocean water is still pushing through the inlet and hasn't fully mixed with the warmer, murkier bay water yet. Wait too long into the tide and the water turns the color of coffee with milk, and the bite dies with it.

Porgies and blackfish are around the usual structure — the rocks off the inlet, the bridge abutments — but truthfully I haven't put much time into them this week. When the striper bite's on at night and the weakfish are showing at the bridge, that's where my hours go. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod during daylight, porgies on the inlet rocks with clam and squid strips will still do it, but it's a different kind of fishing than what's got my attention right now.

The canal's worth a mention because a few guys have been running through to the Peconic side chasing bait schools, and the current through there has been ripping hard on the bigger tides this week — which tracks with everything else. More water moving means more bait getting pushed and pulled through every choke point in this system, from the canal gates to the bridge pilings to the inlet jetties. That's good news if you're willing to fish the hours when the current's actually working for you, and mediocre news if you're set in your ways about fishing daylight and high water because that's when everyone else shows up.

Looking ahead to the new moon on the 12th, I expect the pattern to keep sharpening rather than change. Bigger tidal swings mean stronger current at the inlet, which should keep pulling bass into that deep hole on the late outgoing, and it should keep pushing bait up against the bridge pilings on the incoming for the weakfish. If the wind stays out of the south and doesn't stir the bay into chocolate milk, I'd plan on fishing the inlet late Thursday or Friday night around the peak of the new moon tide — that's when I expect the strongest flow of the week. Just don't sleep on the fact that this bite rewards patience and bad hours more than it rewards showing up whenever's convenient.
