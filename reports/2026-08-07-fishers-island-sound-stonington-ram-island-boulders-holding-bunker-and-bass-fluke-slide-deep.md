# Ram Island boulders holding bunker and bass — fluke slide deep, albies still a rumor
*The rock piles from Wamphassuc to Fishers Island are stacked with peanut bunker and school bass, but the first false albacore haven't shown — yet.*

**STONINGTON, CT — August 7** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

There's a pod of peanut bunker working the boulder field off Wamphassuc Point right now that I've been watching for four days straight, and it tells you everything about where this week sits. They come off the rocks on the last of the ebb, pinned tight against the granite where the current breaks, and every evening around dusk something big pushes through and scatters them into the air. That's your bass. Not blitzing, not showing on top for long, but they're there, working the same six or seven boulders like they're punching a clock.

This is the pattern now — deep structure, low light, bunker-driven. Water's sitting warm, high 70s across the Sound, and with that kind of heat the stripers want the shade and the cooler water down around the rocks, not up on the surface chasing bait in daylight. I've been poling the skiff over the Ram Island boulder field at first light and again right at dark, working the edges where the rock drops into eight to twelve feet, and that's where the fish are holding tight. Live-lined bunker on a 5/0 circle hook, no weight, just enough current to walk it back into the structure, has been the most consistent producer. If you want to go artificial, a 2-ounce bucktail with a white or chartreuse curl-tail trailer, worked slow along the rock seams on the last two hours of the outgoing, will find fish that the boat traffic hasn't already pushed off. Most of what's coming over the rail is schoolie to mid-20s, but there are better fish mixed in — I had one push 34 inches off the north side of Fishers Island working the tide change at Wicopesset Passage, right where the current stacks up against the rock pile on the ebb.

Fluke have done exactly what they do this time of year — they've abandoned the shallow flats and slid onto deeper structure where the bottom stays workable. Fishers Island Sound is holding up its end, and honestly it's one of the better fluke stretches in the whole Sound system right now. I'm finding them on the channel edges and humps between the mainland and the island, 25 to 35 feet, drifting bucktail-and-Gulp combos — white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a ¾-ounce bucktail — nice and slow on the last of the incoming when the current's still got some push but hasn't gone slack. Keeper-sized fish, some pushing 4 and 5 pounds, mixed in with a lot of short fish you'll have to sort through. Patience pays here more than speed.

Sea bass are still doing their reliable summer thing on the harder structure — the reef pockets off Watch Hill and the rock piles tight to Fishers Island's south shore are holding fish all day, not just on the tide change. Jigs tipped with squid strips, dropped right to the bottom and worked in short hops, keep them coming. It's not exciting fishing, but it's dependable, and on a slow bass evening it's saved more than one charter for me this summer.

The boulder fields themselves are quiet on the tautog front right now — that's a fall game for me, and these same rocks off Ram Island and Esker Point that I'll be picking apart with green crabs in October are just holding bass and bait at the moment. No sense forcing a bite that isn't ready.

As for albies — nothing yet. I've been watching the temperature breaks closely because this stretch of water, sitting right at the boundary between the warmer western Sound and the cooler open water off Fishers Island, is exactly the kind of edge that pulls the first scouting fish in. The gradient right now is steep — warmer readings toward New London, cooler out toward the open Sound — and that kind of contrast is what stacks bait and draws albies up the coast. I haven't seen a fin yet off the breakwater or around Ram Island, but I've got my 9-weight rigged and sitting in the rack, because when they show here it's usually fast and it's usually right on the temperature seam.

Tide's coming out of a soft neap stretch, and you could feel it on the water — less push, softer breaks around the boulders, bait sitting looser than it should. That's about to change. We're building back into stronger tidal flow over the next several days, and in a place like Fishers Island Sound, where the whole game is current breaking against rock, that matters more than almost anything else. Expect the bite at Wicopesset Passage, the Stonington Breakwater, and the north side of Fishers Island to sharpen up as that flow rebuilds. If I had one evening to fish this week, I'd take the last two hours of an outgoing tide at Wamphassuc Point with a bunker chunk soaked tight to the rocks, and I'd have the fly rod ready in case something silver and fast shows up chasing the same bait. Nothing confirmed on that front yet — but the water's got the right feel to it, and that's usually how it starts.
