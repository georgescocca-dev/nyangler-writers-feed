# Forty Miles East of Atlantis, a White Marlin Made the Whole Trip Worth the Diesel
*The bluefin slot fish held steady, the bigeye showed at the edge, and a marlin in the spread confirmed what we've been watching build all month.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 20** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the dock at Montauk at 3:15 Tuesday morning, running dark past the Point with about 340 gallons aboard and a hundred-mile shot to the east wall at Atlantis ahead of us. Six hours at a fuel-conscious 17 knots gets you there with enough in the tanks to fish the day and still have a cushion home — that's the math I run every time, because out here the fuel gauge decides how long you get to fish, not the bite. We were rigged for tuna, dredge out, teasers up, and by 10 a.m. we were working the 100-fathom curve where the water goes from a flat green-blue shelf color to that deeper cobalt you get when warm eddy water is pushing up over the edge. That's where it happened — a white marlin came up on the long teaser, walked on its tail for what felt like a full minute, then dropped off before the pitch bait ever got there. We didn't boat him. Didn't need to. That fish told us everything we needed to know about where the season's headed.

We flagged this back a few days ago — said the billfish migration would keep building through July, and there it was, right on the edge where the warm water's pushing in. That's not a fluke sighting. Guys working Veatch and the water off Block Canyon have been seeing the same thing — marlin showing in the spread, sometimes two or three fish a day, mostly whites, occasional blue reported further south. Nothing you can plan a whole trip around yet, but it's building, not fading, and that matters when you're burning the fuel it takes to get out here.

The bluefin have been the more dependable story. Since the trophy category closed July 3rd, the slot fishery — 27 inches to just under 47 — has held up well on both sides of the canyon country. We took two keeper bluefin trolling a mixed spread of cedar plugs and small Ilanders about a mile inside the 100-fathom line at Atlantis, fish in the 30-35 inch class, both on the same spread pass on the shady side of a bait slick. Boats running out of Block Island and Point Judith toward Veatch have reported the same size class, same pattern — small bluefin holding tight to bait, not scattered out over open water. If you're headed that way, don't overthink the spread. A basic 5-6 line troll with a chunk of ballyhoo behind a bird, a couple of skirted plugs, and a naked ballyhoo way back has been plenty.

The bigger story for those willing to run the extra hour or two is the yellowfin and bigeye building at the edges — Hudson, Wilmington, and Washington have gotten most of the attention, but the east canyons are seeing spillover as that warm Gulf Stream eddy water keeps pushing bait up onto the shelf. We marked bait balls stacked from 60 to 180 feet on the sounder working the temp break where the water jumped from a shelf-typical 69-70°F to better than 75°F in the space of half a mile — that's the kind of seam that holds fish, and it's worth working slow, even trolling back over it twice, before committing to a drift or chunk.

Wahoo and mahi have been mixed in as a bycatch more than a target — a few wahoo taken on high-speed spreads run wide off the shotgun position, 12-14 knots with a horse ballyhoo or a jet head lure, and mahi showing under any floating debris or weed line you cross on the way out. Nothing you'd plan the whole day around, but worth a pass if you see color change or grass on the surface.

Weather's been the deciding factor more than anything biological. Stable high pressure has held for close to a week now, and that's the only reason boats have been able to make the run to Atlantis and Veatch on a normal schedule instead of picking weather windows. The first-quarter moon on the 21st brings us into neap tide, which doesn't move canyon fish much directly — these fish are driven by current edges and eddy structure more than tidal stage — but it does mean less push at the inlets on the way out and in, which matters more than people think when you're running Montauk or the Race in the dark.

Looking ahead, if the high holds through the weekend like it's shaping up to, I'd expect the yellowfin bite to keep building rather than back off — the bait's there, the temperature break is holding, and boats aren't reporting the eddy sliding off yet. If I had one day this week, I'd run to the east wall at Atlantis early, work the 100-fathom edge where the color changes, and keep a long teaser out all day regardless of what's happening in the spread — that marlin showed us the bigger picture is still unfolding out there, whether or not we manage to close the deal on the next one.
