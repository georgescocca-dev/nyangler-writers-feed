# Where'd the Fluke Go? Down in the Guts at Great Egg, Not on the Flats
*Peanut bunker showed up in the back bays right on schedule, and the cocktail blues and weakfish followed them in.*

**ABSECON INLET, NJ — August 10** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

Every August I get some version of the same question at the dock: "Coach, I'm marking fish all over my graph but I can't buy a bite — what am I missing?" This week it's fluke guys asking it, and the answer is a lesson in water temperature, not luck.

Surf temps have pushed into the high 70s from Brigantine down through Stone Harbor, and that's shoved the fluke off the open sand flats where they were sunning themselves in June and packed them into anything that holds cooler water — channel edges, drop-offs, the deep guts. If you're still drifting the shallow flats behind Ocean City hoping for a repeat of spring, you're fishing empty water. The fish are there, they're just three to five feet lower and tucked against structure where the current keeps things moving.

Great Egg has been my go-to teaching spot this week for exactly that reason. The channel guts running 18 to 25 feet off the Longport side are holding a mixed bag of keeper-class fluke and a stack of shorts, and the pattern only works right on the tide changes — the last two hours of the outgoing into dead low, when the current is still moving enough to keep a 3/4-ounce bucktail with a 4-inch white Gulp swimming mullet ticking bottom without you having to add weight. Slow drifts, short hops, and don't be afraid to let it sit a beat longer than feels natural. The bite window is narrow but it's real — I put a 22-inch fish and two shorts in the cooler Tuesday morning working that exact stretch before the tide went dead and the bite shut off like someone flipped a switch.

Absecon Inlet is fishing the mirror image — better on the push of clean incoming water, not the outgoing. The sod bank edges along the Brigantine side in 12 to 18 feet are worth a look with live killies or spearing on a standard fluke rig, especially as that green ocean water floods in and pushes the murkier bay water back. It's not a red-hot bite. I'd call it honest — enough fish moving through on the tide to make the trip worthwhile, but you're covering water and culling through shorts to find your keepers, not limiting out on the first drift.

Striped bass are behaving exactly like you'd expect with water this warm — they've gone nocturnal on us. Daytime action around Absecon Inlet and the Brigantine jetty rocks has been thin to nonexistent outside a brief window right at first light. If you want bass, be on the water before the sun clears the horizon, working bunker chunks or a swimming plug along the current seams where the inlet rocks meet moving water. Once the sun gets up and that water starts to warm even more, the fish drop down and go quiet until dusk brings them back up.

Here's the part I've been waiting to write about. Two weeks ago I flagged that the peanut bunker moving into these back bays would pull the cocktail blues and weakfish in behind them — and that's exactly what's showing up now. Townsends Inlet has had genuine peanut bunker pods stacking up on the outgoing tide, and the bluefish chewing through them are running two to four pounds, a real step up from the snapper-class fish that have been the summer standard in the back bay channels behind Ocean City and Sea Isle. Small epoxy jigs and quarter-ounce Kastmasters worked fast through the peanuts are doing the damage. Weakfish are riding the same bait wave — not a big showing yet, but legitimate keeper-class fish have turned up in the channels behind Stone Harbor and Sea Isle on bucktails tipped with a strip of fluke belly, fished slow along the bottom where the bunker schools are getting pushed by the current.

Kingfish are still working the surf wash at Ocean City and Stone Harbor beaches, same as they have been all summer, but the heat has them on a dawn-and-dusk clock too. Bloodworms or Fishbites on a high-low rig in the first wash line right after sunrise is still the most reliable read — once the beach gets crowded and the sun climbs, that bite fades fast.

We're coming off a full moon, and you can feel it in the current — the tides have been running stronger and the bite windows sharper than normal, which is exactly why timing the turn at these inlets has mattered more than usual this week. That extra push is what's been dragging the peanut bunker and the predators following them through the inlets on schedule. Expect that current strength to ease off gradually over the next several days as we move away from full, which should relax the bite windows back toward a more typical late-summer rhythm — less explosive, but more forgiving if you can't hit the exact tide change.

Looking ahead, I'd keep working the inlet drop-offs and back bay channel edges on the tide changes rather than the open flats — that pattern isn't changing while surf temps sit in the high 70s. If the afternoon sea breeze builds like it typically does mid-August, expect some chop and discolored water on the open beach fronts by midweek, which would push me further into the protected inlet structure at Great Egg and Townsends rather than fighting it on the sand.
