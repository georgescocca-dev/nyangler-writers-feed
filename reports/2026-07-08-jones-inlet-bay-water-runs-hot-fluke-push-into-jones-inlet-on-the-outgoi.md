# Bay Water Runs Hot, Fluke Push Into Jones Inlet on the Outgoing
*Spring tides build toward the new moon this weekend, and the fish that matter are following the current out of a bay that's gotten too warm for comfort.*

**JONES BEACH, NY — July 8** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay told you everything you needed to know just by looking at it. Hempstead Bay and the Great South Bay side pushed up into the high 70s — I heard 78.8 off one of the buoys inside, and that's warm enough to shut fluke down on the flats where they'd been sitting all June. When the bay gets that soupy, those fish don't sit around and suffer. They move. And where they move to, every single year, is the channels and the inlet mouth, where the outgoing tide is dragging cooler, cleaner water off the ocean side back through. That shift has already started, and it's the single biggest thing driving my week.

This week the moon is doing its part too. We're building toward a new moon around the 11th to 13th, which means bigger tidal swings — stronger current through the inlet on both ends of the cycle. Bigger outgoing means more bait getting flushed out of the back bay and stacked up right where the channel necks down past Short Beach and the Jones Inlet jetties. Bigger incoming at night means bunker and spearing pushed back up into the guts, which is when the bass that are holding around structure decide to feed hard instead of just sipping. Wind's been mild out of the south-southwest most days, no real blow to roil things up, so the water clarity on the ocean side has stayed decent — that upwelled water running around 71 degrees is a lot more comfortable for a fluke than the bathwater they were sitting in two weeks ago.

So here's what that means on the water. Sloop Channel and the West Channel drift have both turned back on for fluke, and it's specifically an outgoing tide bite — you want that current moving good, dragging your bucktail with the flow instead of fighting it. I've been running 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with a 5-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullet in 18 to 25 feet, slow-dragging bottom, and there's a deeper pocket off Short Beach pushing 30 feet where the bigger fish have been holding — that's where I picked up a 6-pounder Tuesday on a chartreuse bucktail with a Gulp Nemesis trailer. Not every drift produces. I made probably a dozen passes through Sloop Channel over two days to put together four keepers and a handful of shorts, so don't expect a five-minute limit. But the fish are there and they're eating, which is more than I could say for the bay two weeks ago.

Striped bass are still around Jones Inlet, mostly the resident summer fish holding tight to the jetty rocks on both sides, and the play right now is bunker. There's still bait moving through — not the fall blitz-scale schools, but pods big enough to hold fish under them. I've had the best luck snagging a bunker off the east jetty on the tail of the incoming and drifting it back with a fish-finder rig into the current seam where the rocks meet the channel edge. Night tide, especially that push right around and after high water, has been the window — a couple of fish in the mid-20-inch range and one better fish pushing 34 inches came off that program this week. Daytime has been mostly quiet for bass unless you're right on the structure at first light.

Bluefish have been mixed in under those bunker pods, nothing huge, mostly cocktail blues in the 2 to 4 pound range, but they'll hit a diamond jig or a bunker spoon worked fast along the jetty tip at first light, and they'll save a slow morning.

Weakfish are the one I keep getting asked about, and I want to be straight with you — the comeback everyone's hoping for hasn't shown up in the reports yet, at least not around here. Water temp alone isn't the trigger people think it is; the theory on Delaware Bay weakfish this summer was that warm water above 70 degrees would flip the switch, and it's been sitting there for over two weeks with basically nothing to show for it. That tells me temperature is necessary but it's not the whole story — there needs to be bait concentration and the right current to go with it. If you want to gamble on weakfish, I'd try the deeper holes near the Wantagh Bridge on the last two hours of the outgoing at dusk, working a bucktail tipped with a strip of squid slow along bottom. Don't build your whole trip around it yet.

Porgies have been steady and reliable if you just want meat for the cooler — the reef structure off the Jones Inlet sea buoy has been giving up 9 to 12 inch porgies all week on standard high-low rigs with clam, fished right on bottom in 20 to 25 feet. Not glamorous, but it's a bite you can count on when everything else is being stubborn.

Looking ahead, I'm watching that new moon spring tide build through the weekend. If the outgoing keeps flushing bait like it has, I expect the fluke bite at the inlet mouth and Sloop Channel to hold or even improve, and I want to be at the jetty right at that peak night flood on the 11th through 13th when the current's ripping hardest — that's historically when the bigger resident bass decide to really commit. If the wind stays out of the south like it has, the ocean side should stay fishable and clean. My plan for the weekend is jetty bunker at night for bass, fluke drift on the morning outgoing, and I'll keep a rod rigged for weakfish just in case that back bay current finally flips them on. Worth the gas, not worth betting the whole trip on any single species right now.
