# Poor Man's Canyon bluefin bite heats up as thermal breaks stack bait
*Mid-Atlantic canyons firing on all cylinders with yellowfin, wahoo, and white marlin joining the party.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — June 25** — _by Capt. Ray Lugo, Zone Writer_

The Southern Canyons are absolutely on fire right now, and I'm telling you — this is the kind of fishing we dream about all winter long. Poor Man's Canyon has been the star of the show this week, with bluefin tuna finally showing up in numbers that make the fuel burn worthwhile. We're talking 40 to 80-pound fish coming over the rail on chunked butterfish and fresh squid, with the best action happening in that 200 to 400-foot zone where the thermal break is setting up.

The water temperature gradient is what's making this whole show possible. We're seeing a beautiful 6-degree break running along the canyon wall — 72-degree blue water on the offshore side dropping to 66 degrees as you work inshore. That temperature differential is stacking bait like you wouldn't believe, and the bluefin are capitalizing on it. I've been running my spread with three chunk lines and two trolling rods, working that edge methodically. The chunk bite has been best on the incoming tide, especially that first two hours when the current starts to slack.

Spencer Canyon has been producing consistent yellowfin action, with fish in the 30 to 60-pound class hitting spreader bars and cedar plugs. The key has been staying in that 68 to 70-degree water and watching your fishfinder for the bait clouds. When you mark those dense schools of squid and butterfish at 80 to 120 feet, that's where you want to set up. I've been having my best luck with green machines and blue-white Ilanders on the long riggers, pulling at 7 to 8 knots.

The wahoo bite at Lindenkohl has been absolutely insane. These fish are coming up hot on high-speed trolling, and I'm talking about legitimate 40 to 60-pounders that'll smoke 400 yards of line before you know what hit you. Wire leaders are mandatory — I learned that the hard way Monday when a nice fish bit through 80-pound fluorocarbon like it was sewing thread. Rapala X-Raps in the blue-silver pattern have been deadly, especially the 30 size pulled at 12 to 15 knots along the canyon edges.

Mahi mahi are scattered throughout the zone, but the best concentrations have been around any floating debris or weed lines you can find. Norfolk Canyon produced a nice haul of gaffers yesterday, with fish to 25 pounds coming on ballyhoo and small feathers. The trick has been slowing down when you find the birds working — these fish want a more natural presentation than the high-speed wahoo program.

White marlin are starting to show up in better numbers, particularly around Spencer and Poor Man's. We're seeing fish in that 60 to 80-pound range, with most releases coming on circle-hook ballyhoo rigs and small lures. The bite has been best during the midday hours when the sun gets high and the bait starts to scatter.

The bait situation is what's really driving this whole bite. We've got massive schools of butterfish, squid, and small blueback herring throughout the canyon zone. The butterfish are particularly thick around Poor Man's and Spencer, which explains why the bluefin bite has been so consistent there. I've been catching my own bait on the way out — a few drifts with sabiki rigs in 100 to 150 feet will usually load you up with enough butterfish to chunk all day.

Looking ahead, this new moon cycle should keep the bite strong through the weekend. The spring tides are going to move a lot of water, which should keep the bait active and the predators feeding. I'm particularly excited about the Norfolk Canyon area — the water temperatures there have been climbing steadily, and I think we're about to see that zone really come alive with yellowfin and bigeye action.

The key to success out there right now is reading the water and staying flexible. Don't get married to one canyon or one technique. The fish are moving with the bait, and the bait is following those temperature breaks. Keep your eyes on the sounder, watch for the birds, and be ready to adjust your spread based on what you're seeing. This is prime time fishing, and it's only going to get better as we push deeper into summer.
