# Rocky Point bunker balls draw tight — bass are starting to lean on them
*The bay's holding bait like it hasn't all summer, and the guys on the pier say the blitz is close but not here yet.*

**PROVIDENCE, RI — September 7** — _by Vinny Ciervo, Zone Writer_

Down at the Providence River piers this week the talk wasn't about what's biting — it was about what's showing. Bunker balls have been stacking tight off Conimicut and Rocky Point for about ten days now, tighter than anybody's seen since June, and every guy leaning on a rail with a snag hook in his hand has been watching them pinwheel on the surface waiting for the water to blow up. It hasn't yet. Not consistently. But the pieces are lining up the way they do right before it does.

We flagged this last week — said the bunker building inside the bay was mirroring what was happening in Raritan Bay and along the LI south shore, and that this was one of the zones to watch for the first real fall blitz in southern New England. That's exactly what's setting up now. The full moon that just passed on the 6th and 7th tightened those schools even further, pulling bait off the flats and into tighter, nervier balls along the edges at Conimicut Point and down through the East Passage toward Prudence Island. The next real trigger is the new moon tide rolling in around September 10, and that's the date everybody on the pier has circled. New moon tides push more water, more current, more push behind the bait — and that's usually when the stripers stop picking at stragglers and start committing.

Right now the bite on bass is honest but patchy. Fish are working the bunker schools at first light and again on the last couple hours of daylight, mostly hanging under and around the edges rather than exploding through them. The better window has been the top of the incoming at Nyatt Point and the start of the outgoing off Conimicut, where current starts moving bait past the point and stripers slide up on the seam to intercept it. Snag-and-drop bunker is still the most consistent producer — snag one off the school, let it flutter down under the commotion, and hang on. Guys working topwater — big walk-the-dog swimmers and poppers — have had success early and late when the surface gets nervous, but midday with the sun high has been dead water. If you're throwing metal, a bunker-spoon or a heavy Ava jigged through the bait ball has picked up a few slot fish, but nobody's calling it a blitz yet. It's a bite in progress, not a bite arrived.

Bluefish have been the more reliable player on those same bunker schools — aggressive, chopping through the pods off Rocky Point and out toward the mouth of the bay, and they'll hit just about anything that moves fast through the bait. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig off the bottom near the pods have worked too, especially on slack tide before the current picks back up.

Scup fishing has stayed the most dependable thing going on the bay right now, and it's not close. The rocky ledges off Nyatt Point and down around the Rocky Point breakwater are holding scup stacked in 15 to 25 feet, and they're eating squid strips and sandworms on standard high-low bottom rigs without much fuss. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod for an evening, that's still the play — just get there on the tide change, because scup shut down hard on a dead slack.

Fluke are winding down, the way they always do this time of year, but there's still a window left in Greenwich Bay and the channel edges around Prudence Island. Depths of 12 to 18 feet on the drift, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, working slow along the channel drop-offs where the bottom transitions from mud to sand — that's been the pattern for the fish still around. Numbers are down from July, but the size has actually ticked up, which is typical as the fish that are left start staging to move out toward the mouth of the bay before they push offshore.

Out past Newport and around the lower bay mouth there's been word of albies and bonito showing, which tells you the water temperature and bait situation is lining up the way it does in a normal early-September transition. That's a different game than the bay proper — smaller, faster tackle, epoxy jigs and small metal — but it's worth knowing it's happening, because when those fish push it usually means the whole system is waking up at once.

Looking ahead, the next few days should be about patience more than urgency. The bunker are here, the bass are interested, and the new moon tide on the 10th is the date that should tip this from a picking bite into something with teeth. If the wind stays out of it and the current keeps building through the week, Conimicut Point and Rocky Point are the two spots to be working the tide changes — dawn and dusk, snag bait or bucktail, and don't be surprised if by the weekend the surface starts doing what it's been threatening to do all week.
