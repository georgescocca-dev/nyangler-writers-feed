# 138 Feet on Barnegat Ridge: One Bluefin Bite for Every Twenty Trolls, But Sea Bass Won't Quit
*The warm edge we flagged two weeks back never left — it just moved the tuna bite inshore and left the sea bass biting steady on the reef structure.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 17** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

138 feet. That's the number I keep coming back to this week — the depth at the northeast corner of Barnegat Ridge where the bottom breaks hard and the current stacks bait against the rise. I've fished that exact contour in Augusts going back a decade, and when the numbers line up the way they have lately, it tells me more than any single report can. This week it told me the tuna are still around, but they're not easy, and the sea bass are the sure thing.

Let's start with what's dependable. Shrewsbury Rocks in 45 to 65 feet has been holding jumbo sea bass all week, the kind that make you re-tie because the 20-pound fluoro leader looks thin all of a sudden. Squid strips over a double-hook bottom rig, six-ounce bank sinker to hold in the current, and don't be shy about adding a strip of clam to the top hook — the bigger fish on that structure have been picking at the clam scent first. Sea Girt Reef in a similar depth range, 50 to 70 feet, is producing the same story: good numbers, decent average size, but you have to work the wreck pieces tight. Sea bass this time of year sit right on the structure edge, not scattered over open sand, so if your drift isn't ticking bottom right on the pile, you're just burning bait. Axel Carlson Reef has been a little more hit or miss — some drifts stacked with keepers, others dead — which tells me the bait pods are moving around out there more than usual, probably pushed by the same warm-water intrusion that's been messing with the tuna grounds farther out. Triple Wrecks rounds it out as a solid backup when the others go quiet; smaller average fish there but more consistent action, good for filling a cooler on a day when you need numbers over size.

Now the tuna. I'll be straight with you — it's been a tough bite. We flagged two weeks ago that the warm-water edge sitting off our grounds showed no sign of retreating, and it hasn't. What that warm pool has done, though, is thin out the bite on the deeper lumps that usually carry bluefin this time of year, and pushed what action there is inshore onto structure like Barnegat Ridge and the Klondike humps. Boats working that 100- to 160-foot range on the Ridge and the Klondike this week are getting looks — spreader bars and cedar plugs pulling small to mid-size bluefin, with the occasional yellowfin mixed in on the temperature break — but the hookup rate has been low, something like one solid strike for every twenty trolling passes. That's not a bite you plan a whole day around. It's a bite you fish on your way to or from the reef structure, trolling the edges for twenty or thirty minutes before you commit to the sea bass grounds. If you connect, it's a bonus. If you don't, you haven't lost the day.

Mahi have been the pleasant surprise tucked into that same water. Anywhere you find a temperature seam or a scrap of floating structure near the Ridge or Klondike drop-offs, small pods of mahi have been sitting under it — schoolies mostly, two to four pounds, but eager. Small chunks of butterfish on a light spinning rod, no weight, just cast to the shadow line and let it flutter down, has been the move. Nothing that fills a fishbox on its own, but it turns a slow troll leg into a few extra fish in the cooler, and it's fun fishing on a day when the bigger stuff isn't cooperating.

On tog — nothing to report, because there's nothing to report legally. Ocean blackfish season is still closed here, so if you're marking fish on the wreck pieces at Shrewsbury Rocks or Triple Wrecks, write the number down and forget about it until October. That patience pays off; the fish that are sitting tight to structure right now, untouched, are the same ones that'll be biting hard the week the season opens.

The tide's been building back toward the August 28 full moon, and that's the number I'm watching hardest for the coming week. Stronger tidal push usually means better current over the reef structure, which tightens up the sea bass bite and gets more bait moving through the Ridge and Klondike corridor — which in turn is what it'll take to turn that one-in-twenty tuna strike rate into something better. If the warm pool holds its position rather than sliding east or west, I'd expect the inshore tuna pattern to stay roughly where it is — spotty, but present. If it shifts, the whole picture changes fast, and that boundary is the single thing worth checking before you commit fuel to a long troll leg versus a straight run to the reef structure.

My plan for the next few days: mornings on Sea Girt Reef and Shrewsbury Rocks for sea bass while the water's calm, a troll pass through the Ridge and Klondike on the way back in to test the tuna, and a rod rigged light for mahi under anything floating. That's a full day's fishing with a real shot at three species, and it doesn't depend on one bite carrying the whole trip.
