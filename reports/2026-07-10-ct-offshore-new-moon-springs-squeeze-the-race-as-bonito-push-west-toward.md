# New moon springs squeeze The Race as bonito push west toward the Sluiceway
*Tighter slack windows at Valiant Rock, a mixed bass bite on wire, and the first real bonito sniff east of Plum Gut.*

**THE RACE, CT — July 10** — _by Capt. Sabrina Cole, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound did what it does after a blow — it sorted itself out slow. We had a stretch of NE flow that sharpened the breaks out past Plum Gut and put some cooler, cleaner water back into the mix after that stretch of thick, warm surface water sat on us through the holiday run. That NE push is the same mechanism that's been sharpening the 20 to 30 fathom breaks offshore for the bluefin crowd, and closer to home it did us a favor — it knocked back some of the surface warmth that had been shoving bait deep and scattering the bass off Race Rock. The bigger story, though, is the cold wall between Block Island Sound and Rhode Island Sound falling apart. That wall has held bonito on the Block Island side for years. It just took a double-digit jump in RIS and it's not holding anymore, which means the corridor's opening up west — Newport and Point Judith are seeing early bonito, and I don't think it stops there. Little Gull and the Sluiceway are next in line if that trend holds.

This week the moon's the story that matters most for how you fish the rip. We're running into new moon springs, peak alignment landing Friday through Monday, and that means bigger exchange, faster water, and a slack window at Valiant Rock that's going to be shorter and meaner than it's been in weeks. When the tide's moving five knots through The Race on a spring cycle, the difference between fishing the turn right and fishing it forty minutes late is the difference between a rod-bending bite and a long boat ride home. High water at New London is running late afternoon into evening the next few days, and slack at Valiant Rock is stacking up right around sunset — call it 8:15 to 8:35 as the week rolls on. That's your window. Don't show up early and don't linger — spring tide current fills back in fast once it turns.

The bass bite has been honest, not easy. We've been working wire line with bucktails — the 2 to 3 ounce bucktail with a strip of pork rind or a white Fin-S trailer, dropped right on the edge where the rip meets the slick water off Valiant Rock, 25 to 40 feet down depending on the stage of tide. The pattern's been: stack up on the last twenty minutes of the ebb, hold through slack, and fish the first hour of the new flood before the current gets too strong to hold bottom without going to lead you don't want to lift. Fish have been mixed — plenty of schoolies in the 20 to 26 inch range mixed with a decent number of stripers in the high 20s to low 30-inch class, a few pushing 15 pounds. Nobody's filling a cooler with slot fish right now, but if you time the slack right you're not getting skunked either. Eels have worked too when you can find them — slow-trolled or drifted right through the seam at Race Rock on the last of the ebb has produced the better fish of the week.

Bluefish have been the more reliable player through Plum Gut and along the eastern face of the Sluiceway. Poppers and darters at first light, before the current builds, have been drawing choppers up in the 4 to 8 pound range, and once the tide gets moving they'll hold right in the rip current where the bait gets pinned. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod some mornings, that's been it.

Fluke have moved with the water temperature, same as always. Great South Bay's been running warm enough to push the better fish out of the bay and into the channels and inlets, and the upwelled water on the ocean side — clean, cooler, sitting around 71 degrees — has been the ticket. Plum Gut and the edges of The Race have both been holding fish in that 25 to 40 foot range on the outgoing tide. White and chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4 to 1-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the drift, have taken fish to 5 pounds, with a decent number of shorts mixed in. It's not a wide-open bite, but it's a legitimate one if you work the drift patiently instead of running and gunning.

Black sea bass have been steady around the structure at Valiant Rock and Race Rock — jigs tipped with squid strips or just plain bucktail jigs bounced bottom in 30 to 50 feet have been producing keeper-class fish in the 16 to 18 inch range without much trouble. That's been the most dependable meat in the boat this week when the bass and fluke bites went quiet on the wrong tide.

On the bonito, I want to be straight with you — nobody's connected inside the Gut yet. The corridor's opening from the east, and with the cold wall gone in Rhode Island Sound, the fish have room to run west toward Little Gull and Great Gull over the next week or two if the warm water keeps pushing. I'd have an epoxy jig rod rigged and ready — Deadly Dicks, Hogy epoxy jigs, anything in a sand eel profile — and I'd be checking the rip edges off Great Gull on the last of an incoming tide, because that's where the bait's going to get squeezed first if the corridor keeps sliding this direction. It's not a sure thing yet. But the water's telling me it's close.

Look, the springs are going to make this week technical. Fish the slack windows tight, respect how fast that current fills back in at Valiant Rock, and don't be surprised if the bass bite stays honest-but-mixed rather than explosive. If the bonito corridor keeps widening the way it has, I think we're a week, maybe two, from the first real hookups inside our water. Until then, wire line at first light, fluke on the ocean side incoming, and keep an epoxy jig within reach just in case.
