# Manasquan Rocks at First Light: Bass on Bunker, Gone by Sunrise
*The stripers are a flashlight fishery right now — but the full moon tide is turning the fluke bite into the best reason to be out there.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 10** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Four-forty in the morning, low tide just starting to turn, and there were already two guys camped on the north jetty at Manasquan Inlet when I pulled up — headlamps off, standing dead still, watching a bunker pod nervous up against the rocks. That's the tell this time of year. You don't hear stripers in August, you feel the water change. One guy was working a Super Strike Little Neck popper, slow cadence, letting it sit dead in the wash between twitches. Took him maybe fifteen casts before something T-boned it right at the base of the jetty in maybe six feet of water. Fish went about 27 inches — good keeper, not a cow, but a real fish on a real cast, and by the time the sky started going gray he'd already put his rod down. That's the whole window right there. Fifteen, twenty minutes of gray light before the sun gets over the horizon, and then it's over. The water's too warm for these fish to keep working the rocks once there's light on it — they're pushed into a strict low-light pattern from Sea Bright down to Manasquan right now, and if you're not on the rocks casting before 5:15, you missed it.

Sea Bright's been the better bet for the dawn bass lately, honestly, because that stretch sits close enough to the Hook that it's catching the tail end of the bigger bunker schools working down from Raritan Bay. Guys throwing bunker-colored Danny plugs and darters off the rocks near the old fishing pier have had short windows of real violence — blitzy, five-minute flurries when a school gets pinned against structure — but it shuts off fast once the sun's up and the bait scatters. If you want a shot at that, fish the last hour of the outgoing on a falling tide when bunker get squeezed against the jetty face, and be ready to leave your rod rigged the second you see nervous water.

Once the sun's up, though, the real story on this beat is fluke, and it's been the more honest fishery all week. We're coming off the full moon and that extra tidal push has sharpened the drift bite noticeably at both inlets. Manasquan Inlet on the last two hours of the outgoing has been the better of the two — work the channel edge off the Route 71 bridge down toward the jetty tips, 18 to 28 feet, dragging 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 4-inch chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets, slow enough that you feel bottom every third or fourth pump. Shark River Inlet's producing too, mostly on the incoming this week, same depth range along the north side channel near the Belmar side — a few keepers to 22 inches mixed with a lot of shorts, which tells you there's a strong year class coming through but the doormats are still scattered. Nobody's filling a cooler. Guys are grinding out two or three keepers on a good tide and going home satisfied, which for August fluke around here is about right. If the moon-driven current keeps the tide changes crisp through midweek, I'd expect that bite to hold — once it eases off toward the new moon, the drift slows and you'll need to work harder for the same fish.

Bluefish have been the more reliable action for anybody who just wants bent rods, and it's split into two distinct fisheries right now. Back in Shark River and up in the Manasquan back bay canals, snapper blues have gone thick — small, aggressive, snapping at anything shiny. Kids on the docks with a bobber and a sliver of bunker are having a field day, and there's a real run of slightly bigger cocktail blues mixed in behind them, which lines up with something we flagged last week — that the peanut bunker pushing into the back bays would pull the bigger blues in behind them inside of two weeks. That's exactly what's happening now, just a little ahead of schedule. Small wire leaders, quarter-ounce Kastmasters or a strip of bunker under a float, fish it on the last of the incoming when the bait gets pushed up into the creeks.

Out front, adult bluefish are still mixing into the bunker schools that show up at classic pinch points, and Sea Bright sits right at the bottom edge of that pattern coming down off Sandy Hook. If you catch a morning when the bunker get bunched tight against the rocks, don't be surprised if it's blues doing the damage instead of bass — they'll hit the same swimming plugs and poppers, just with more violence and less finesse.

No sign of albies yet, and it's early for them on this stretch — that's more a late-September story once the water starts to slide backward. Blackfish are closed this time of year regardless, so don't waste your bunker on the rock piles hoping for tog; that's an autumn program. Porgy trips out of Belmar and Manasquan have been steady but unspectacular, mostly a bottom-bouncing affair on the deeper structure for guys who just want dinner.

Looking ahead, I'd keep leaning on the early window for bass — dawn and the last light of dusk, structure-tight, bunker-driven, and gone the second the sun gets high. The fluke bite should stay sharp through the next couple of tide cycles while the moon's influence holds, then I'd expect it to soften a bit as we drift toward the new moon. If I only had one morning this week, I'd be on the Manasquan jetty at first light with a popper in hand, and I'd have a bucktail rigged in the truck for when the bass shut down and the fluke tide turns.
