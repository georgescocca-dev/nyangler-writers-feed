# Porgies Steady, Blues Chewing, Bonito Rumor Worth Chasing at the Gut
*The bass are still sitting on bait instead of busting it, but the moon's building and the water's setting up for the first real fall push.*

**MATTITUCK, NY — September 4** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

There's a fella who's clammed the flats off Rocky Point since before some of these young guides were born, and he called down to the shop Tuesday morning to say he'd seen something skipping bait out past the moorings that didn't look right for a bluefish and didn't look right for a bass either. Small, quick, gone before you could get glasses on it. Could've been nothing. Could've been the first bonito of the year working the edge of Plum Gut. We flagged that possibility last week — said this stretch sits close enough to the Race to catch the overflow once the Rhode Island push started sliding west — and here it is, a week later, showing up exactly where we said to watch for it. Doesn't mean there's a school of them stacked up yet. Means keep a small metal or an epoxy jig rigged on light wire in the boat, because if they show, they don't stick around long enough for you to go home and get one.

The rest of the week was a more familiar story. Water's still carrying the last of the summer warmth — Kings Point over on the west end of the Sound reading near 71, New London down around 69 and change, which tells you the whole Sound is still sliding cooler as you move east, same as it does every September. That gradient matters. It's why the bass out toward Orient and the Gut are behaving different than the fish getting worked over in the western Sound and down around the bay side. They're not blitzing. They're staged — sitting tight on bunker and peanut bunker schools that are still thick but not yet balled up tight enough to trigger the kind of top-water chaos everybody's waiting for. The moon's building toward full the sixth and seventh, and that's usually the mechanism that tightens bait up and gets stripers committing instead of just picking. We're not there yet on this beach. Give it the better part of a week to ten days, especially if a real cool front finally shows up behind this stretch of warm, sticky air.

Bluefish, though — that bite hasn't needed any encouragement. They've been chewing hard from Mattituck Inlet clean through to the Orient side, and it's about the most dependable thing going right now. Warm water's kept them wound up and spread out wide instead of bunched in one or two spots, which actually makes them easier for the average angler to find. Diamond jigs in the half-ounce to one-ounce range, bounced through the rips off Horton Point on the last of the ebb, have been drawing cutoffs and blues in the three-to-six-pound range pretty steady. Poppers at first light off the Rocky Point rocks have been drawing some too, though you'll lose a few to the leader if you're fishing anything lighter than 40-pound fluorocarbon. This ought to hold right through Labor Day week — the warm water isn't going anywhere fast, and neither is the bait they're chewing on.

Striped bass are there, they're just making you work for them. The better fish have been coming off the bottom rather than the surface — bucktails, three-quarter to one-ounce, white or chartreuse, dragged slow along the drop-off in front of Horton Point on the last two hours of the incoming, or worked the same way off the rocks at the Orient side closer to the Gut. A few slot fish, a couple of overslots mixed in, nothing that's going to make the six o'clock news but honest fishing for anybody willing to fish the tide change instead of the middle of it. Live eels at night off Mattituck Inlet have also been producing, which is the same trick that's worked there for sixty years and shows no sign of quitting.

Porgies are the one bite around here you can set a clock by, and this week didn't change that. The reef structure off Rocky Point and the rougher bottom approaching Plum Gut have been holding scup in good numbers on standard high-low rigs, squid strips or clam, fished right on bottom in twenty to thirty feet. Nothing fancy required. If you've got somebody in the boat who just wants bent rods and a fish fry, that's where you send them.

Fluke are thinning out the way they always do this time of year — the doormats that were sitting in the deeper holes off the inlet mouths are starting to slide toward the harder structure out toward the Gut, chasing the same baitfish that everything else is chasing. Still catchable on white Gulp swimming mullets tipped on bucktails, but the easy back-bay action is winding down and won't be back till next spring.

Weakfish have been a whisper more than a pattern — the odd fish mixed in with the porgy bite on bait, nothing worth planning a trip around.

Looking ahead, the next few days should run close to what we've had — southwest breeze most afternoons, water holding warm enough to keep the bass patient rather than desperate. If that first proper cool front finally pushes through behind the full moon, watch the bait density in the usual staging spots tighten up fast, and that's when the blitz switch tends to flip out here. Until then, fish the tide changes, keep an eye on the Gut for any more of those unexplained skipping baitfish, and don't be surprised if the bluefish are still the most honest bite on the board come next weekend.
