# Neap's Coming to Plum Gut — Slow Your Jig Down and Fish the Dark
*Porgy and fluke keep the boat busy by day, but the bass want the Gut after the sun's gone.*

**ORIENT, NY — July 21** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

My father used to say a rip has a temper, same as a man, and you learn to fish it by knowing when it's calm and when it's looking for a fight. He fished Plum Gut for forty years with nothing but a compass, a chart he'd drawn himself, and a healthy respect for the fact that the water there doesn't care how long you've been doing this. I think about that every time I idle out past Orient Point and watch the current start to build against the light. This week it's been building slower than usual, and that's telling.

We're sliding into a first-quarter neap, and anybody who's fished the Gut long enough knows what that means — the current through there and through the Race backs off, the standing waves flatten out some, and the fish that were pinned tight to the roughest water start spreading a little wider across the flow. It's not a bad thing. It just means you've got to adjust. I've gone from a three-ounce diamond jig down to a two, sometimes a ounce-and-a-half bucktail with a white pork rind trailer, because the water isn't ripping the way it does on the big tides, and a heavy jig just plows through without working right. Slower presentation, more time in the strike zone. That's the whole trick this week.

Striped bass have been in the Gut on the tide changes, same as always this time of year, but I won't stand here and tell you it's been a daylight bite, because it hasn't. The water's warm enough now — mid-to-upper 60s pushing 70 depending where you check it, warmer out toward the Race than it was a few weeks back — that the better fish have gone nocturnal on us. Dawn and dusk are your windows, and after dark if you've got the stomach for running that water at night, which I'd only recommend to somebody who already knows where the rocks are. Eels fished on a fish-finder rig, drifted through the Gut on the last two hours of the ebb, have taken slot fish and a few overslot up toward 38, 40 inches. Bunker chunks have worked too when there's been current to move the scent, fished right on bottom where the rip meets the flat water off the Plum Island side.

During the day, though, it's fluke, porgy, and blackfish keeping the rods bent, and that bite has been honest and steady, which is more than I can say for the bass. Fluke are coming off the drop-offs and channel edges from Horton Point on east — Gulp swimming mullet in pink shine or white, three to four-inch, on a half-ounce bucktail, drifted with the tide over sand-to-rock transitions. Nothing enormous, mostly 16 to 19 inches with a keeper mixed in here and there, but a full box isn't hard if you keep the boat moving and stay on structure instead of drifting open sand. Porgy have been thick on the reef stretches off Rocky Point and around the mussel beds — small hooks, squid strips, bottom rigs, and you don't need much finesse, they'll find the bait. That bite has held up all summer without a hiccup and I don't see a reason it changes soon. Blackfish, same story on structure, though they're a touch quieter with the water this warm — green crab on a jig head, fished tight to the rock piles, still worth the trip if you're patient with it.

Weakfish have been scarce, and I won't pretend otherwise. A few showed early in the season around the inlet edges but they've thinned out with the warmth. If the evenings stay calm and settled the way they have been, I'd expect a rebuild on some of the mud-bottom stretches toward dusk — that's a pattern I've seen plenty of times, weakfish liking a quiet, warm evening more than a bright hot afternoon. Worth a cast or two with a bucktail and twister tail if you're passing through, but I wouldn't build a whole trip around it yet.

Bluefish have been around in patches, not the kind of blitz you'd see farther down the coast where the bunker schools are thick, but enough that if you find bait pushed up along the beach near Mattituck Inlet or off Horton Point, you'll get slammed on a diamond jig or a topwater plug fast enough to lose some tackle. They come and go with the bait, no real pattern to it beyond that.

Looking ahead, the neap should keep softening through the weekend, which widens your workable window at the Gut beyond just the peak of the tide — good news if you've been getting shut out trying to time it perfect. I'd fish the last of the outgoing at first light for bass, switch over to fluke and porgy through the meat of the day on structure, and if the wind stays out of the south and lays down like it has been, I'd try one evening drift for weakfish just to see. No promises there, but the conditions are lining up the way they usually do before that bite rebuilds.
