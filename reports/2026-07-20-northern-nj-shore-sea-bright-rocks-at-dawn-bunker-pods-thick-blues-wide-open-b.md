# Sea Bright Rocks at Dawn: Bunker Pods Thick, Blues Wide Open, Bass Waiting on the Dark
*The bait's stacked from Sea Bright to Manasquan and the blues found it — stripers are still playing it cagey until the sun drops.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — July 20** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Quarter to six, low tide bottoming out at the Sea Bright rocks, and there's a kid down from Rumson throwing a white bucktail into water that looked like it was boiling from the inside. Bunker everywhere — not scattered pods, but that dense, oily, nervous-water mess that tells you something's under it before you even see the first blow-up. He hooked up on his third cast. Ten-pound bluefish, maybe better, and it wasn't a fluke catch. Two guys down the jetty were doing the same thing with tin — Ava 27s dressed with surgical tube, red and white, worked with a fast retrieve just under the surface where the blues were slashing.

This is the pattern right now and it's not subtle. Bunker have been running thick along the beachfront from Sea Bright down through Monmouth Beach and into Long Branch, and the blues have found them in broad daylight, which is unusual enough to be worth writing home about. We flagged this exact setup last week — said the bait would hold as long as the weather stayed stable, and that's precisely what's happened. High pressure's parked over the whole coast, no wind to speak of most mornings, and the bunker schools haven't had a reason to move off the beach. When they finally do get pushed by a blow, this bite will shut down fast. Until then, it's about as gift-wrapped as jetty fishing gets — you just need to be there before the sun gets too high and the blues drop back down.

Here's the catch, and there's always a catch: the stripers aren't playing along with the blues in daylight. Water off the beach is sitting warm — Bight readings pushing mid-70s — and that's shoved the bass into a strict low-light program. I've fished the Sea Bright rocks at first light three mornings this week and only turned bass after dark or in that gray fifteen minutes before the sun actually breaks the horizon. Manasquan Inlet's north jetty has been the most consistent spot for it — fishing the last two hours of the outgoing, working a Daiwa SP minnow or a black Bomber Long A tight to the rocks where the current pulls bait past the tip. Nothing huge, mostly 24 to 28 inches, but they're there if you're willing to be out before the joggers.

Shark River Inlet's been a similar story after dark — swim shads in white, 3/4-ounce, fished on the drift through the deeper hole on the inside of the jetty, especially on the last of the ebb when everything funnels through that narrow throat. A guy I know who fishes it religiously landed a 34-incher there Tuesday night on an eel, which tells you the bigger fish are still around, just not stupid about when they show themselves.

Fluke's been the more dependable daytime bite, and it's shifting exactly the way I figured it would a week or so back — back bay action slowing as that water gets uncomfortably warm, and the fish sliding out to the inlet mouths and the nearshore structure where there's cleaner, cooler water moving. Manasquan Inlet channel on the drift, working 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails right along the edge where the sand drops into the deeper channel — that's produced fish to 4 pounds on the last of the incoming, when the current's still got some push but isn't ripping your bucktail sideways. Belmar's fishing boats working just outside the inlet in 25 to 35 feet have had similar counts, plenty of shorts mixed with keepers, best on the tide changes when the bait gets confused for twenty minutes and the fluke stack up on it.

Porgies are around in decent numbers off the Manasquan and Shark River jetties for anyone who wants a break from the bigger game — small hooks, bloodworms or clam, right on bottom in 15 to 20 feet, good action for kids or anybody who just wants to bend a rod without overthinking it.

No albies yet, and I wouldn't expect them for another month or so — that's a September conversation, not a July one, though I'll start watching the bait breaks off Spring Lake and Manasquan Inlet as we get into August.

Moon's sliding toward the first-quarter neap tomorrow, which is going to soften the current in the inlets over the next several days. That's not necessarily bad news — a slower drift at Manasquan and Shark River can actually stretch out the fluke bite because your bucktail stays in the strike zone longer instead of getting ripped through on a hard tide. For the bass, less current usually means a slightly wider window at first light before things shut down for the day.

My money for the next few days is on the same script: bunker and blues at first light anywhere from Sea Bright to Long Branch, bass strictly a dawn-and-dark game at Manasquan and Shark River inlets, and fluke holding at the inlet mouths on the tide changes. If the wind stays out of the south and light like it has been, I don't see a reason for any of that to change fast. If it swings hard out of the east and stacks up some swell, the bunker will scatter off the beach and the blues will go with them — so if you've got a morning free this week, I wouldn't wait on it.
