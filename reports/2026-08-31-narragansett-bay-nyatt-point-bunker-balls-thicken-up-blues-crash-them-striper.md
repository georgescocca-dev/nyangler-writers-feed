# Nyatt Point bunker balls thicken up, blues crash them, stripers still biding their time
*Bait is stacking in the upper bay just like we said it would — now it's a waiting game for the moon and the tide to flip the switch on the fall bass bite.*

**PROVIDENCE, RI — August 31** — _by Vinny Ciervo, Zone Writer_

Stand on the state pier around first light and you don't need a fish finder to know what's building in this bay. The bunker balls off Field's Point and up toward the Point Street bridge have gotten noticeably thicker over the last ten days, dimpling the surface in that nervous, packed-in way that means something's pushing them. We flagged this last week — said menhaden and silversides were building in the upper bay, the same leading indicator we've watched pay off further south before the fall push — and here it is, thicker and tighter than it was even a few days ago.

What's eating them right now is mostly bluefish, and they're not shy about it. Chopper blues in the 3-to-6-pound class have been slashing through those bunker schools from Conimicut Point down through the mouth of the Providence River, and on the right morning you can see the whole thing happen from shore — bait skying, gulls dropping, blues crashing through white water. Poppers and metal — a 2-ounce Kastmaster or a Deadly Dick worked fast on top — have been the move at Conimicut and off the rocks below Rocky Point, especially on the last two hours of the ebb when current pulls that bait off the flats and funnels it past the points.

Stripers are still playing it cagey. There are fish around — guys working eels and bunker chunks on fish-finder rigs at Nyatt Point and in the deeper water off Conimicut have picked at slot bass on the last of the outgoing tide, mostly after dark — but nobody's calling it a real bite yet. That's normal for this stretch of the calendar. The bass know the bait's there, but with the moon still building toward full and tidal flow not yet at its peak, the concentration hasn't hit the tipping point where they commit in numbers. Buckeye Brook taught me that lesson years ago on the spring end of things — the herring don't run heavy until the water and the moon line up, and the bass show up right behind them. Same principle now, different bait, different season. When that bunker gets pushed into tighter, more desperate pods by bigger tides later this week, that's when the stripers stop sampling and start feeding hard.

Fluke have gone soft the way they always do this time of year. It's not a collapse, it's the seasonal handoff — warm surface water has pushed the better fish off the flats and into the channel edges and deeper holes where there's more oxygen and cooler water underneath. The Greenwich Bay channel and the drop-offs along the west side of Prudence Island have been giving up fair numbers, most fish in the 16-to-19-inch range with a keeper mixed in here and there. White or pink Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow right on bottom through the deeper cuts, have outfished the faster strip-bait presentations. This pattern should hold through most of September before the real offshore pull starts, so there's still time to put a few in the cooler if you fish it smart and stay on the deeper structure instead of the shallow flats.

Scup are exactly where you'd expect them — stacked on the rocky ledges around Prudence Island and the reef structure off Rocky Point, and they haven't slowed a bit with the warm water. Sea worms and squid strips on a simple high-low rig, dropped right to the rocks on the last of the incoming tide, will fill a bucket without much trouble. Black sea bass are riding shotgun on the same structure, a little more scattered than midsummer but still a reliable bycatch anywhere there's broken bottom or old wreckage near the bay mouth.

Weakfish have been quiet — not much to report there, and with the water this warm they're not likely to show in numbers until things cool off in a few weeks.

Looking ahead, the next few days should look a lot like the last few: bluefish staying aggressive on the bunker schools as long as that bait holds together, fluke grinding out fair numbers on the channel edges, and scup staying dependable on the ledges. The bigger story is the moon. As it builds toward full over the coming days, tidal flow picks up, and that's exactly the mechanism that should tighten the bunker schools further and start pulling stripers into a real feeding pattern instead of the opportunistic pecking we're seeing now. If you can only fish one window this week, make it the last two hours of the ebb around Conimicut or Nyatt Point, right as that current pulls bait off the flats — that's where the blues are already committed, and it's the most likely spot for the first real striper blitz to show once the bait gets squeezed hard enough. Keep an eye on the evening temps too; a few cool nights on top of a building tide is usually the combination that flips this bay from waiting-game to game-on.
