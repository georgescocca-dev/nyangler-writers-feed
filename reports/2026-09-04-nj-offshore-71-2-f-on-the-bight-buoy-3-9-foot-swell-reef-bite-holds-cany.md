# 71.2°F on the Bight Buoy, 3.9-Foot Swell: Reef Bite Holds, Canyon Tuna Stay Put
*A sloppy sea state is keeping some boats tied up, but the sea bass on the reef wrecks and the offshore tuna bite both look exactly like they did a week ago — for the same reason.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — September 4** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

71.2 degrees and a 3.9-foot swell running five seconds apart — that's the number off the NY Bight buoy this week, and it's the number that's been setting the schedule more than the calendar has. Five-second period swell is short and stacked, the kind that turns a comfortable ride to the reef wrecks into a washing machine for the first couple hours off the dock. It's not stopping the fleet, but it's editing the trip list. Boats that would normally be splitting time between the inshore reef structure and a run out toward the Klondike lumps have been picking one or the other, not both, and that's thinned out the numbers of trips reporting in on any single piece of bottom.

The tide is building toward full moon, and that's starting to show in current speed on the reef sites — drifts that were lazy two weeks ago are moving with purpose now, which matters for how much lead you're carrying on bucktails and how fast bait spins on the way down. Water color inshore has stayed reasonably clean given the wind, but there's enough push on the swell that anyone running structure inside 20 fathoms should expect some turbidity on the bottom third of the water column, especially right after a blow.

We flagged this a week ago — said the warm filament sitting on the canyon edges would keep the offshore tuna and billfish bite intact as long as nothing tropical came through the western Atlantic, and that call has held. Nothing in the tropics has pushed into this stretch of the Atlantic, and the warm water sitting off the canyon-adjacent grounds hasn't budged. That's kept the deep-water side of this beat productive on schedule, and it's the single biggest reason boats willing to run the extra distance are still finding tuna in the same general water they were finding them in ten days ago. The threat is still the same one we named then — a tropical system anywhere in the western basin would be the thing that ends this, not a cold snap or a moon phase.

Closer to the beach, the story is the reef and wreck network doing what it typically does in early September — which is to say, still holding black sea bass, but with the bite going mixed rather than steady depending on which piece of structure you're on and what stage of tide you hit it. Axel Carlson Reef and Sea Girt Reef have both been fishable between weather windows, and the pattern on both has been the same one that's worked all summer on this stretch of bottom: Gulp or squid strips on a double-hook bottom rig, dropped tight to the high relief pieces on the up-current side, with enough weight to hold bottom against the building tide without dragging off the wreck entirely. The keeper-size fish have been coming from the tighter structure — the actual wreck iron and reef balls, not the open sand between pieces — and that's typical for this time of year as the bigger sea bass tuck into the highest relief they can find ahead of the fall push.

Shrewsbury Rocks and the Triple Wrecks have seen less traffic this week simply because of the ride, but the boats that have made it out are reporting the same story — decent numbers of sea bass in the 12 to 15-inch range mixed with plenty of shorts that need throwing back, and the occasional better fish coming off bucktails tipped with squid worked slow along bottom rather than bounced. Nobody's calling it a banner week on the inshore structure, and it wouldn't be honest to say otherwise — the bite is there, but it's a tide-and-timing game right now, not a wide-open one. Best window on most of these sites has been the last two hours of the incoming, when the current slows enough to hold bottom with reasonable weight but still moves enough to keep bait scent working.

Mahi have gone quiet on the pot lines that were holding fish through midsummer, which tracks with the calendar — that pattern typically winds down through late August into September as the pot buoys lose their thermal edge and the dolphin move on. Nothing wrong with checking floating structure on the way out if you're already running past it, but it's not worth building a trip around right now.

Farther out, the Klondike and Barnegat Ridge remain the pieces of bottom worth knowing the numbers to heading into fall — both hold bait and structure that draw tuna through this stretch as the season turns, and both are the kind of grounds where a logged waypoint two decades old still means something. Right now they're context more than confirmed action; the real heat is still out on the canyon-adjacent water where the warm filament sits.

Looking ahead, the moon keeps building toward full over the next several days, which should keep tightening up current on the reef structure and could either sharpen the sea bass bite or push it into shorter, more defined windows depending on how hard the tide runs. If the swell backs off even a foot, that's the window to get back out to Shrewsbury Rocks and the Triple Wrecks before the water starts its seasonal cooldown. Offshore, the call stays the same as it's been — as long as that warm water holds and nothing spins up in the tropics, the tuna and billfish bite on the canyon edges should keep running on the same clock it's been on all month.
