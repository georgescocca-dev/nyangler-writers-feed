# Silversides Pile Into Robinson's Hole — Bonito Water, Just No Bonito Yet
*The bait is thick, the temps are close, and every captain from Woods Hole to Cuttyhunk is checking the rips twice a day for the first flash of green.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — July 20** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

The silversides are balled up so thick in Robinson's Hole right now you could scoop them with a net off the transom. I ran through there Tuesday on the tail of the outgoing and watched three separate baitballs get pushed and pinched against the Naushon side, terns working them from above, something bigger pushing from below — but when I got the glasses on it, it was just blues. Good blues, don't get me wrong, three and four pounders slashing through peanut bunker and silversides both, but not the fish everybody's really watching for.

That's where we are this week. The bait's here. The water's getting there. The albies and bonito are not — yet. I've been saying for a couple weeks now that this stretch between Woods Hole and the Vineyard is one of the first pieces of water in the whole region to see false albacore and bonito show, usually early-to-mid August once the temps peak, and the signs are lining up right on schedule. Block Island's already sitting in the upper 60s, which is comfort range for bonito, and that water doesn't have far to travel to reach Quick's Hole and Robinson's Hole once the tide starts pumping it through the Elizabeths. I wouldn't be surprised to hear the first bonito report out of the Vineyard side — probably around the Lobsterville rip or off Menemsha — inside the next two to three weeks. Keep a bonito bar or a Deadly Dick in the bag from here on out, because when they show, they show fast and they don't wait for you to rig up.

In the meantime, the fishing that's actually paying off is fluke and scup, and it's been solid, not spectacular, but solid enough to fill a cooler if you put in the drift time. Menemsha Bight has been the most consistent piece of structure — sandy bottom mixed with scattered rock on the edges, and fluke are sitting right on that transition. Four to six pound fish have been coming on white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the last two hours of the incoming when the current's still got some push but isn't ripping. Same story working the drift along the north side of Cuttyhunk toward Canapitsit — squid strips on a fluke rig, bounced bottom, has been the better bait when the Gulp gets ignored, which happens more than you'd think in water this warm.

Scup have been a reliable backup everywhere there's structure — the Woods Hole draw bridge pilings, the rock piles off Nashawena, the Cuttyhunk breakwater. Small hooks, squid or sandworm, right on bottom, and you can fill a bucket in an hour if you find the school. Not glamorous but it's honest fishing, and on a slow striper morning it's what keeps the rod bent.

Speaking of stripers — that bite has gone the way it always does once the bay water sits in the low 70s. They're not stupid, they're just not feeding through the middle of the day anymore. The keeper-class fish have shifted into a dawn, dusk, and after-dark pattern, and the rips are where you find them once the light drops. Robinson's Hole and Quick's Hole both produced fish this week on the last two hours of dusk into full dark, working swimming plugs and eels along the current seams where the rip breaks against structure. One boat working the Cuttyhunk side after dark on bunker chunks put three fish over 30 inches in the box before midnight, all on the strong push of the outgoing. If you're going to fish stripers right now, don't bother showing up at noon — show up when the sun's going down and stay till the tide turns.

We're heading into the July 21 first-quarter neap, which is going to soften the current through all these island rips for a few days. That's not bad news — it actually widens the bite window. When the current's ripping hard on a full or new moon tide, you've got maybe ninety minutes of a clean drift before it's either too slack or too strong to fish right. On a neap, that window stretches out, which is good news for anybody working eels or swimbaits through Quick's Hole or the western end of Vineyard Sound, because you'll have more time to find the fish instead of scrambling to make one pass count.

Bluefish have been the most reliable daylight action across the board — the bunker pattern that's been running strong down the coast has pushed some of that same energy into the bay, and as long as this stretch of calm, settled weather holds and doesn't scatter the bait, I expect the blues to keep showing in daylight blitzes right through the Elizabeths. Topwater poppers or 1-ounce Deadly Dicks worked fast through the froth have been getting hit as soon as they land.

Look, if you want my honest read heading into next week: fluke and scup are your bread and butter right now, stripers are a low-light and after-dark game, and bonito/albie season hasn't started but it's close enough that I'm rigging bonito outfits and leaving them rigged in the rocket launchers every time I leave the dock. If the water keeps easing up into that high-60s to low-70s window through Robinson's Hole and Quick's Hole over the next ten days, I'd bet on the first real bonito flash showing before the first week of August is out. Until then, work the bait, fish the low light, and keep your eyes on the rip — because when it happens here, it happens in an afternoon, not a season.
