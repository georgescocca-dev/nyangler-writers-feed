# Ran to the Wilmington Tuesday instead of the Baltimore Canyon — here's why that paid off
*Six hours trolling the northeast edge put three yellowfin and a mahi in the box, and confirmed the warm-water setup we've been watching build all month.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 24** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

Had three boats calling me Monday night asking whether to run to the Baltimore or stick with the Wilmington, and I made the call to stay home water — northeast corner of the Wilmington, up around 38°28' N, 72°40' W, working the push where the warm eddy has been sitting against the shelf break. That decision came down to fuel math and what I'd seen the week before: the temperature break out there has been holding tighter and cleaner than anything to the south, and I didn't want to burn an extra hour and a half of diesel chasing a report from a boat that fished it two days prior.

Good call this time. We ran the troll spread — naked ballyhoo and a mix of Green Machines and small Ilanders in the pattern, six lines out — along the edge where the water goes from a milky green inshore chop to that dark, almost black-blue offshore color. That line was sitting in about 300 to 900 feet of water, right where the bottom starts to fall off the shelf into the canyon proper. Water temp on the break was pushing into the upper 70s, close to 79 in spots, which is exactly the kind of warm-core structure we flagged building last week. We said the yellowfin bite would hold on that temperature break through the canyons, and out on the Wilmington's edge, that's precisely what we found.

Three yellowfin in the 25 to 40-pound class came off the spread between 8 and 11 in the morning, all on the troll — two on a green-and-yellow machine run short on the shotgun, one on a naked hoo pulled long off the left flat line. We picked up a bull mahi around 15 pounds trolling past a small weed line drifting on the surface just inside the break — nothing dramatic, no real grass mat, just a scattered patch of sargassum that had a few small mahi stacked under it. Worth a look every time you see anything floating out there right now; even a five-gallon bucket's worth of weed can hold fish this time of year.

What we didn't find was bigeye, and I want to be straight about that. We marked some deep bait on the machine around 600 feet but never got a jump from anything heavier than the yellowfin class, and I talked to two other captains that night who had similar days — decent yellowfin counts, no bigeye to show. If bigeye's your target, I'd be thinking about a night chunk on anchor over structure rather than running the troll pattern during daylight. That's a different program and a different day, and right now I'm not seeing enough bigeye sign to justify switching off the troll during daylight hours.

White marlin showed once — a single fish that came up on the right flat line, took a look at a naked ballyhoo, and never committed. No hookup, no second look. That's been the pattern most of July out here — marlin are around, but they're not eating aggressively, and I think that's tied to the amount of bait suspended in that upper 60 feet of water column. When there's that much natural forage stacked up, your trolled spread is competing with a full pantry, and the marlin just aren't hungry enough to chase plastic and rigged bait when there's real food everywhere.

No wahoo this trip, which surprised me a little given the water temp. Usually when you're seeing upper 70s on the break this time of year, you start marking a few wahoo runs, especially if you're pulling a high-speed spread with cedar plugs or small Yo-Zuris off a planer. We didn't run that gear Tuesday — stuck with the standard ballyhoo/skirt spread for the tuna program — so it's possible we just didn't present the right bait. Next trip out I'm planning to run one wahoo bait high-speed off the corner just to test it, since the water's right even if we didn't connect.

Bluefin have been more of a Block Island and canyon-corridor story than a Wilmington story lately — the slot fishery up north is grinding along steady since the July 3 trophy closure, but down here we're just not seeing the bluefin numbers we'd expect. If you're set on bluefin, that's a longer run and a different trip plan, not a Wilmington day.

Looking at the week ahead, the high-pressure pattern that's been parking over the mid-Atlantic shows no sign of breaking, which means the warm-core water sitting on the Wilmington's edge should hold its position and its temperature. If that eddy stays put through the weekend, I'd expect the yellowfin bite to remain the most dependable program out there — troll in the morning while the light's low and the fish are still up chewing, then switch to a chunk with butterflied bunker if the bite goes quiet by midday. That's the pattern I'll be running Thursday with a full boat, and it's the same play I'd suggest to anyone weighing a Wilmington trip this week: go early, work the break, and don't be afraid to sit on the chunk if the troll goes cold by noon.
