# Plum Gut ran hard like we called it — bass went nocturnal, porgies never blinked
*The big tide stacked fish on the seams from Horton Point to Orient, but you'll need to fish the dark hours for the bass.*

**ORIENT, NY — July 18** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

There's a kid — can't be more than sixteen — who's been putting a twelve-foot skiff in at Mattituck Inlet every morning this week, and every morning he comes back with fewer bucktails than he left with. Tuesday I watched him lose three in a row to blues he never even saw hit, just felt the line go slack and then heard him swear at the sky. I didn't say anything. Everybody learns the hard way that you don't run bare mono around bunker schools with teeth in them. By Thursday he'd wised up, tied on a piece of wire, and came in with a five-gallon bucket half full of chopper blues off Rocky Point. That's how it goes out here — the Sound teaches you, slow and a little expensive.

We said last week that the big spring tide would push hard current through Plum Gut and stack bass, blues, and fluke on the seams and drop-offs, and that's exactly what showed up. The current through there was running like a river for three, four days straight, and anybody who fished the edge of it — right where the fast water meets the slack behind the rocks off Orient Point — found company. Fluke to five pounds came off that seam on white Gulp on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the last two hours of the ebb. Nothing spectacular in numbers, but quality fish, and consistent enough that a few of the charter boats made it their whole morning.

The water's been warm on this end of the Sound — Kings Point's reading better than 71, and even out toward New London it's holding in the upper 60s — and that's pushed the striped bass into the pattern we see most Julys: they don't want to feed hard in daylight when the water's carrying that kind of heat, so they've gone almost entirely nocturnal. I'm not going to tell you the bass disappeared, because they didn't. I'm going to tell you that if you're fishing Horton Point or the rip off Rocky Point at two in the afternoon expecting a bass blitz, you're going to be disappointed, and that disappointment isn't the fish's fault. Fish the last hour of light into full dark, working the current breaks with eels or a big swimming plug worked slow, and you'll find them holding tight to structure, using the deeper water and the current itself as cover from the heat. A few guys drifting live eels off Plum Gut after sundown this week put slot fish in the box — nothing giant, mostly high twenties to low thirties, but real fish, not shorts.

Bluefish are the daylight story right now, and they've been the most dependable thing on this whole stretch. Bait schools have been thick from Mattituck clear out to Orient, and the blues are sitting right underneath them the way they do — not chasing bait down, just holding under it and cutting loose whenever something swims through. Diamond jigs with a tube teaser, worked with a jigging motion off the reef structure near Horton Point, have been about as close to a sure thing as this Sound offers. Poppers work too if you catch them up shallow on the bait, which has been happening most mornings before the wind builds.

Porgies never asked anybody's permission to be good, and they're still the bread-and-butter bite on the reef structure from Rocky Point out to the rocks around Plum Gut approach. Sandworm and clam on a simple hi-lo rig, right on the bottom, drifting the reef edges — that's been filling coolers for the guys who don't need a story to tell, just dinner. Blackfish are closed this time of year in these waters, so leave the crabs at home till fall — but the porgy bite more than covers for it if you're after something to eat.

Weakfish have been showing in ones and twos on the calmer evenings — nothing I'd call a real bite yet, more a hint that they're around than a pattern you can plan a trip on. If the calm holds and the water stays warm through the week, I'd expect a few more to show up in the same spots as the bluefish, working the same bait. I wouldn't drive far for them yet.

Looking ahead, the tide's easing off toward that first quarter moon on the 21st, which means the current through the Gut and around Orient Point is going to soften up some. That usually means slower presentations start outproducing the aggressive stuff — smaller bucktails, lighter jigs, working the seams instead of bombing across them. If I had one evening this week, I'd fish the last of the ebb at Plum Gut into the first hour of dark, eel or plug for the bass, and not be surprised if a few blues crash the party too. The porgies will still be there in the morning regardless of what the tide does — they always are.
