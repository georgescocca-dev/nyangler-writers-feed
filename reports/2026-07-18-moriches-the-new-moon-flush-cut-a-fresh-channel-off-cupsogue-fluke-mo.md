# The New Moon Flush Cut a Fresh Channel Off Cupsogue — Fluke Moved Right Into It
*Moriches Inlet scoured deeper on the west side after the spring tide, and the bait — and the bass and fluke that eat it — followed the new bottom.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — July 18** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

I walked the jetty at Cupsogue Tuesday morning at half-tide and the bottom told me everything I needed to know. The west lobe of the inlet, the one that always builds after a hard spring flush, had pushed out another ten or fifteen yards since I was out here two weeks ago. That new moon on the 13th did exactly what I said it would — it ran a powerful flush through the throat of the inlet, and it scoured a fresh channel edge right off the tip of the jetty where the old one had started filling with sand. Bait got funneled through that cut on the ebb, and everything that eats bait followed it in.

That's not a coincidence, and it's not luck — it's just how this inlet works. Moriches doesn't sit still. Every big tide event moves sand, and every time the bottom changes, the fish reposition to the new edges. Right now the best structure isn't where it was in June. It's that new drop-off on the ocean side of the west jetty, roughly 4 to 7 feet of water butting up against a scoured trough that's running closer to 12, and it's holding fluke stacked tight on the seam.

We flagged the Moriches-Shinnecock corridor heating up for fluke through the back half of July, and that's exactly what's showing up. I had three solid drifts off the inlet mouth Wednesday on the last two hours of the outgoing, working a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, dragged slow across that new channel edge. Picked off four keepers in the 19 to 22-inch range, plus more shorts than I wanted to unhook. The pattern holds through the corridor east toward Shinnecock too — same story, channel edges and current-swept structure, not open sand. If you're marking bait on the meter but not getting bit, you're probably ten feet off the edge. Move onto the lip of the drop and stay there.

Bluefish are the beach story right now, and it's a good one if you don't mind covering water. Incoming tide at Cupsogue and out toward Pikes Beach has bait pods getting worked over hard — snapper-sized blues mixed with cocktail fish in the 2 to 4 pound range, blitzing right in the wash some mornings. Metal — a 1-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — thrown into the boil and cranked steady is all you need. The ocean's been sloppy, running 2 to 3 feet with a short period out of the south, so don't expect glassy conditions. The blues don't care. They're stacked under the bait, not chasing it around, and that pattern should hold as long as this bunker stays along the beachfront.

Striped bass are the tougher conversation. Bay water's pushed well up into the 70s, and that's shoved the bass into a nocturnal pattern — this isn't a fish that disappeared, it's a fish avoiding warm water during daylight and feeding on the temperature break after dark. I've been fishing the rocks at the west jetty and the drop-off on the bay side of the inlet from an hour before dark through the top of the tide, swimming eels slow along the bottom edge where that cooler water pools after sundown. Results have been real but not easy — a handful of fish in the 28 to 34-inch range over three trips, with a couple better ones lost in the rocks. If you want bass right now, you're fishing at night, you're fishing structure, and you're not complaining about the hours.

Weakfish are starting to show some life again, and this tracks with what I expected — calm, warm evenings are exactly when this bite rebuilds. I had a report of a few keeper weakfish taken on the bay side near Tuthill Point working a Gravity Tackle sand eel on light spinning gear, right at last light on a soft outgoing tide. It's not a numbers game yet, but the fish are there and the pattern makes sense — weakfish like exactly the still, warm-evening conditions we've had this week. Worth a couple hours at dusk if you're already out.

Porgies are steady and reliable on the inlet structure itself — clam on a standard high-low rig around the rocks at Cupsogue and the deeper holes inside the inlet mouth will keep a rod bent without much drama. Blackfish are out of the conversation until the fall opener, so don't waste bait there.

Looking ahead, the moon's easing toward first quarter on the 21st, which means the current through the inlet is going to soften over the next several days. That's going to change the fluke game slightly — slower drifts, lighter jig heads, maybe dropping to a half-ounce bucktail to keep contact with bottom without dragging too fast across that new channel edge. If the ocean lays down at all, I'd also take a look at the outside edge of that west lobe on a calm morning — that kind of structure right after it forms often holds fish nobody's found yet. For bass, stick with the after-dark program at the jetty rocks until the bay cools even a degree or two. And keep an eye on the bunker along the beach — as long as it's there, the blues will be too.
