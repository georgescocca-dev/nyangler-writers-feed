# Forty Gallons to Veatch, a Bigeye at Boatside, and a Long Look at the Sky
*The eastern grounds are still holding warm water and hungry tuna, but every captain on the radio this week was talking storms before they talked fish.*

**MONTAUK, NY — August 31** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

A mate on the radio out of Montauk Tuesday night summed up the whole week in one line: "We got the bigeye at color, lost him at the leader, and then spent twenty minutes arguing about the five-day." That's about where the fleet's head is right now — one eye on the fish finder, one eye on the tropics. The fishing itself has been the easy part.

The warm-water setup that's had this zone buzzing all August hasn't gone anywhere. Veatch, Hydrographer, and Oceanographer are still sitting inside water that's carrying real color and real bait, and the eddy structure that's been feeding this bite has shown no sign of breaking down. We flagged this pattern last week — said the tuna action should hold as long as that eddy stayed put, and it has. Boats running out of Montauk, Block Island, and the Rhode Island ports are still connecting on multi-species days: yellowfin in the 40- to 60-pound class on the troll, scattered bigeye on the night drift, and enough mahi and wahoo mixed in on the color changes to keep a crew busy between tuna bites. White marlin have been showing on the troll spreads too, mostly as incidental releases behind the ballyhoo, which is a good sign the bait base is broad and healthy out there, not just one species stacked on structure.

The run itself is still the real cost of doing business out here, and nobody in this fleet needs reminding of that. Out of Montauk, Veatch and Atlantis are the closer targets, but "closer" still means the better part of the day burned just getting there and back at a sane cruise speed. Block Island and the RI ports have a slightly shorter reach into Hydrographer and Oceanographer, but the fuel math doesn't change much — you're planning a full tank, a weather window on both ends, and a crew that understands an early wake-up beats getting caught running home in building seas. This week that math got more complicated, because the forecast discipline mattered as much as the tuna bite.

What's actually working: natural baits and the jig bite both continue to produce, which lines up with what's been said on this beat for the better part of two weeks now. Butterfish and small mackerel fished dead-bait style on wind-on leader are still drawing bigeye and yellowfin on the drift, particularly overnight when the bite tends to concentrate. The jig program — 180-gram class speed jigs worked with a mechanical yo-yo retrieve — has kept producing yellowfin and the occasional bigeye on the drop, especially over structure edges where the current is stacking bait against the drop-off. Streaker-style rigged natural baits behind the spread have also been drawing strikes, adding a little insurance to straight ballyhoo spreads that can go quiet during slower stretches of current.

Mahi have been the bonus fish all month, showing up under floating debris and around any temperature break a boat happens to cross running canyon to canyon. Nothing enormous, mostly schoolies in the 5- to 12-pound range, but a welcome distraction when the tuna bite goes cold for an hour. Wahoo have been sporadic — a fish here, a fish there, usually on a high-speed lure run through a rip line rather than the standard trolling spread. Worth having one rigged and ready, but not worth planning the whole day around.

The bigger story this week isn't really the fish, it's the sky. The warm filament pushing this whole pattern north has been unusual enough that it's turned this stretch of ocean into one of the more interesting canyon zones going right now, and the only real threat to it is tropical activity spinning up somewhere south and disrupting the setup that's been holding all month. Nothing organized enough yet to change plans, but every captain running this water is watching the tracks closely, because a storm doesn't have to make landfall here to wreck a canyon trip — it just has to churn up the water column and scatter the bait that's been holding this bite together.

Looking at the next few days, the pattern should hold if the tropics stay quiet. The moon is building toward full, which tends to increase current strength out at the canyons and can concentrate bait tighter along the edges — that's usually good news for the troll and the night bite alike, assuming the water stays clean. Expect the exotic mix — yellowfin, bigeye, mahi, wahoo, and the occasional white marlin release — to keep showing through the weekend and into the Labor Day run, with boat traffic picking up as the holiday brings more hulls out to the drop-off. If a system does organize in the western Atlantic, that's the wildcard that changes everything, and it's worth checking the track before committing to a long run rather than after you're already forty miles out with a full crew and a half tank.

For now: if the forecast holds clean, this is still one of the better windows of the season to make the run. Fuel up early, watch the five-day like it's part of your pre-departure checklist, and treat any hint of tropical development the way you'd treat a fouled prop — deal with it before you leave the dock, not after.
