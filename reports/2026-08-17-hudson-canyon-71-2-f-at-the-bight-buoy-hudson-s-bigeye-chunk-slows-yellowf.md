# 71.2°F at the Bight Buoy: Hudson's Bigeye Chunk Slows, Yellowfin Hold the Edges
*A warm pool sitting right on the canyon wall has cooled the night bigeye bite — but the Claw and the Dip are still giving up daytime yellowfin and mahi for anyone working the edge instead of the deep water.*

**MANASQUAN INLET, NJ — August 17** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Seventy-one point two degrees at the Bight buoy, seas holding under four feet at five-second intervals. That's not a canyon read, but it tells me plenty about what's happening between the beach and the color change — the whole shelf is carrying more heat than it should for mid-August, and that warm water isn't stopping at the 60-fathom line. It's pushing right up onto the edge of Hudson Canyon, and that's the single biggest reason this week's bite looks different than it did two weeks ago.

Here's the honest version: the after-dark bigeye chunk bite that had guys buzzing near the edge earlier this month has gone soft. We flagged the warm water sticking around as a good sign for that pattern holding steady — and it did hold, for a stretch — but a warm pool sharing space with the canyon has a way of spreading bigeye out instead of concentrating them. I ran two trips out of Manasquan this week and put buckets down over 300 fathoms both nights with butterfish chunks and whole squid strips on 60-lb fluoro leader, and the count was thin. One bigeye each night, both around 150 pounds, both on the same slow incoming drift between midnight and 2 a.m. That's a fish worth the trip, but it's not the volume bite we were seeing when the water was tighter to the wall.

So this week I'm not committing a full tank of fuel to sit over water that's been quiet. The better play — and the one paying off for boats I've talked to at the dock — is working the shallower structure and the edges instead of pushing deep. That means the Claw and the Dip during daylight hours, trolling the 100-fathom line where the warm water is stacking bait against the break instead of just soaking through it. A standard spread of ballyhoo behind Green Machines, naked ballyhoo on 50-lb fluoro, and a couple of cedar plugs way back has been drawing yellowfin in the 25 to 40-pound class, mixed with schoolie mahi under any piece of floating structure — lobster gear, debris, weed clumps, anything holding shade. If you find a real weedline with color to it, slow down and work it with a spreader bar and a naked ballyhoo dropped back on a flat line. That's where the mahi show up in pairs and threes, not just the stray teenager everyone expects.

White marlin have been in the mix but scattered — I heard of two raised off the Dip on a dredge spread with a mix of squid chains and mullet, neither one converted, which tells you they're around but not committed yet. Wahoo have been quiet; if the water keeps holding this warm, I'd expect a few to show on high-speed spreads with cedar plugs and skirted Ilanders once the bait gets pushed tighter to structure, but I'm not seeing it yet and I won't pretend otherwise.

The number I'm actually watching this week isn't a temperature — it's the deep blue eddy water that's been pinwheeling in from the east and working toward the canyon complex. That kind of clean, blue, nutrient-poor water sounds like a bad thing, but when it collides with the warmer inshore water sitting on the wall, it can sharpen a temperature break fast and pull bait — and bigeye — right back onto the edge. I've seen this happen before: one good push of that eddy water onto the Hudson grounds and a soft pattern flips inside 48 hours. If it stalls out east of the canyon instead, expect the same mixed bag we're getting now — decent yellowfin and mahi on the troll, a long night for one or two bigeye on the chunk.

Tide-wise, we're building toward the August 28 full moon, and that stronger current should start sharpening bait concentrations along the 100-fathom break by the weekend. We called that current buildup a few weeks back, and it's arriving on schedule — the question is whether it lines up with that eddy water making its move at the same time. If both happen together, I'd expect the bigeye chunk to come back to life and the daytime troll to get more consistent instead of just good in flashes.

If I had one trip this week, I'd run to the Dip first thing, troll the edge through the morning with ballyhoo and a spreader bar looking for yellowfin and mahi under anything floating, then push out to deeper water only if I found bait stacked tight on the sounder by mid-afternoon. I wouldn't plan on a bigeye trip unless that eddy water shows real signs of pushing onto the grounds — right now the smarter money is on the shallower structure doing the producing, not the deep chunk. Keep an eye on that boundary between the warm shelf water and the clean blue pushing in from the east. That's the line that decides which version of this week's bite you get.
