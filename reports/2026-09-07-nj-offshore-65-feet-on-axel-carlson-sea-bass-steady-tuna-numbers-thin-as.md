# 65 Feet on Axel Carlson: Sea Bass Steady, Tuna Numbers Thin as the Water Starts to Turn
*The reef network is still producing but the offshore push toward the new moon is the number worth watching this week.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — September 7** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

Seventy-one point two. That's the last read on the surface temp out past the Bight, and the number that matters isn't the reading itself — it's the direction. That's down from where it sat through most of August, and with a three-to-four foot swell running out of the south on a five-second period, the top of the water column has been getting stirred and cooled all week. That combination — cooling surface temp, short-period swell out of the south, and a moon building toward new phase on the 10th — is the setup that usually starts nudging this whole zone toward its early-fall pattern. Sea bass tighten up on structure, mahi thin out on the pot lines, and the tuna game gets more about timing the push than running blind and hoping.

Start with the reef network, because that's where the numbers are most honest right now. Axel Carlson sits in around 65 feet, and that's been the depth where sea bass have stayed the most consistent — not stacked, not a slaughter, but a real fish-a-drift kind of bite on the up-current side of the reef balls. Green crab and clam on a standard two-hook bottom rig is still doing the job, but the better fish this week have come off a Gulp swimming mullet or a 4-inch jig teased just off bottom on the drift rather than dead-sticked. Sea Girt Reef has been running the same story in similar depth — mixed bag with sea bass leading, a few short fluke mixed through, nothing that's going to fill a cooler in an hour but steady enough to be worth the run if the wind lays down.

Shrewsbury Rocks is the one to watch differently. It's shallower, rougher bottom, and this time of year it starts to be as much about what's cruising over the structure as what's living on it. No confirmed run of albies or bonito on the rock pile yet this week, but the water's cooling into the range where that starts to happen, and with the new moon tide arriving September 10 and typically dragging bait tight against structure like this, that's the spot in the network most likely to produce the first real bonito or albie sign of the fall. Worth keeping a small metal or a Deadly Dick rigged and ready if you're running past on the way to deeper water.

Further out, the Klondike and Barnegat Ridge stayed quiet on tuna this week — and it's worth being straight about that instead of dressing it up. The bluefin bite those lumps produce in June and July runs on peanut bunker and school bunker holding tight over structure, and with that bait pattern breaking down as we move into September, the fish that were there have either scattered or pushed out toward deeper water where the temperature break holds better. That doesn't mean the ridge and the lumps are done — it means the timing window narrowed, and running out on hope alone isn't the play right now. If you're set up to make the run, the smarter approach is treating it as a scouting trip: troll a mixed spread through the lump edges early, and if you're not marking bait on the meter inside the first hour, don't burn the whole tank looking for something that isn't there this week.

Mahi have been the quietest of the bunch. The pot-line pattern that stacks them through July and August depends on warm, clean water sitting up shallow over the lines, and with the surface cooling and the swell working the top of the water column, that clean-water bite has been breaking apart. A few stragglers are still turning up on trolled spreads that happen to cross a pot buoy, but nobody's targeting them exclusively anymore, and that's the right call.

Triple Wrecks remain the one piece of structure in the network holding steady across the board — sea bass and the occasional blackfish showing early, which tracks with what you'd expect as the water starts its slow slide toward tog season. Nothing worth calling a tog bite yet — it's early, and the water still needs to drop another several degrees before that gear comes out in earnest — but the wreck is holding bottom fish well, and it's a name worth keeping in the rotation through the rest of September.

The next few days are about that new moon push on the 10th. If the pattern holds the way it usually does this time of year, expect the tide to strengthen, bait to get pulled tighter against structure, and the reef bite to sharpen up right along with it — that's typically when Shrewsbury Rocks and the shallower reef sites start producing the first real numbers on bonito and albies instead of just possibility. Offshore, the smart money is watching whether that warmer filament holds against the drop past the ridge; if it does, there's still a window for a late tuna push before the water turns for good. If it breaks down, this becomes a reef-and-wreck month, plain and simple, and that's not a bad month to have around here.
