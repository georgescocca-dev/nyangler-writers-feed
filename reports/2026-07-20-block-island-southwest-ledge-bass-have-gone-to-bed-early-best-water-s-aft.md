# Southwest Ledge Bass Have Gone to Bed Early — Best Water's After Dark Now
*Warm shallow water has pushed the big bass nocturnal, fluke are holding steady on the humps, and bonito are close enough to start checking the rips.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — July 20** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

There's a particular quiet that settles over the south side of the island on these July evenings, right around eight-thirty, when the wind finally lets go of the southwest and the water off the Southwest Ledge goes flat and dark green under the last light. You feel it before you see it — the chop drops out, the gulls stop working, and for about twenty minutes the whole place holds its breath. That's when I start rigging eels. Because right now, that quiet window is where the big bass are living.

The shallow water south of the Cape has been running warm enough, long enough, that the keeper-class bass have shifted hard into a low-light and after-dark pattern, and Block Island is no exception. I've had better luck the last two weeks fishing the Ledge and the North Rip from an hour before sunset until well past midnight than I have at high noon, even on a good tide. Live eels drifted along the north side of the Ledge in eighteen to thirty feet, slow-trolled or dead-drifted with the current, have been the most consistent producer — a couple of fish over forty pounds came off that structure in the last ten days on eels fished right on bottom as the tide turned. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig at the North Rip after dark have also been drawing fish up out of the deeper water, especially on the last two hours of the ebb when the current starts stacking bait against the rip line. During the day, the same water that produced all spring has gone quiet by ten in the morning — the fish are still there, just not feeding, holding deep and tight to structure until the light drops again.

Fluke fishing has been the more dependable daytime program, and it's not close. The water around Block Island runs noticeably cooler than what you find south of Montauk — we're sitting in that comfortable mid-to-upper-60s range that fluke like, rather than the warmer water that's been pushing stripers off their feed elsewhere. I've been working the humps and channel edges between the Ledge and the Coast Guard station, drifting white and chartreuse Gulp swimming mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, over broken bottom in twenty-five to forty feet on the last of the incoming and first two hours of the outgoing. That moving water is doing the work — dead slack and these fish stop showing interest almost immediately. Sizes have been solid, several to five and six pounds mixed in with a good number of shorts, so you're sorting fish but you're not starving for bites. Scup have been thick around the same structure and off the docks in the Great Salt Pond, small hooks and sandworms or squid strips doing the job for anybody who wants a bucket of dinner without much drama.

Bluefish have been scattered rather than schooled up tight around the island itself, though the bigger blitz activity building along the beaches to our west and south — bunker pods getting worked hard in daylight — tells me it's only a matter of time before that class of fish pushes up this way as those bunker schools keep moving. When it does show here, it usually shows first off Sandy Point and along the east side, and when the blues are on bunker like that you don't need finesse — a wire leader and a diamond jig or a topwater popper thrown right into the surface commotion is about all the presentation required.

Now for the one I've been watching closest. We flagged it last week — said bonito would likely show at Block Island within a week or two as water temps pushed into their comfort range, and while nobody's iced one off the island yet, the water's sitting right where it needs to be for that first wave to show up on the temperature breaks. I've had my eye on the rip lines off the Southwest Ledge and the temperature seam that sets up east of the Beacon on the last of the ebb — that's usually where the first fish of the season get spotted, working small bait pods right on the surface. If you're itching to be the first one to connect, small Deadly Dicks or Albie Snax worked fast through any nervous water or diving-bird activity out there is the move, but I wouldn't plan a whole trip around it just yet. Squid have also been building in numbers around the broader Rhode Island grounds, which is a good sign — that's usually the forage that pulls bonito and eventually albies in tight to structure once they do arrive.

We're heading into a first-quarter neap this week, which is going to soften up the current through the rips and widen out the productive window for both the eel bite and the fluke drift — less brutal current means you can work a spot longer before you're fighting the drift instead of fishing it. If I had one evening to fish this week, I'd be on the North Rip an hour before sunset with eels rigged and ready, working through dark into the top of the tide change. Bring a rod for fluke too and hit the humps on your way out — no reason to waste good daylight water waiting on the sun to drop.
