# Moriches Bay fluke fishing fires as thermal layers stack bait in the shallows
*Doormat summer flounder to 7 pounds crushing bucktails in 6 feet as bay temps climb past ocean water.*

**MORICHES, NY — June 12** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The thermal engine that drives Moriches Bay is hitting full stride, and the fluke fishing is responding exactly like the physics predict. Bay water pushed past 74 degrees this week while the ocean side of the inlet held at 68 — that 6-degree gradient is creating a conveyor belt of bait that's stacking summer flounder in the skinny water like I haven't seen since early July last season.

The action is concentrated in that magic 4-to-8-foot zone where the bay's shallow structure meets the deeper channels. I'm talking about the edges off Tuthill Point, the drop-offs along the Westhampton flats, and especially that sweet spot where the east-west channel curves toward Smith Point. The fish are sitting right on those transitions, ambushing spearing and juvenile bunker that are getting pushed around by the thermal layers.

Tactics are all about finesse in this skinny water. I'm running 1/4-ounce bucktails — anything heavier and you're bouncing bottom too hard in 6 feet of water. White and chartreuse combinations are producing, tipped with 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets in pearl or nuclear chicken. The key is the drift speed — you need that Minn Kota to slow you down when the wind tries to push you too fast across the structure.

The quality has been exceptional. Multiple fish in the 5-to-7-pound range this week, with several pushing that magical doormat threshold. These aren't the skinny spring fish — these are thick, post-spawn females that have been feeding hard on the abundant bait. The 20-inch minimum is actually helping here; we're seeing more fish in that 19-to-22-inch slot that would have been kept under the old regs, and they're growing into real quality.

What's driving this bite is the bait concentration. The thermal stratification in the bay is creating these invisible walls that funnel spearing and juvenile bunker into predictable areas. When you find that 2-degree temperature break on your fish finder, that's where the food chain stacks up. The fluke know it, and they're positioning accordingly.

Tide timing matters more than usual right now. The outgoing has been the producer, especially that last two hours before low water. The ebb pulls cooler, oxygen-rich water from the deeper channels across the flats, and it carries scent trails that draw the fluke up from their holding spots. I'm seeing the best action from 7 AM to 10 AM on the morning ebb, then again from 6 PM to sunset on the evening drop.

The inlet itself has been productive for mixed bags. Striped bass in the 24-to-32-inch range are working the rips on the flood tide, hitting 6-inch Hogy soft plastics and live peanut bunker. The bass fishing isn't spectacular, but it's consistent — perfect for filling out a fluke trip when the flatfish bite slows.

Porgies are thick on the deeper structure around the inlet mouth. The 15-to-20-foot humps are loaded with keeper-sized fish, and they're aggressive on small bucktails tipped with clam or squid strips. It's a good backup plan when the fluke get finicky in the afternoon heat.

Looking ahead, this thermal pattern should hold through the weekend. The new moon on Friday means moderate tides — not the ripping currents that can scatter bait, but enough water movement to keep oxygen levels up and fish active. I'm expecting the skinny water bite to stay strong, especially if we get some cloud cover to break up the intense sun that's been hammering the shallows by midday.

The real test comes next week when we're forecast to hit the mid-80s air temps. If bay water climbs past 78 degrees, we might see the fluke push to the deeper edges or become more nocturnal. But for now, this is prime time for Moriches Bay summer flounder — the kind of fishing that makes you remember why you fell in love with this dynamic system in the first place.
