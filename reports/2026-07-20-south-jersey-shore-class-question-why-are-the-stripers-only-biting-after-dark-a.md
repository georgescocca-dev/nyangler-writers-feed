# Class Question: Why Are the Stripers Only Biting After Dark? Ask the Bait
*Warm water has pushed the bass nocturnal, but fluke, bluefish, and weakfish are keeping the daylight shift honest from Absecon to Stone Harbor.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — July 20** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get the same question every July, usually from a reader standing at the rail with a bucktail he hasn't gotten bit on since 2 p.m.: "Professor, where'd the bass go?" The answer's the same lesson I teach every summer — they didn't go anywhere. They just clocked out for the day shift.

Water's been sitting in the low-to-mid 70s off our beaches and warmer than that in the back bay flats behind Brigantine and up in the Absecon meadows, and striped bass don't like working that hard in daylight when the metabolism's already running hot. What you're left with is a bass that feeds at dawn, at dusk, and best of all after full dark, staged tight to current edges where the water's moving and slightly cooler than the flats around it. If you want a striper this week, fish the last hour of light through the first two hours of dark on the outgoing at Absecon Inlet, working the rock jetty seam with a bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig, or swing an eel through the deeper cuts off the Brigantine side. That's not a guess — that's the pattern holding up and down this coast right now, and it's the same reason the party boat guys are telling their sea bass and fluke customers not to bother with striper rods until the sun starts dropping.

Now here's where the classroom gets interesting, because the daylight bite hasn't disappeared — it's just changed subjects. Fluke remain the most dependable fish in this zone, and they haven't blinked at the warm water the way the bass have. I've had good drifts in 18 to 25 feet on the Great Egg Harbor Inlet edges, working the channel that runs out past the old Ocean City bridge pilings, using 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, teased with a strip of squid. The keeper-to-short ratio has been better on the last two hours of the incoming, when the moving water pushes bait up against the drop-offs and the fluke stack on the up-current side waiting for it. Sod bank fluking in the Absecon back bay — the skinny water behind the meadows toward Brigantine — has also been producing, especially on the sod bank drop from 4 feet down into 8, fished on the tail of the outgoing when the bait gets flushed off the grass edges. Chartreuse bucktails with a Gulp trailer have out-produced straight bait rigs there this week, probably because the water's got a little color to it after the recent thunderstorm runoff.

Bluefish are the story I flagged for you last week, and it's playing out almost on schedule. We said the bunker-driven blitz pattern working the beaches to our north was going to spread south along the barrier islands as the bait schools kept moving with the tide, and sure enough, guys working the surf at Ocean City and down toward Sea Isle City have been into slashing blues in the first hundred yards off the beach during the last few mornings, right under diving gulls and terns. If you see birds working tight to the wash, don't waste time — get a metal lip swimmer or a Hopkins spoon out there fast, because those blitzes come and go in twenty-minute windows. Townsends Inlet has had some spillover activity too, blues chopping bunker right at the inlet mouth on the last of the outgoing, which tells me the bait's pushing down past Stone Harbor as well. Watch for that same pattern to keep sliding south through this week if the weather stays calm — the minute we get a blow that scatters the bunker pods, this bite goes quiet fast, so I wouldn't sit on it.

Weakfish are the other bright spot, and this is a fish that rewards patience over horsepower. Calm, warm evenings have been rebuilding that bite in the deeper holes of the back bay channels — Great Egg has been the more consistent producer, particularly the bend behind the meadows where the channel drops to 12 feet or better. Light jigheads with a chartreuse or pink Gulp shrimp, fished slow along bottom on the last of the incoming tide right at dusk, has been the most reliable combination. These aren't the 4-pound weakfish of years past — mostly 14 to 18 inches — but enough of them showing to make an evening worthwhile if you're willing to work slow and quiet.

Kingfish are around in the surf troughs at Ocean City and Stone Harbor, small but steady on bloodworm-tipped high-low rigs fished in the first sandbar trough on a moving tide, morning or evening both working fine since kingfish don't care much about light the way bass do.

We're heading toward a first-quarter neap on the 21st, which is going to soften the current in the inlets over the next several days. That's actually good news for the fluke program — slower water means longer, more controllable drifts through the Absecon and Great Egg channels instead of getting rushed through the sweet spot on a hard ebb. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take an evening tide at Absecon — fluke on the last of the incoming, then switch over to bunker chunks for bass as the light fades and the current starts to slacken with the neap. Two lessons in one trip, which is really how I like to teach it.
