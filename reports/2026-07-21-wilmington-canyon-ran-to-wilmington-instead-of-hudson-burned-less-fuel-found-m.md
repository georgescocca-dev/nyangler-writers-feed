# Ran to Wilmington instead of Hudson — burned less fuel, found more bigeye
*A chunk-all-night, troll-at-dawn trip on the east wall paid off with bigeye, a stacked yellowfin bite, and our first white marlin shot of the season.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 21** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

I had a decision to make Tuesday afternoon — Hudson or Wilmington. Both canyons were showing decent reports, but Hudson meant another 20 miles of running each way, and with fuel where it is, that's not nothing on a 30-hour trip. I looked at the eddy positioning, saw the warm water pushing in tighter to Wilmington's east wall than it had been the week before, and made the call to save the diesel and fish closer to home. Good call this time — it usually pays to fish what's in front of you instead of chasing a rumor 40 miles up the shelf.

We ran out to the 600-fathom curve on the east side, around the corner where the canyon starts widening out past the Baby Wilmington, and found a hard color change — hard blue water butting up against a greener, cooler push off the shelf. That's the kind of edge that holds bait, and it held bait. Flying fish were nervous on the surface most of the afternoon, always a good sign, and we marked bait balls stacked from 120 feet down to almost 300 on the sounder right on the break.

We committed to the chunk that night instead of trolling into dark, and it was the right move. Set up on the edge with the current pushing us slowly along the wall, butterfish chunks in a slow drip, and by 10 pm we had our first bigeye — a fish right around 140 pounds that took a long time to get up from depth on 80-pound fluoro leader and a plain hook buried in a butterfish chunk. We picked at two more through the night, lost a couple to green mono and sharp turns near the boat, but ended up boating three bigeye by first light. That's a solid night's work for one boat — not a stacked-up, every-bait-getting-bit kind of bite, but steady enough that nobody was standing around.

Daylight, we pulled the chunk gear and put the spread back out — naked ballyhoo behind Sea Witches, a couple of small Ilanders in blue/white and green/yellow, and a spreader bar down the middle. Yellowfin showed up almost immediately working the same temperature seam, mostly in the 35- to 55-pound class, and we picked away at them trolling the break in both directions for about four hours before it went quiet around 10 am. Six yellowfin in the box, plus a wahoo that smashed a high-speed planer rig with a green Yo-Zuri bomber we had out wide — that fish nearly emptied a reel before we turned it.

We also had a legitimate white marlin shot — a fish came up on a dredge teaser mid-morning, wouldn't commit to a hookbait, but it's the first real billfish sign we've had out there this year. We flagged a couple weeks back that the marlin migration would build through July, and this is exactly the kind of thing you'd expect to start seeing right about now. Doesn't mean they're stacked up yet, but the pieces — warm water, bait, the right depth — are lining up.

Mahi were a bonus, not a target. We picked up four dolphin, biggest around 12 pounds, off a scattered weed line we crossed running between the chunk grounds and the troll spread the next morning. If you've got time between tuna stops, it's always worth a look at any floating junk or grass — a couple of small green/yellow feathers pulled fast will draw a strike almost every time there's fish under the weeds.

The bigger picture here is that this stable high-pressure stretch we've had for going on two weeks now is doing exactly what it's supposed to do — keeping the eddy water pinned up against the canyon wall instead of sliding off, and that's holding bait and fish in range instead of scattering them. We called the new moon tide would tighten bait up along the shelf break, and that's held true — the concentration we found on the sounder Tuesday night was about as tight as I've seen it this season.

It wasn't a lights-out trip. The daytime troll bite was good but not overwhelming — six yellowfin in four hours of trolling a known edge is solid work, not a feeding frenzy. If you're going, I'd plan around the chunk at night for the bigeye and treat the morning troll as the bonus round rather than the main event right now. That could flip if the yellowfin push harder onto the temp break like they were starting to Wednesday morning.

Looking ahead, if this high pressure holds through the weekend — and right now there's no reason to think it won't — I'd expect the pattern to keep building rather than fade. Wilmington, Hudson, Washington, and the canyons to the south are all reading similar setups. If you've got a trip planned, I'd fish the same wall we did, work the chunk overnight, and keep a dredge out during the troll in case that marlin sign turns into something more than a look.
