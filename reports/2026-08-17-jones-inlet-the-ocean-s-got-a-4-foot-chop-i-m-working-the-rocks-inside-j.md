# The Ocean's Got a 4-Foot Chop — I'm Working the Rocks Inside Jones Inlet
*While everyone chases blitz reports from up the coast, the real, honest bite this week is in the current seams behind the jetty and the back bay creeks.*

**WANTAGH, NY — August 17** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

My phone's been buzzing all week with guys sending me screenshots of bunker blitzes up in Rhode Island and the Cape — fish exploding on top in broad daylight, no low-light window needed. Good for them. Down here, the ocean's carrying a 3-to-4-foot chop out of the south with a short, sloppy period, and there's no version of that where I'm walking the tip of the Jones Beach jetty to get pounded by whitewater for a maybe-fish. So while half the surf guys are standing out there getting salt in their eyes for nothing, I've been working the calm water behind the rocks and inside the inlet, where the current does the work instead of the swell.

That's the read this week — the ocean's too worked up to fish comfortably, and the bay and inlet mouth are holding the temperature break that's actually stacking bait. We've got water pushing low-to-mid 70s out front and even warmer in the shallow bay flats, which means the stripers aren't dumb enough to feed hard in the heat of the day. I said last week the morning schoolies would keep showing on the same current lines at first light, and that's exactly what's happening — dawn bite, current seam off the north side rocks near the Coast Guard station, small bunker and peanuts getting pushed through on the last of the outgoing. Schoolies to 26 inches, a few slot fish mixed in if you're working a swimming plug — I've been throwing a bone-colored Yo-Zuri Mag Popper and a 3/4-ounce bucktail with a white Fin-S trailer, working it slow along the seam where the moving water meets the slack. Best 45 minutes of the day, every day, right around first light before the sun gets up over the dunes. After that it shuts down hard until the light drops again in the evening — that's the trade-off with water this warm, you get two tight windows and a whole lot of dead air in between.

Bluefish are the one thing that hasn't slowed a bit. Snapper blues are thick as fleas in the back bay creeks off Freeport and up around the Wantagh Bridge pilings, little guys 6 to 10 inches hammering anything shiny under a bobber — great way to get a kid hooked on this sport, literally. But the adult class is building behind them just like I figured it would. I'm seeing 3- to 4-pound blues mixed right in with the bunker schools inside the inlet mouth, and they're not shy about it — silver spoons, small metal, even bucktails get bit the second they hit bottom. If you're marking bait balls getting nervous on your sonar drifting past the Jones Inlet Coast Guard station, that's blues underneath, not stripers, more often than not this time of year.

Fluke's a mixed bag right now and I won't lie to you about it. The Sloop Channel drift has been giving up fish, but it's a lot of shorts for every keeper — the fish are bunching up near the inlet mouth where the tide keeps things cooler than the stagnant flats further up the bay, which is exactly the pattern you'd expect with the bay water sitting warm and lazy. I'm drifting 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 1-ounce bucktails, staying tight to the channel edges in 15 to 20 feet, moving with the current instead of fighting it. Some days you'll put three or four keepers in the cooler over a few hours, other days you're culling through twenty shorts for one fish over 19 inches. If the wind lays down and gives you a clean drift on the last two hours of the incoming, that's your best shot — the fish get more aggressive when there's a little push of water carrying bait past them instead of them having to hunt for it.

Now here's the one I'm actually excited about — weakfish are showing again in South Oyster Bay, and I don't say that lightly because it's been a rough decade for that fishery around here. Guys working the flats off Short Beach after dark with bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, or soft plastics like a 4-inch pearl swimming shad, have been picking off weakfish in the 2- to 4-pound range on the deeper edges where the flat drops into the channel. It's not a stacked bite — you might get two or three bites in a night — but every one of those fish matters right now, because if this bay-side comeback is real, it's the best news this inlet's had in years. Handle them gentle if you're letting them go.

Looking ahead, we're building toward the full moon on the 28th, and that's going to push more water through this inlet than we've seen in weeks. That usually means the bunker get shoved through faster and the stripers and blues get more concentrated around the current breaks instead of spread out over the flats. I'd expect the dawn bite to hold steady through the week and probably sharpen up as the tides build — if I had to pick one morning to be standing on the rocks with a popper in my hand, I'd wait for a morning with a light northwest wind and fish the last two hours of the outgoing. That's when this inlet shows you what it's really got.
