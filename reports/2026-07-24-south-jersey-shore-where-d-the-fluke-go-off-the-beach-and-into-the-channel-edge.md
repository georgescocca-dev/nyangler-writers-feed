# Where'd the fluke go? Off the beach and into the channel edges
*A lesson in why keeper fluke disappear from the sod banks in late July — and where they're hiding instead.*

**ABSECON, NJ — July 24** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

Got the same question three times this week, from three different anglers at three different docks, so let's turn it into a lesson. \"Darnell, I fished the sod banks in Absecon same as always and all I caught was shorts. What happened to the fluke?\"\n\nHere's what happened. It's July 24th, the back bays have been baking under stable southwest wind and high sun for going on two weeks, and shallow water that read comfortable in June is now running warm enough that a keeper fluke — the mature, 19-inch-and-up female — has options, and she's taking them. She's moved off the skinny sod bank flats where you found her on the last new moon and settled into the deeper channel edges and inlet structure where the water stays cooler and the current runs harder. This is textbook mid-summer behavior. I've watched it happen every July for thirty years and it never fails to catch somebody off guard.

So class, where do you go? Three spots I'd put students on this week. First, the Absecon Inlet channel itself, working the deeper cuts along the Rum Point side on the last two hours of the outgoing. Drop a 3/4-ounce white bucktail tipped with a 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet — chartreuse or pink shad — down into 18 to 24 feet and drag it slow along bottom, right where the channel bends. That current edge concentrates bait, and bait concentrates fluke big enough to matter. Second, the Great Egg Harbor channel off Longport, same tide stage, same depth range — the outgoing there really rips past the marina and any fluke holding on that edge is doing it because the water's moving fast enough to stay oxygenated and cool. Third, and this one gets overlooked, the Ocean City back bay channel behind the 9th Street bridge — deeper hole there runs 15 to 20 feet and it's held quality fish for me in past Julys when the flats went quiet.

Tide stage matters more than usual right now. Dead low and dead high, forget it — that's when the shallow water sits stagnant and warm and even the shorts get lazy. You want the two hours surrounding max current, either direction, when water's actually exchanging. That's when the thermal layering breaks down enough to pull a keeper up off the bottom to feed.

Now the other half of the report, and this one doesn't need a lesson plan because it's happening right in front of everybody's face. The bluefish blitz along the beachfront has done exactly what I told you it would do last week — bunker are still pinned tight to the beach by that same southwest wind, and blues have been running them from Stone Harbor and Avalon straight up through Ocean City and into Brigantine. We flagged this pattern holding as long as the wind stayed southwest and the bait stayed pinned, and that's exactly what's played out. If you're on the beach at first light with a white or chrome Kastmaster, an Ava jig, or a bucktail, and you see gulls working tight over nervous water, you don't need to think twice — cast into the edge of the school, not the middle, and hang on. Fish are running 3 to 6 pounds, some better, and they'll hit topwater poppers just as readily if you want the visual. Best windows have been dawn and again the last hour of daylight, when the sun's off the water and the blues push the bunker up shallow.

Striped bass have gone almost entirely nocturnal on us, which is normal for this stretch of the calendar with surface water running into the mid-70s. If you want a bass right now, you're fishing Absecon Inlet or Townsends Inlet after dark, working the deeper structure and current seams with bunker chunks or a swimming plug worked slow along the jetty rocks. Daytime bass fishing in the back bays is mostly dead right now — I wouldn't waste a summer morning on it when the fluke and blues are both giving better odds.

One more thing worth watching closely, and it's not fully my beat but it touches the southern end of it — weakfish are showing real signs of life up in the Delaware Bay back-bay channels. That's a pattern building on warm bay water and tide-change concentration, not a one-day fluke of activity, and if it holds and pushes toward Great Egg the way these things sometimes do, I'll have more to say about it next week.

Looking ahead, if the southwest wind holds through the weekend like it's been doing, expect the bluefish blitz to keep building and possibly push further north toward Atlantic City's beachfront by Sunday. For fluke, I'd keep working those channel edges on the moving tide rather than wasting daylight on the flats — that's where the keepers are living until we get a real cool-down or a wind shift stirs the shallow water back up. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd be on the Absecon Inlet channel at first light, bucktail in hand, working the last of the outgoing before the blues show up and remind me there's easier meat to be had thirty yards away.
