# Peanut Bunker Push Into the Middle Ground — Bass Slide Into the Dark
*Bait's stacked thick from Norwalk to Port Jeff, but the bass have gone nocturnal on you — here's how to find them anyway.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 18** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spent Tuesday morning idling off Middle Ground Light with the sounder up and my eyes on the surface more than the screen, and here's what I found: peanut bunker balled up tight in maybe fifteen feet of water, nervous, flickering, with terns working them from above but no visible boils underneath. That's the tell. When the bait's getting pushed but nothing's exploding on it in daylight, the bass are down there working it after dark instead. Kings Point is reading 71.6 degrees this week — that's warm enough to push stripers into a thermal retreat, not gone, just smarter about when they eat.

This is the pattern across the whole Central Sound right now. Bunker schools — some peanut-sized, some already three to four inches and hardening up — are stacked from the Norwalk Islands down through Stratford Shoal and across to the Connecticut side off Charles Island. Where you find those schools tight to the bottom on the sounder during the day, you're looking at a night program. Anchor up on the drop-off edges around Stratford Shoal in eighteen to twenty-five feet as the sun goes down, soak a live bunker or a chunk on a fish-finder rig, and give it an hour either side of the tide change. I heard from two guys working the graveyard shift off Middle Ground last week who put overslot fish in the boat between eleven and one in the morning — nothing epic, two or three fish a trip, but real bass, upper 30s to low 40 inches, on fresh bunker chunks fished tight to bottom.

Daytime, the story shifts. If you want bass with the sun up, you need moving current and structure that breaks it — the last two hours of the ebb around the Norwalk Islands or the rips off Charles Island are still producing, but you're fishing the seam, not the flat. Bucktails in the three-quarter to one-ounce range, white or chartreuse, worked slow along the current break, have been the better call than anything trolled through open water. This lines up with what I've been seeing for weeks now — the fish that do feed in daylight are doing it where the tide concentrates bait against structure, not out in the middle of the Sound where the water's just soaking up sun.

Fluke fishing is the one thing I can point to and say is dependable right now, no asterisks. Stratford Shoal and the rip lines off Middle Ground continue to hold keeper-class fish in eighteen to thirty feet, and they're not picky — white Gulp Swimming Mullets on half-ounce bucktails, tipped with a squid strip, dragged slow on the drift, are doing the damage. I had a report of a six-pound fluke off the Shoal on the last of an incoming tide, which tells you these fish are still using that current to set up ambushes on the same bait pods the bass are shadowing at night. Porgies are riding shotgun on the same structure — anchor up on any rocky patch inside the Norwalk Islands with sandworms or clam and you'll fill a bucket without much drama, though nothing trophy-sized to report.

Bluefish are the wildcard piece right now. They're not blitzing the surface like the beachfront stuff further south, but they're absolutely underneath these same bunker schools, and when you find a school getting nervous with no birds working it, drop a diamond jig or a bucktail through it and you'll usually connect. I had blues to six pounds come through off Port Jefferson harbor mouth chasing peanut bunker right at the mouth of the channel — fun fish on light tackle, and they'll keep showing wherever that bait holds.

Weakfish have been quieter than I'd like to report, but a few calm, warm evenings lately have started to rebuild some activity around the deeper holes off the Connecticut shore — nothing consistent enough to plan a trip around yet, but worth a few casts with a soft plastic on the dropoffs if you're already out there after dark for bass.

We flagged bonito working into the Western Sound last week, and while nothing's crossed my transom yet here in the Central stretch, the bait conditions are exactly what you'd want if that push is coming — tight bunker schools, warm surface water, clean edges around Stratford Shoal and the Norwalk Islands where bonito like to slash through. I'll be watching close over the next week, particularly on calm mornings when the water's glassy enough to spot fins.

The moon's building toward first quarter on the 21st, which means tides are easing, not surging — good news for the slower bucktail presentations working the rips, tougher for anybody hoping for a hard-current blitz. If I had one trip this week, I'd fish the last two hours of the ebb off Charles Island for daylight bass on bucktails, then come back after dark and anchor a chunk off Stratford Shoal for the bigger nocturnal fish. Bring both game plans — this water's not making it easy on you right now, but the bait's here, and where the bait goes, eventually the fish follow.
