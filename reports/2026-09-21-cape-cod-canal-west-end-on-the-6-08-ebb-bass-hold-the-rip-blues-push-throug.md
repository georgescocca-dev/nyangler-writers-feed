# West End on the 6:08 Ebb: Bass Hold the Rip, Blues Push Through Behind
*Harvest Moon tides are tightening up the Canal's fall pattern — the current windows are getting sharper and the bass are starting to answer on schedule.*

**SAGAMORE, MA — September 21** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

6:08 this past Thursday evening — that's when the west end ebb really got its shoulders into it, current running a solid 4-plus knots off the Mashnee side, and that's when the rip line off Hog Island turned from a lazy seam into a real striper conveyor belt. That's the number to know right now. The Canal has been a stop-and-start bass fishery for three weeks — good one tide, dead the next — but the Harvest Moon's bigger pulls are starting to iron that out, and the last several days have been the most predictable stretch of the young fall run.

Here's the why. Bigger moon tides mean more water moving faster through a narrow ditch, and that means bait gets flushed hard against the current seams instead of drifting around loose. Peanut bunker and small mackerel schools that have been scattered through Buzzards Bay and Cape Cod Bay are getting pinned against the rip lines at both ends on the max current, and the bass that have been trickling into the Canal on the fall push are finally getting a clock they can set their feeding by. We flagged this last week — said the bass were building at the Canal and that the tide sets would dictate when they stack against the rip — and that's exactly what's playing out now on both ends.

On the east end, the pattern is the last ninety minutes of the east-to-west flood, when the current stacks up over the drop-off by the Sandwich side and Scusset Beach's rip starts throwing white water. That's been the most consistent window of the week — topwater walk-the-dog plugs at first light, then switching to a pink or mackerel-pattern bucktail once the sun's up and the bass slide down out of the wash. Fish have run mostly schoolie-to-mid-20s class, with enough 30-inch-plus mixed in on the harder tides to keep rods bent. Scusset's been a mixed bag of striper and bluefish — the blues have been riding shotgun on the same bait pushes, hitting mackerel jigs and topwater with the kind of aggression that shreds a soft plastic in two swipes. If you're throwing gulp or paddletails on the east end, bring extras.

West end is where the more consistent numbers have come from this week. The seam that forms off Mashnee Island and runs down past Hog Island on the back half of the ebb has been holding fish stacked tight against the current break — not chasing bait on top, but sitting low and letting it wash to them. White bucktails, 1.5 to 2 ounces depending on how hard the current's running, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right on bottom, have been the most reliable producer. Chunk bait — fresh mackerel or bunker chunks fished on the bottom in the eddy pockets along the rip — has also been quietly productive for anglers willing to sit still and let the current do the work instead of covering water. Phinneys Harbor and Wings Neck have both been holding smaller fish and some fluke activity in the calmer water off the main current, worth a look if the main rip is too crowded or blown out.

Bluefish have been the more consistent player of the two species through the week, which tracks with what's happening south of here — bigger bunker schools funneling through on these Harvest Moon tides have blues riding every bait push from New Jersey up through southern New England, and the Canal's getting its share. Expect that to keep building over the next several days as more forage gets pushed through the system.

False albacore and bonito haven't shown in numbers inside the Canal itself — that's not really their water — but the push out of Rhode Island and up through Narragansett Bay and Fishers Island Sound has been aggressive enough that anglers working the Cape Cod Bay side outside Scusset and the Buzzards Bay side off Wings Neck should keep an eye out, especially on calm, bait-rich mornings. If that westward push keeps broadening the way it's been, the mouth of the Canal on either end wouldn't be a surprise place to see the first fin flashes of the season.

Looking at the next few days: the moon's still working in our favor, current speeds should stay elevated through the weekend before the tides start easing back toward the following neap cycle. That means the windows stay sharp — hit the last of the flood on the east end at first light, hit the back half of the ebb on the west end in the evening, and don't waste time fishing the slack in between unless you're specifically working bottom for fluke in the calmer stretches off Phinneys Harbor. If a cold front pushes through behind this pattern, expect the bait to compress even harder against structure and the bite to get shorter but more intense — worth adjusting to smaller windows and being ready the moment the current turns. Right now, the Canal is doing what it does best in fall: rewarding anglers who fish the clock, not the daylight.
