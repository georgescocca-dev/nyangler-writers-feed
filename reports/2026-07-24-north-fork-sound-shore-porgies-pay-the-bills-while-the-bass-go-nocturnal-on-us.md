# Porgies pay the bills while the bass go nocturnal on us
*Daytime stripers have gone quiet with the heat, but scup on the reefs and a slowing Gut current are keeping the North Fork honest.*

**MATTITUCK, NY — July 24** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

Old Ray Tuthill used to say a hot July sun does two things to a striper — makes him smart and makes him lazy. Ray's been dead eleven years now but I thought about him three mornings running this week, standing on the rocks off Horton Point at six a.m. with nothing showing but a few hopeful terns and water so flat and warm-looking you could've poured it over ice cream. No fish. Not a swirl. By eight the sun's up over Shelter Island way and it's plain why — that water's pushing into the 70s right off the beach, and a bass with any sense at all has gone down deep or gone home to sleep it off till dark.

That's the story this week, plain and simple. Kings Point over west is reading near 71, and even out here where New London's holding closer to 69, the shallow structure along our stretch — the boulder fields off Rocky Point, the rip lines coming off Orient — warms up fast enough by midday that the bass want no part of it in daylight hours. I've fished this shoreline sixty years and I've seen this pattern more summers than not. When it sets in, you stop being a daytime fisherman and you start being a flashlight-and-thermos fisherman, or you go find something else that bites.

Something else that bites, out here, is porgy. And thank God for porgy, because without scup this would've been a real quiet column. The rockpiles off Mattituck Inlet and the rougher bottom running toward Rocky Point have been steady — not spectacular, steady, which in my book is worth more. Anchor up on the drop where the mussel bed gives way to broken rock, drop a hi-lo rig with squid strips or sandworm on size 6 or 8 hooks, and you'll keep a rod bent most of the tide. Biggest ones — some pushing a pound and a half, nice fat scup — are coming off the deeper edge on the last of the outgoing, before the current goes fully slack. Bring enough bait. They'll pick you clean if you're stingy with the hooks.

Now the bass — and I don't want to paint too rosy a picture, because daytime effort for keeper stripers along the Sound side has been tough sledding. But work the dusk into full dark, and there's still fish to be had. I had a report from a fella working the boulder line east of the inlet who took two decent bass, one about 30 inches, working a black Danny plug on the swing right at the edge of the rocks, right as the light went out of the sky. That's the program now — last hour of light through the first couple hours of full dark, current moving, plug or bucktail worked slow and tight to structure. Daytime, you're better off dropping down with bait — clam or bunker chunk fished deep off the drop-offs — if you insist on fishing the sun hours at all.

Bluefish have been mixing into bait pods right along the beach, chopping up whatever's schooled up tight to shore. Nothing like the blitzes they're seeing to the west, from what I hear, but enough that if you're casting metal — a Hopkins or a Kastmaster — off the beach at Rocky Point in the early morning you can find a few and get your arm tired. Good practice while you wait for the bass to cooperate.

Now for the part I actually want to talk about — the Gut. We said last week the neap tide moving in around the 21st would slow the current through Plum Gut and the Race, and that's exactly what's happened. If you fished it this week you felt it — that push isn't near what it was two weeks back. Slower water means you need to adjust, and I mean it: switch down to lighter jigs, quarter to half-ounce bucktails instead of the heavy iron you'd normally need to hold bottom in that current, and slow your retrieve down to match. Guys still muscling through with the same heavy gear they used at full moon tides are working too fast for what the fish want right now. There's still bass and some bluefish working through there, particularly on the last of the ebb toward Orient, but it's a finesse game until that current builds back.

Weakfish — I'll be honest, I haven't seen or heard much out of our stretch. There's talk of a real weakfish pattern building down in Delaware Bay, which is interesting but does me no good standing on a North Fork jetty. If that resurgence pushes any further up the coast I'll be the first out looking for them in the back guts near the inlet, but for now I'm not counting on it here.

Fluke's been what fluke always is in late July — plenty of shorts in the shallow water, and you've got to work the channel edges and the deeper humps off Mattituck Inlet on a moving tide to find anything worth keeping. White Gulp on a bucktail, dragged slow along bottom where the current cuts a groove — that's still the ticket, just don't expect every drift to produce a keeper.

Looking ahead — that current in the Gut should keep rebuilding through the week as we move off the neap, and when it does I expect the bass and blues stacked up waiting on bait to push through with a little more purpose. If the current builds and the water stays clean, I'd plan an evening around Orient Point toward the end of the week, working bucktails on the drop as the tide starts to run harder again. Daytime, stick with the porgies. They've been honest with me all summer, and honest is worth something.
