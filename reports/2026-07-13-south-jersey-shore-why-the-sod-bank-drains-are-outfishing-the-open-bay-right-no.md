# Why the sod bank drains are outfishing the open bay right now
*A lesson in current, temperature, and why the fluke you want are hugging the mud lines this week, not sitting out in the open sand.*

**ABSECON, NJ — July 13** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get the same question every July, usually from a guy leaning on the rail at the ramp with a rod he hasn't wet yet: "Why am I marking bait all over the open bay and not getting bit?" It's a fair question, and it's a teaching moment every time. The answer, almost always, is temperature and current working together, and this week that lesson is on full display from Absecon Inlet down through Great Egg.

The back bays have warmed into the high 70s on the flats, and the ocean side, if the recent Bight buoy numbers are any indication, is sitting closer to 73 degrees with a lazy 2 to 3-foot swell running on a 10-second period. That's not rough water, but it's enough to keep the surf a little disorganized and push bait tight to structure rather than spreading it out. So the fish — fluke especially — aren't loafing on open sand in this heat. They're stacking where the water actually moves and cools a few degrees: the drains coming off the sod banks on the outgoing tide.

That's the pattern I've been fishing and teaching all week. Absecon Inlet's Rainbow Channel and Risley Channel edges, eight to fifteen feet, dropping tide, are the classroom. As that water pours off the marsh it's carrying grass shrimp and killies with it, and the fluke sit right on the edge of that current seam waiting for the buffet to come to them. A 3/4-ounce white or chartreuse bucktail tipped with a 4-inch Gulp swimming mullet, drifted slow and bounced just off bottom, has been the winning combination. Keep the boat moving with the current, not against it — that's the mistake I see most, guys trying to power-drift a falling tide and dragging the bait through the strike zone too fast. Slow it down. Let the current do the work. We've had keeper fluke to 22 inches coming out of that same seam three tides running, mixed in with a lot of shorts that need to go back — the size class isn't uniformly good, so expect to cull.

Graveling Point and the Great Egg back channels are fishing similarly, though I'd call that bite more moderate than hot — a few keepers scattered among a lot of teen-inch fish. If you're marking grass and mud bottom transitioning to harder sand on your graph, that's the spot to make repeat drifts.

Weakfish have been a first-light proposition only. Once the sun climbs and that back bay water heats up further, they slide off the flats and go quiet. Best window has been that gray hour right at dawn on the last of the incoming in the Rainbow Channel and up toward Longport, using a 1/4-ounce jighead with a pearl or chartreuse Gulp shrimp, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right along the channel edges where the mud gives way to shell. Nothing gaudy — a handful of legal fish mixed with a lot of short ones, but if you're up early it's worth the rods.

Bluefish are giving the inlet some life on the tail end of the incoming, chunked bunker fished on the bottom right along the Absecon Inlet rock jetty producing cocktail blues in the two-to-three-pound class, with the occasional heavier fish mixed in. If you'd rather cast than sit, diamond jigs worked through the same rip will connect just as well and keep you moving.

Kingfish are doing what they always do in mid-July — patrolling the surf troughs at Ocean City and Sea Isle City on incoming water, and they've been a reliable, if modest, producer. High-low rigs with small hooks, bloodworm or Fishbites, cast just past the first break. It's not a numbers game most mornings, but it's a teaching bite — good for kids and for anyone who wants steady action without needing a boat.

Striped bass are in their typical summer sulk. The resident fish haven't disappeared, but they've gone nocturnal and structure-bound. If you want a shot, fish bunker chunks on the bottom around the Absecon Inlet rocks after dark, on the last two hours of the incoming, and be patient. This isn't a volume bite in July — it's one or two quality fish for the guys willing to put in the hours after midnight.

Triggerfish have started showing on the near reef structure off Great Egg and around the outer rock piles at the inlet, which is right on schedule for this time of year. Small pieces of clam on a size 4 hook, light enough to still get bit but strong enough to handle their surprisingly stubborn pull. It's a finesse fishery — more about presentation and patience than volume — but a welcome change of pace when the fluke bite goes quiet on the tide change.

Looking ahead, the moon is building toward new phase this week, which means bigger tide swings and stronger current through the inlets — good news for the sod bank pattern, since more water moving means more bait getting flushed off those flats. If the swell backs off like it's been trending, I'd expect the surf kingfish bite to clean up and the triggerfish action on the reef structure to firm up too. My plan for the coming week is simple: fish the outgoing around Absecon and Great Egg for fluke, be on the water at first light for weakfish, and save the inlet rocks for after dark if stripers are the target. That's where the lesson plan says to be.
