# 71.2°F on the Bight Buoy, and the Warm Filament Still Sits East of the Wall
*The temp break that's been feeding the yellowfin bite hasn't moved — which means the run gets longer before it gets easier.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 9** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

71.2°F. That's the read off the buoy sitting out in the Bight this week, and while that number is inshore of where we actually fish, it tells me something useful: the shelf water pushing offshore hasn't cooled down, and whatever's feeding the warm filament east of the Hudson wall is still getting resupplied. I've said it for two weeks running now — the core of warm water that's been driving the yellowfin bite is parked east of the canyon, not directly over it. That hasn't changed, and it's the single biggest factor in how your day goes out there right now.

Run time from Manasquan to the top of the canyon is a little over 90 miles, call it four hours at a comfortable 22 knots, and once you're standing on the 100-fathom line the story is consistent with what I told you last weekend: the real concentration of bait and fish is still off to the east, sitting on the temperature breaks rather than parked on Hudson structure itself. We flagged this on the 4th — said the warm filament feeding the yellowfin bite all week was still in place east of here and the program would stay the same east-wall, temp-break game. It has. Boats willing to run the extra 8 to 12 miles off the edge and work the 66-to-70-degree color changes are finding the better grade of fish. Boats stopping right on the wall are getting a mixed bag — some marks, some quiet stretches, nothing you'd call consistent.

What's actually working: trolling a mixed spread of small green machines and blue/white Ilanders pulled behind bird teasers at 6 to 6.5 knots wherever the water color shifts from that green-gray inshore water to the deeper cobalt blue. Naked ballyhoo rigged on 50-wide setups off the long riggers has been drawing strikes when the trolled plastic gets ignored — August yellowfin in the 25-to-45-pound class can be particular about that. When you mark bait balls on the sounder, especially anywhere the temp break tightens up, shutting down and chunking butterfish has paid off better than staying on the troll. Small pink-and-white jigs worked around any floating weed lines are worth a few casts too — mahi have been scattered under the grass mats this month, nothing like a June school-fish blitz, but enough to fill a fish box if you're patient and willing to idle and look.

For the bottom guys, the swordfish game hasn't changed much either — daytime drops in 1,400 to 1,800 feet along the drop-offs adjacent to the canyon proper, squid strip and glow combos on the deep drop rigs, drifting with the current rather than fighting it. That part of the fishery doesn't care much where the warm filament sits on the surface; the deep scattering layer and the bottom temperature structure down there are far more stable week to week than what's happening up top.

I want to be straight with you on the overall grade of the bite: it's good, not great, and it's location-dependent in a way that punishes guys who won't run the extra distance. I talked to a couple of captains at the dock this week who worked the immediate canyon structure and came home with two or three fish for the trip — not bad, but not what they were hoping for given the time of year. The boats that pushed east onto the sharper temperature breaks did noticeably better, with some solid multi-fish days on yellowfin and a mahi or two mixed in under the weed. White marlin have been quiet on our side of the canyon this week — I haven't had a confirmed release to report, and with the bulk of pelagic activity sitting east, that's not surprising. If billfish are the priority, that's another reason to make the longer run rather than working the immediate Hudson structure.

Sea state has been manageable — nothing brutal, moderate swell, the kind of day you can work a spread comfortably without beating up the crew. Wind's been cooperative too, which matters more than people give it credit for when you're running 90-plus miles each way.

Looking ahead: the new moon lands August 12, and tidal current is already building toward that peak range. We called this too — said building tidal current into the new moon should sharpen bait concentrations along whatever edge holds the warm water, and that's exactly the mechanism to watch this week. Stronger current usually means tighter bait balls and cleaner marks on the temp breaks, which should make the east-wall program even more productive if the filament holds its position. The variable that matters most right now is whether that warm core drifts west toward the canyon itself over the next several days — if it does, the bite comes back to Hudson proper and cuts your run time down. If it stays put, keep planning on the longer push east. I'd plan for the second scenario until the water tells me otherwise, but I'll be watching the break position daily and adjusting the run accordingly. Either way, get your baits in the water early — the morning bite window on the temp breaks has been outproducing the afternoon two trips out of three this month.
