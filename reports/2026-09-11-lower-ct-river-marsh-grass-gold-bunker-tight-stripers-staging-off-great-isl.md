# Marsh Grass Gold, Bunker Tight — Stripers Staging Off Great Island
*The new moon tide squeezed bait against the river mouth this week, and the first real fall commitment from bass is here — patchy but building.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — September 11** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The marsh grass along Great Island has gone from green to that first dull gold, and the ospreys that spent the summer working the flats are mostly gone south already — a couple stragglers hanging around the DEP Marina osprey platform, but the bulk of them pulled out with the last of August. That's the calendar telling you what the water's telling you too: the shad run is six months behind us, the summer pattern is breaking up, and the river mouth is sliding into its fall identity, which around here always means one thing — bunker getting squeezed and bass starting to lean into them.

The new moon that came through on the 10th put a real charge into the tide cycle, bigger swings than we've seen since the spring freshet, and that's exactly the kind of push that tightens bait against structure. We said last week that striper staging would intensify as bunker concentrated near the mouth ahead of the fall run, and that's what the fleet is finding now — not a blitz, not yet, but a real pattern building at the mouth and down along Great Island's edges where the river meets the Sound. Guys working the flats off Old Lyme are marking bunker schools stacked tighter than they've been all summer, pushed up against the eastern edge of the channel on the last of the ebb.

Water's still carrying late-summer warmth — nothing that's screaming fall yet temperature-wise, but the nights are cooling fast enough that surface temps are sliding a degree or two lower every few days. That's the trigger. Bass don't commit hard to bait until the water gives them a reason to burn the calories, and the first cool nights of September are starting to do that. The bite right now is a morning and evening game — first light and the last hour of daylight, when the light's low and the bunker are pinned tightest against the grass edges and the deeper cuts off Great Island.

Tactically, this is a moving-bait, moving-boat pattern more than a soak-and-wait one. The better reports are coming from anglers working bunker spoons and umbrella rigs along the edge of the main channel from the mouth up past Saybrook Point, targeting the deeper water where the current rips hardest on the ebb — 15 to 25 feet, right where the river dumps into the Sound. Livelining bunker snagged right off the surface schools is producing too, fished on a simple three-way rig with just enough weight to keep the bait down in the current, drifted along the drop-offs on the Old Lyme side. Bass coming on these baits are mostly schoolie to mid-20-inch fish so far, with a scattering of better fish in the upper slot mixed in — nothing epic, but a legitimate uptick from where things sat two weeks ago.

Bluefish are riding shotgun with the bass right now, and the snapper blues have been thick in the marina basins and up around the Essex waterfront all week, which is the usual precursor to bigger blues showing more consistently as the water cools further. Small metal — quarter-ounce Kastmasters, Deadly Dicks — worked fast through the snapper schools off the docks will keep a rod bent for anyone bringing kids down after school, and it's worth watching those snapper concentrations, because that's often the tell for when the adult blues push into the same water the bass are working.

White perch fishing has quietly been solid in the upper stretches around Essex, working the deeper holes off the town dock on the last of the incoming with small bucktails tipped with a sliver of bloodworm or a piece of clam — not glamorous, but a dependable September pattern while the bigger fish sort themselves out down at the mouth.

Looking at the next several days, the tide energy from the new moon hasn't fully bled off yet, and that's the thing to lean on. Bait should stay concentrated through the coming tide cycles as the moon pulls away from new phase, and if the pattern from this week holds, the staging at Great Island and the mouth should keep tightening rather than scattering. The conditional here is water temperature — if we get a real string of cool nights this week, expect that staging to convert into the first legitimate blitz activity at the mouth within the next tide or two, the same jump we're already seeing confirmed up and down the coast this month. If the warmth hangs on a little longer, the pattern stays what it is now: a good, honest morning-and-evening bite for anglers willing to work the tide edges rather than a wide-open event.

For anyone with just one window this weekend, the last two hours of the ebb at first light off Great Island is the play — bunker schools pushed tight against the grass, current ripping the edge of the channel, and bass that have started, finally, to treat this as more than a passing meal.
