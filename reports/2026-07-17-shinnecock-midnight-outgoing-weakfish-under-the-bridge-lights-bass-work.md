# Midnight Outgoing: Weakfish Under the Bridge Lights, Bass Working the Inlet Rocks
*The day boats have the fluke drift, but after dark is when Shinnecock really shows its hand.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — July 17** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday night to skip the last two hours of daylight and just wait. Every July I get anglers asking why I don't fish the flood at the inlet — and every July I tell them the same thing: the bass in that rock pile want moving water and darkness, not sunshine and slack. So I sat at the jetty with a thermos of coffee until the tide turned, and by 11:45 the outgoing had built enough push to make the eels come alive on the drift. That's the window. Not sunset, not the first hour of outgoing — the meat of it, an hour to ninety minutes after the turn, when the current's ripping hard enough to hold bait against the rocks on the ocean side.

The inlet bass have been there, but they're not stupid about it. With the open ocean running warm — 71-plus out past the bar — the fish are using the inlet as a highway between the cooler, moving water inside and the bait pushing through on the tide. I've been anchoring up short of the Coast Guard station in that deep hole, 18 to 22 feet, and drifting whole and half-eels back with just enough weight to hold bottom without snagging the rip current seams. Two solid bass in the 28 to 34-inch class over the last four nights, plus a handful of shorts that came off before I could get a hand on them. It's not a blitz. It's a game of patience and staying quiet — no deck lights, no slamming hatches, just the hum of the outgoing doing the work. If you're going to fish it, fish it like you're not supposed to be there.

Ponquogue Bridge has been the more consistent story this week, and it's a night bite through and through. The pilings throw shadow lines under the sodium lights, and once that current starts running — best on the back half of the outgoing, when the flow drops but hasn't gone dead — the weakfish stack up right on the edge of light and dark. I've had guys tell me daytime jigging under there is a waste of gas in July, and this week I'd agree. After 10 p.m., though, small bucktails — quarter-ounce, white or chartreuse — tipped with a sliver of squid or a Gulp shrimp in the 3-inch size, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right along the shadow line, has been drawing weakfish in the 2 to 4-pound range, with one 6-pounder landed Thursday night that made the whole trip worth it. Keep your drag loose. Weakfish have paper mouths and this time of year the bite can be soft — more of a tick than a thump.

Daytime, the fluke drift in the bay has been fair, not great, and I'll be straight about that. The flats off Tiana and the deeper cuts toward the canal mouth are holding fish, but you're covering a lot of water for every keeper. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow over the sand-to-mud transitions in 8 to 14 feet, has been the winning combo, with fluke to 22 inches coming out of the deeper holes on the last two hours of the incoming, when the water clarifies up after the outgoing stirs the bottom. The canal itself is worth a look if you're chasing that extra push of current — the flow running down from the Peconic side has been dragging bait through, and there've been a few weakfish and short bass showing up right at the mouth where the canal dumps into the bay. It's not a secret spot, but it's underfished because most guys don't want to deal with the current there.

Bluefish have been a background character more than a headline this month. Snappers are thick in the bay — good news if you've got a kid with you and a bobber rig — but the jumbo blues have mostly stayed outside, working bait pushes past the inlet mouth early in the morning. If you want a shot at them, small metal — a Deadly Dick or a Kastmaster — worked fast at first light off the ocean side of the jetty has been the play, though I'd call that bite more opportunistic than reliable right now. Porgies inside the inlet on clam, fished tight to the rocks in 10 to 15 feet, have been steady and dependable — not exciting, but if you want a bucket of dinner fish while you wait for the tide to turn for bass, that's your move.

Looking ahead, the tides are building bigger as we push toward the next moon phase, and that usually means better current at the bridge and more consistent pushes through the inlet after dark. If that current keeps building the way I expect, I'd fish the outgoing at the inlet starting an hour after the turn, and I'd be at Ponquogue by 10 p.m. working those shadow lines on the back half of the ebb. The ocean's staying warm, which means the inside water keeps doing the heavy lifting — that's where the bait is, and that's where the fish will keep showing up. Bring bug spray. Bring patience. Leave the deck lights off.
