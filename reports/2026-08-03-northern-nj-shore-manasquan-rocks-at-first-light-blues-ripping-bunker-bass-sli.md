# Manasquan Rocks at First Light: Blues Ripping Bunker, Bass Sliding in Behind
*The bunker blitz we flagged two weeks back has spread the length of the beat — but you still need to be standing in the right spot when it happens.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 3** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Five-forty this morning, north jetty at Manasquan Inlet, and there's already three guys spread out on the rocks before the sky's done anything more than turn gray. One kid I didn't recognize was throwing a pencil popper like his arm was . An old-timer next to him — wire leader, bunker spoon, the whole rig looking like it hasn't changed since Nixon — wasn't saying a word, just working it slow and steady through the current seam off the tip. Neither one had a fish in the box yet, but neither one looked worried, and about ten minutes later I found out why. A pod of bunker came pushing out of the inlet on the last of the ebb, maybe forty yards off the rocks, and the water underneath them just came apart. Blues, and plenty of them, blowing up the surface like someone dropped a case of dynamite in the wash. The kid's popper got hit on the second cast after that. The old-timer's spoon got bit on the drop.

That's the story of this whole stretch right now, and it's been the story for a couple weeks — bunker schools holding thick from Belmar down through Manasquan, blues stacked underneath them and blowing up any time the bait gets pinned against structure. We said back on the 20th that this pattern would keep spreading north toward Sandy Hook and south toward LBI as long as the high pressure held, and that's exactly what's happened. I fished Sea Bright rocks Tuesday morning and the same thing was going on up there — bunker balled up tight against the third jetty down from the fishing pier, blues crashing them on the last hour of the incoming. It's a simple equation this time of year: find the bunker, find the blues. They're not hiding from each other.

Stripers are in on this too, but they're playing a different game. During the day, with surface temps sitting in the mid-to-upper 70s, the bass are down deep on structure — the rock piles off Spring Lake, the deeper pockets at Shark River Inlet, tucked under the blitz rather than in it. Dawn and dusk is when they come up to feed on the same bunker the blues are hammering, and that's your window. I've been picking off schoolies and a few keepers on white bucktails, an ounce and a half, worked slow along the bottom of the inlet channel at Manasquan on the last two hours of the outgoing — 15 to 20 feet of water, right where the current funnels past the rock pile. After dark, eels have been the better call for the bigger fish. Threw eels off the Belmar jetties two nights ago on the incoming and connected with a 34-incher that never showed itself until it was halfway up the rocks. That fish wasn't up chasing bunker on top — it was sitting deep, letting the current bring the meal to it.

Worth mentioning: the tide range is narrowing into the neap around the 5th and 6th, and when that current softens up, the bass tend to tighten into the strongest remaining flow and the deepest, coolest structure they can find. That means the bridges — Route 35, the Shark River span, the Manasquan River bridge pilings — start producing better than the open beach on eels and bucktails fished tight to the structure after dark. If the surf bite goes quiet next week, that's where I'm headed before I write it off as a bad week.

Fluke fishing has been the most reliable part of the report, honestly, and the least dramatic. This is peak season for them and it shows — doormat-class fish coming off the channel edges inside Manasquan Inlet and Shark River Inlet, plus the humps and rip lines just outside both. Five-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the drift in 20 to 30 feet, have been putting fish in the box, including one four-pounder I watched come over the rail of a boat working the Manasquan Inlet channel Wednesday on the last of the incoming. If you're fishing from the jetties for fluke, work the deeper pockets right off the rocks on the tail end of the outgoing — bait gets funneled out and the fluke sit right there waiting on it.

Here's the honest part: it's not been easy money every single dawn. I put in three mornings this week for two that were worth talking about. The blitz windows are real but they're short — twenty minutes, sometimes less, before the bunker push through and the blues go with them. You need to be standing in the right spot when the school comes by, which means showing up before first light and being willing to walk the jetty rocks in the dark to find where the bait's holding that particular tide. Guys who show up at seven and fish one spot for three hours are the ones telling me it was slow.

Looking ahead, I don't see any reason for the pattern to change much through the weekend — stable high pressure, warm stable water, bunker schools still thick enough to keep the blues committed. If the bait continues sliding south the way it has, don't be surprised if the hot zone drifts down toward Bay Head and beyond by the weekend. And once we're into that neap water midweek, start thinking bridges and deep structure after dark for the stripers, because the current's not going to be doing the work for them the way it has been. Bring eels. Bring patience. Watch the birds before you watch anything else.
