# Bunker Pods Stacked at Stratford Shoal — Bass Feeding Underneath, Not On Top
*The bait's telling you where the bass are, but you won't see them until the sun goes down.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — August 3** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spent Tuesday morning idling the edge of Stratford Shoal with the sun still low, and what I saw told me more than any temp gauge could. Big bunker pods — thick, dark rafts of them — pushed up tight against the rip, moving with the tide instead of against it, gulls working the edges but not diving hard. That's the tell. When the birds are hovering instead of crashing, the bunker aren't getting pressured from below. Not yet, anyway. Not in daylight.

That's been the story of the last week across Central Sound. The bait is here — bunker stacked at the Shoal, peanut bunker showing thick in the back coves off Oyster Bay and Cold Spring Harbor, silversides working the shallows around the Norwalk Islands on the last of the incoming. But the bass underneath them have gone quiet during the day, and the water tells you why. Kings Point is reading 70.9 on the west end, New London's still a touch cooler at 69.4 on the east end of my beat, and that gradient matters. The bass have pulled off the surface and dropped onto the deep structure — the drop at Middle Ground Light, the rockpile edges at Stratford Shoal, the deeper cuts between the Norwalk Islands — and they're waiting for dark.

We flagged this exact pattern last week — said the striper bite would stay a low-light player as long as surface temps sat in the mid-70s, and that's precisely what's playing out. If you're fishing this zone right now and you're not on the water for the last hour of light through the first couple hours of dark, you're fishing the wrong window.

The guys doing well on bass are working bunker schools at last light with big paddle-tail swimbaits — 9-inch white or bunker-colored — worked slow just under the school, or switching to live bunker on a fish-finder rig once it's properly dark, drifted along the Stratford Shoal rip on the last two hours of the ebb. That current edge concentrates everything — bait gets pushed and pinned, and the bass slide up out of the depths to work it without burning energy in warm surface water. Eels fished the same way off Middle Ground Light have also been producing after 9 PM, mostly mid-20s to low-30s fish, with the occasional better one mixed in.

Bluefish are the ones cashing in on the daylight hours the bass are giving up. Choppers are hammering those same bunker pods from mid-morning through early afternoon at Stratford Shoal and off Charles Island — if you see the surface actually erupt, that's blues, not bass. Bucktails and diamond jigs worked fast through the melee will connect, and there's enough size mixed in now — 3 to 6 pounds, some better — that it's worth carrying wire leader if you're throwing hardware into a busting school.

Fluke have been the most dependable part of the report, and nothing about that has changed. Structure-oriented drifts off the Norwalk Islands, the sod banks inside Oyster Bay, and the sandy stretches adjacent to the Port Jeff reef are still giving up a steady mix of keepers and shorts. White and chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow on the drift, continue to be the go-to. It's not a blitz bite — we said that last week too, and it's held — but it's a bite you can plan a trip around. Two to four keepers a trip if you cover water and stay on structure, mixed with plenty of teasers.

Porgies are thick on the Stratford Shoal rockpile and around Middle Ground, and if you want a stress-free afternoon with a cooler full of fish for the table, that's still the easy button right now — small hooks, sandworms or clam, anchored up tight to structure on the tide change. Blackfish season's a ways off yet, but a few incidental catches on the porgy rigs suggest they're already staging on that hard bottom.

Here's what I'm watching heading into next week. The moon's building toward new, and the tides are going to get stronger through the weekend into early next week — more current means more push on that Stratford Shoal rip, more current means bait gets displaced and concentrated harder against structure. That's usually the trigger that either wakes the bass up earlier in the evening or, if a front comes through and knocks the surface temp down even a couple degrees, could shift them back toward a more traditional daytime pattern. I'd keep an eye on any cooling trend this week — even a modest one could put fish back on top during normal hours instead of forcing everyone into the night shift.

And for those of you who know what I'm watching for this time of year — no bonito signs yet in Central Sound. It's early. The exposed edges toward Montauk and the Race tend to see them first, and once that push starts working west along the bait, the Norwalk Islands and Charles Island stretch are usually next in line. I'll be watching the bait, not the fleet, and I'll let you know the minute it shows.
