# Cockenoe on the Last of the Ebb: Fluke Steady, Bass Now a Night-Shift Game
*With Kings Point pushing past 70 and the reefs stable, the day bite belongs to fluke, bluefish and porgies while the stripers wait for dark.*

**NORWALK, CT — August 3** — _by Tony "Gold Coast" Bellafiore, Zone Writer_

Saturday morning I had a decision to make before the sun even cleared Sheffield Island: fish the flood on the inside of the Norwalk Islands where the moorings fill up by 9, or slide out to Cockenoe Reef and work the last two hours of the outgoing before the weekend armada showed up. I took Cockenoe. On the ebb, the bait gets pulled off that reef edge and funnels down into the sand seam on the east side, 18 to 22 feet, where the fluke stack up waiting for it. That's the chess move this time of year — fish the tide that moves bait away from structure, not the one that pins boats on top of you.

It paid off. Fluke to 5 pounds came off that seam on 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets threaded on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow with just enough lift to keep ticking bottom. Mixed in were plenty of shorts — same story we flagged last week, keeper-size fish scattered through a lot of undersized company, a steady grind rather than a blitz. That's exactly the pattern right now on every sand patch adjacent to structure from Cockenoe down to the flats off Penfield. If you're covering ground with the bucktail and getting hit but coming up short, don't leave — just slow the drift and let it sit a beat longer before the hop.

Bluefish are the other bright spot, and they're not subtle about it. Anywhere bait gets pushed against structure — the north side of the Norwalk Islands, the rip off Greenwich Point, the edge of the Cows — you'll see the surface go nervous mid-morning as the blues work through. Straight bucktail or a diamond jig with a tube worked fast through the wash will get bit instantly. Bigger fish have been mixing in with the smaller choppers, which tracks with a fresh wave pushing down the Sound. Wire leader isn't optional right now unless you enjoy losing tackle.

Porgies on the Cows are about as reliable as this zone gets in early August. Anchor up on the rockpile in 15 to 25 feet, soak sandworms or clam on a simple high-low rig with size 6 or 8 hooks, and you'll have a bucket of dinner fish inside an hour on the right stage of tide — I like the two hours either side of dead low, when the current slacks enough to hold bottom without a two-ounce sinker fighting you the whole drift. Black sea bass are riding shotgun on the same structure, showing up as a welcome bonus on the reef edges around Cockenoe and Penfield when you're jigging for the porgies or working a bucktail for fluke.

Stripers are the one piece of this puzzle that's changed gears. With Kings Point reading just under 71 degrees and holding, that's warm enough on the surface to push the bass down into the deeper basin water and onto a mostly nocturnal clock — this is the same pattern showing up west of us through the whole Sound. Daytime, they're not up chasing bait on the reef tops the way they were in June. At night, though, resident bunker schools are holding in the deeper water between the Norwalk Islands and Sheffield, and off Greenwich Point in that 30-to-40-foot basin, and that's where the bass are working them after dark. Live-lining a bunker under a balloon or working a bunker-imitation swimbait slow through that depth after 9 PM has been the move — quieter water, less pressure, and fish that are actually willing to feed instead of just holding deep and sulking through the heat of the day.

One thing I'm not doing right now is chasing blackfish. It's too early — that's an October-into-November program on the rockpiles at Penfield and Greenwich Point once the water starts sliding back down through the 50s. Anybody telling you different this week is either guessing or fishing a spot I haven't seen produce yet.

Boat traffic is the other variable you have to play around all August. Sheffield Island and the inner Norwalk Islands turn into a parking lot by mid-morning on weekends, so if you're working structure there, get it done on the early stages of the tide before 9 AM or wait until the dinner crowd clears out around 5. Cockenoe and Penfield see traffic too but there's more room to spread out and still find clean water on the drift.

Looking ahead, I don't expect a big shift in the next several days. The thermal gradient across the Sound — warmer here in the west, cooler as you move toward New London — should hold, which means the fluke and porgy bite stays steady and the bass stay nocturnal unless we get a real cooling push or a wind event that mixes the water column. Tides are building slightly as we work toward the next new moon, and if that current gets some teeth to it, I'd expect the night bass bite off Greenwich Point and in the Cockenoe basin to sharpen up. That's where I'll be putting my time this week — bucktails and Gulp through the fluke window at first light, then back out after dark for the bass once the current really starts moving.
