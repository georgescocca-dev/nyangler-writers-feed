# 72.9°F on the Bight Buoy — the Real Break Is 40 Miles Out at the Dip
*A clean 2.3-foot sea and a green-to-blue color change at the 100-fathom curve turned a mixed week into a genuine yellowfin bite for boats that ran the extra ten miles.*

**SHINNECOCK INLET, NY — July 13, 2026** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

72.9°F. That's what the NY Bight buoy read this week, with a 2.3-foot swell riding in out of the southeast on a clean 10-second period. Numbers like that tell me two things before I even leave the dock: the ocean's got some shape to it but nothing that keeps you home, and the surface layer on the shelf is warm enough that it's not doing much work for us inshore of the canyon. The real story this week wasn't the shelf water — it was the color change and the temperature break sitting right on top of the 100-fathom curve, roughly along the edge running from the Dip down toward the Claw.

I ran out of Shinnecock Tuesday and again Friday, and both trips the pattern was the same: green, chlorophyll-rich water pushed out to about the 60-fathom line, then a hard wall into cleaner blue water as you crossed onto the canyon edge itself. That's your foraging zone right there — bait gets pinned against that edge, current wraps it around the contour, and everything from squid to sand eels gets concentrated exactly where the yellowfin want to sit. I marked the break at 39°34'N, 72°31'W on Tuesday, and it had slid about six miles southwest by Friday — that's a fast-moving edge, so don't trust a waypoint from last week's report. Check your own chlorophyll overlay the morning you run.

The yellowfin bite off that break has been real but not a slam dunk. We trolled a standard spread — Green Machines and small Ilanders off the shorts, naked ballyhoo skirted with sea witches out the riggers — and picked at fish in the 25 to 45-pound class along the color change in about 250 to 900 feet of water. Two solid trolling bites both days, then it shut down hard once the sun got high. That's when we switched to jigging — vertical, fast-fall metal jigs like a Nomad Vertrex or a Shimano Butterfly, dropped on marks we found suspended at 120 to 180 feet over deeper water — and that saved both trips. If you're not marking fish on the sounder, don't just keep trolling blind; work the edge until you find bait balls or hooks on the screen, then go vertical.

Bigeye have shown at night on the chunk, but it's a grind right now, not a guarantee. Anchoring up or slow-drifting over structure inside the canyon proper — the deeper water off the Dip, 1,200 to 1,800 feet — with butterfish and small chunks of bunker soaked on a slow current has produced, but you're looking at one or two fish a trip for boats putting in the full overnight. Circle hooks, 60 to 80-pound fluorocarbon leader, and patience. The moon's been past full and waning gibbous this week, which usually means the chunk bite tightens up on the darkest hours before first light — that's been true again this week.

Wahoo have been incidental but not absent — one nice fish in the 45-pound range came on a high-speed lure, a Braid Marauder run on wire at 12 to 14 knots along the edge where the water temp broke sharpest, right at that green-to-blue seam. If you're rigging specifically for wahoo, that temp break is your target line, not open water. Mahi have been scattered around anything floating — we found a small pod under a lobster ball pile southeast of the Claw, maybe eight to ten fish in the 5 to 12-pound range, gone in fifteen minutes once we'd picked through them. Worth a stop if you see debris on the way out, not worth a dedicated run.

White marlin have been the disappointment of the week. We raised one fish Friday on a dink ballyhoo skipped behind a bird teaser, no hookup, and that's the only marlin action I've heard about out of Shinnecock or Moriches boats this week. Water's not cold, bait's around — I think they're just still scattered south and haven't stacked up yet.

Swordfish daytime drops have quietly been the most consistent thing going. Working the bottom in 1,400 to 1,900 feet off the lower canyon edge, squid strips and a light dropped down to 1,200 to 1,600 feet, we've had two swords in the 100 to 150-pound range in the last two weeks off consistent effort. It's a long, quiet game — six to eight hour drifts — but the daytime bite has been steadier than the night chunk bite for bigeye.

Looking ahead: if that green-water edge keeps sliding southwest at the rate it has, I'd expect it to sit somewhere off the Dip to the Texas Tower line by this weekend — that's where I'd start Saturday if the wind stays out of the southwest under 15 knots, which is what the pattern's shown the last several days. If the edge stalls or pushes back north instead, I'll shift effort up toward the Claw and re-scout the color change from scratch. Either way, don't run past the first hard temperature break you find without working it — that's where the fish are eating this week, not necessarily where they were eating last week.
