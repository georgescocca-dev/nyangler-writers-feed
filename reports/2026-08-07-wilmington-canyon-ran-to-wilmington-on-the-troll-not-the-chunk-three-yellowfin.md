# Ran to Wilmington on the troll, not the chunk — three yellowfin and a mahi for six hours of gas
*The warm-core water is still stacked on the canyon edge, and this week trolling out-produced sitting still with a chunk bucket.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — August 8** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

Made the call Tuesday night before I even looked at the forecast twice: we were trolling Wilmington, not chunking. That's not always the right call this time of year — some weeks the fish want dead bait sitting still over a temp break and you're throwing away gas running a spread — but the water we've had lately told me troll. When the edge is sharp and the fish are moving through instead of parking on bait, you cover ground and make them find you. That's exactly the kind of week this was.

We ran out around 4 a.m., a flat calm ride, which by itself tells you something — this time of August you don't get many mornings like that, and I wasn't going to waste it sitting at the dock. Found the water we wanted along the northeast corner of the canyon, right where the shelf breaks steep into deep color. Blue-to-green transition, current pushing hard enough that the ballyhoo were leaning in the spread the second we cleared the pattern. That's the kind of edge that's worth burning fuel to reach — a real seam, not a smear on a chart that's three days old.

Spread was standard for this water this time of year: naked ballyhoo staggered short and long riggers, a couple of Green Machines and a purple/black Ilander pulled off the long corner, cedar plug way back to pick off anything sulking behind the pattern. First fish came on the short rigger ballyhoo about forty minutes into the troll — yellowfin, mid-40s, textbook strike right at the edge where the color went from washed-out green to that deep cobalt. Two more came through in the next hour, one on the Ilander, one on a naked bally, all within a quarter mile of that same stretch. Then it went quiet for almost three hours. That's the honest part of this report — it wasn't a bite that lasted all day, it was a window, and we happened to be in it. We picked up a mahi, a decent one, maybe 15 pounds, on the long corner ballyhoo trolling through some scattered weed lines south of where we'd been working the tuna. No wahoo for us this trip, though the water's right for them — clean, blue, that kind of edge usually holds one skulking somewhere in the spread if you're running a wire line back deep, which we didn't have out this trip. Lesson for next time.

No white marlin came tight to the boat, but we had two respectable teasers — one that never committed, one that we tried to pitch a naked ballyhoo to and blew the shot. That fish is out there. The kind of edge we were on, with bait balled up and current pushing, is exactly where you expect to raise them mid-morning once the sun gets up and the temp break sharpens visually. If I had another day out there this week I'd rig a pitch bait ready to go the second we see a bill, not scramble for it.

Here's the bigger picture on why this bite is happening at all. The warm-core water sitting over the canyon has held its position for a couple weeks now, and that's kept bait and fish oriented on that same northeast stretch instead of scattering. Bluefin have been showing up unusually close to the Jersey beach this summer, which tells you the whole shelf is running warm and pushing that thermal boundary further inshore than normal — good for beach guys, and it's part of why the offshore pattern out at the canyon has stayed so consistent. The concerning piece, if there is one, is a modest cooling signal that's shown up in water further south the last few days. Nothing that's touched Wilmington yet, but if that cooler water pushes north it could reshuffle where the bait sits and drag the bite off that northeast corner. I'd rather know that now than get surprised by it Saturday.

Fuel-wise, this was a break-even trip at best — three yellowfin and a mahi doesn't fill the boat, but it covers the day and puts fish on the dock, and that's the math that matters running a business out of Cape May. If the same warm water holds through the weekend, I'd make the same call again: troll the edge early, work the corner hard in that first light window, and have a chunk bag ready as a Plan B if the current lays down and the fish stop chasing. What I wouldn't do is sit and chunk cold on a moving current with fish clearly transiting instead of holding — that's a good way to watch bait soak all day.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward new phase on the 12th, which usually sharpens current and can concentrate bait tighter along the break — worth watching for a stronger bite window rather than a longer one. If that southern cooling signal stays put and doesn't creep north, I like our odds of finding yellowfin and mahi in that same stretch again this week, with a real shot at a white marlin now that the teasers are working. If it pushes up into Wilmington, we adjust and go find the new edge — that's the job.
