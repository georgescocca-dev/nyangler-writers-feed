# Sea Bright Rocks at Dawn: Bass on the Bunker, Blues Chopping Behind Them
*The Harvest Moon tides are stacking bait against the jetties from Sea Bright to Manasquan, and the fleet on the rocks is finally getting paid for showing up early.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — September 21** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

First light on the Sea Bright rocks this week has looked the same four mornings running: six, eight guys strung out along the north end, swimmers and darters going out before you can see your own leader, and somebody yelling "bunker" before anybody's had coffee. That's the tell. When the peanut and adult menhaden schools push tight to the beach on a big tide, the guys who know the rocks stop worrying about lures and start worrying about which pocket the bait's going to get funneled into next. This week, the answer has mostly been the north side of the Sea Bright jetties on the last two hours of the incoming, and the Monmouth Beach stretch right behind it once the sun gets some color in the water.

We flagged this back on the 14th — said to expect the bite to intensify over 48 to 72 hours as tidal range built back up, and the Harvest Moon has delivered exactly that. Bigger swings mean more water moving, more current pressure pinning bait against structure, and less guessing about when the window opens. The bass have been shadowing the bunker pods hard, sitting under and behind them rather than off to the side, which tells you they're not just picking off stragglers — they're committed to the school. That's a September pattern that usually holds until the water temperature really drops, and there's no sign of that happening yet up and down this stretch.

The rocks are still the play. Sea Bright's north jetty has been holding bass to the high twenties and one reported fish pushing 30 inches, taken on a white Danny plug worked slow across the bunker pods on the last of the incoming, right as the tide starts to slack and the bait bunches up instead of scattering. Same story at the Monmouth Beach line — Guppy-style swimmers and 2-ounce bucktails with a white curly-tail trailer, cast out past the school and swum back through the edge where the bass are stationed, not into the middle of the boil. That's the part guys keep getting wrong: cast into the meatball and you're competing with forty thousand real bunker. Cast to the seam where the bait thins out and the bass are working the edges, and you're in business.

Blues are riding along behind every one of these pods, and they've been getting bigger and meaner as the week's gone on, which lines up with what we said last Friday about bigger bunker schools pulling in more aggressive fish. Sea Bright and the stretch down toward Asbury Park have both seen blues in the four- to six-pound class mixed into the bass blitzes, happy to eat anything metal — Ava 27s and 37s jigged hard through the wash, or bunker spoons trolled just outside the surf line for the boat guys working close to the beach. Snapper blues are still thick in the back-bay pockets around Shark River and the Belmar basin, which is good news if you've got kids who just want bent rods and don't care what species is on the other end.

Shark River Inlet and Belmar have been more of a jetty-and-current game than a blitz game this week — the bunker schools haven't pushed as hard into that stretch, but the rock structure on both sides of the inlet has still been giving up bass on the last two hours of the ebb, when the current rips hard past the tip and stacks bait against the rocks. Bucktails in white or chartreuse, 3/4 to 1 1/2 ounce depending on how much water's moving, dragged slow along the bottom on that outgoing stage, have been the more consistent producer there than plugs.

Manasquan Inlet remains the most reliable jetty in the zone right now, and that's saying something given how good Sea Bright has looked. The north and south jetties both have been holding fish on the dawn high and again on the last of the outgoing, with the north side favoring an east or northeast wind and the south side better when it's blowing out of the southwest — that's just how the current sets up against the rocks there, and it hasn't changed in twenty-five years. Swimming plugs and darters at first light, then a switch to bucktails once the sun's up and the fish slide down deeper along the rock face. Spring Lake's jetties have been quieter but not dead — a few slot bass reported on peanut bunker imitations worked through the wash there in the last hour of light.

Fluke is winding down, as it does this time of year, but it hasn't shut off completely. Warmer-than-normal water has kept some keepers around off Manasquan and in the deeper pockets off Belmar longer than usual, with bloodworms and cut mullet still producing on bottom rigs for anyone still chasing them. That window is closing fast, though, and the first real cold front that pushes through will likely end it for the season.

Looking ahead, the next 48 to 72 hours should keep building on what's already working — the Harvest Moon tides aren't done yet, and as long as the bunker keep getting pushed hard against the beach, the jetty bite from Sea Bright through Manasquan should stay active on the tide changes. The thing to watch is any cooling signal late in the week. If a front comes through and drops the nearshore water even a few degrees, expect the bass to start moving with more purpose south, and the window at any single jetty to get shorter and sharper rather than longer. If you've only got one morning to give it, make it a dawn tide change at Sea Bright's north jetty or the Manasquan north wall — those two spots have been the most consistent producers all week, and the Harvest Moon tides aren't going to hand out many more free ones like this before the pattern shifts.
