# Porgies thick off Shelter Island, weakfish trail the peanut bunker into the bays
*Mid-August has the Peconic running its own clock — warm, calm, and stacked with porgies, fluke, and weaks working the tide changes.*

**GREENPORT, NY — August 10** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

The Peconic doesn't care what the ocean's doing. While the open beaches are getting hammered by a lumpy Atlantic and the Sound's fighting a steep west-to-east temperature split, our water sits protected behind the forks and just keeps doing its own thing — warm, mostly clean, and settled into that classic mid-August rhythm where porgies, fluke, and weakfish carry the load. I've said it before and I'll say it again: this bay warms first in spring and it holds its heat longest into fall, and right now, first week of August behind us, it's running full summer pattern with no complaints.

We flagged this last week — said a little cooled water behind that front would perk up the daytime porgy and fluke bite, and that's exactly what's happened. The scup around Shelter Island have been thick and cooperative, especially on the Route 114 bridge rips and out around Cedar Island and Coecles Harbor. High-low rigs with size 6 or 8 hooks, half a sandworm or a piece of clam, fished in 15 to 25 feet on the last two hours of the incoming through the top of the tide — that's been the window. Guys anchoring up tight to the bridge pilings on a slack-to-moving tide are filling coolers with porgies from 12 to 15 inches, with a scattering of humpback jumbos mixed in for anyone soaking clam on bottom instead of jigging.

Fluke are doing what they always do in August — stacking up wherever the channels concentrate bait and current. Plum Gut is still the marquee spot but it's not for the faint of heart on a strong tide; if you can time a drift through there on the last of the ebb before it slacks, you're in business. Better bet for most boats has been the Orient Point channel edges and the deeper cuts in Noyack Bay off Jessups Neck, working 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with Gulp Swimming Mullet in chartreuse or pearl, dragged slow along the bottom in 20 to 30 feet. Keeper-size fluke, three to five pounds, have been coming steady on the outgoing, with the occasional doormat pushing six or seven pounds turning up on the deeper drop-offs where the water stays a few degrees cooler than the flats around it.

Weakfish are the story I've been watching closest, and they're tracking exactly the pattern we called — following the peanut bunker right into the protected coves. Hashamomuck on the north shore of the bay has had bunker balled up thick near the boat ramp, and weakfish have been slashing through them at dusk. Small bucktails, quarter-ounce, white or pink, cast into the edges of the bunker schools and worked with a slow twitch, have been drawing strikes from weakfish in the 16 to 20-inch range. Three Mile Harbor and Sag Harbor Cove are showing the same setup — bunker pushed into the shallows, weakfish and the occasional bass working the margins where the current funnels bait against the bank. If you're on the outgoing tide right at last light, that's your window, and it's been a real one, not a rumor.

Striped bass have been the tougher nut in this heat, same story as the rest of the coast — daytime bite is mostly shut down outside of current-swept structure, and the real action is happening dawn, dusk, and after dark around the bunker schools. Orient Point and the Plum Gut rip are still the go-to for that low-light bass bite — live bunker on a fish-finder rig, or bucktails worked deep along the rip edges as the tide starts to move, has been drawing slot fish and the occasional overslot in the dark hours. Nothing exploding, but consistent enough that it's worth setting the alarm early if bass are the target.

Bluefish-wise, the snapper blues have moved into the back bays in good numbers — Three Mile Harbor and the shallows off Sag Harbor have snappers chasing anything shiny, great light-tackle action for kids with small Kastmasters or Gotcha plugs off the dock. The bigger cocktail blues haven't shown in force yet, but with peanut bunker spreading through the bays the way it has, I wouldn't be surprised to see that change inside the next week or two as those blues start tracking the same bait the weakfish are on.

The full moon this week has put some real push into the tides, and that's sharpened up the bite right at the changes — fluke and porgies both have been snapping on when the current kicks in hard after a slack period, then going quiet again once it settles into a steady flow. Worth planning your trip around that first hour of moving water rather than fishing the whole tide blind.

Looking ahead, if the wind stays out of the south-southwest like it's been, I'd expect this pattern to hold — porgies stacked at Shelter Island, fluke working the channel edges, weakfish riding the bunker into the coves at dusk. If we get a stretch of northerly wind that roughs up the open bay, I'd shift effort into the protected coves and harbors where the fishing barely notices the weather. Scallop season's still a ways off, so all the effort right now is squarely on these summer finfish, and there's enough happening to keep a rod bent most evenings if you put in the time on the tide.
