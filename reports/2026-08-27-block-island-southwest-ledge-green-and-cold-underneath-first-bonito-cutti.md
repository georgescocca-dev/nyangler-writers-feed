# Southwest Ledge Green and Cold Underneath, First Bonito Cutting the North Rip
*Bass have gone nocturnal on the Ledge, bluefish are the daytime constant, and Block Island's first bonito of the season are finally showing where we said they would.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — August 27** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

There's a smell to the air out here this week that tells you more than any thermometer will — that heavy, almost sweet bait smell drifting off the pond at first light, the kind that means the peanut bunker have finally packed themselves into the coves in real numbers. The water on top is still summer-warm, sitting in the low 70s off the beaches, but anyone who's dropped a temp gauge down the face of the Southwest Ledge this week knows there's a cold, green layer sitting underneath that warm cap, and that's where the bass have gone. This is the pattern we flagged a few days back — that late August on the Block would settle into a holding pattern rather than a push, and that's exactly what's happening. The real fall run doesn't start until the nights cool off hard, and we're not there yet.

Wind's been mostly out of the southwest at 8 to 15 knots, which keeps the south side a little bounced around in the afternoon but leaves the rips workable on the changes. The moon's building toward full over the next several days, and that's already sharpening up the tide swings around the island — the Southwest Ledge and North Rip both pulling harder water on the last two hours of the ebb than they were a week ago. That's good news. Stronger current means better oxygen exchange over the structure, and it's pulling bait tighter to the edges instead of letting it spread out across the flat, soupy water that's been softening the fluke bite everywhere from here to the Cape.

Striped bass are there, but this is a structure-and-shadow fishery right now, not a run-and-gun one. The better boats are working the Southwest Ledge in 35 to 55 feet on the top of the tide and through the first hour of the drop, dropping live eels or fresh-cut bunker chunks down into the rock piles rather than trolling over them. Daytime action has been slow and scattered — the fish are sitting deep against the cold layer and not moving much until the light drops. The real program has shifted to the night bite: eels drifted slow along the Ledge's east face after dark, or bunker spider-rigged off the drift right at the change of tide, have been the two most consistent ways to connect with better fish, some pushing into the 20s and low 30s. The North Rip is still producing on the harder tide stages — big swirling current edges where a school bunker on a fish-finder rig, fished tight to the boil line rather than out in open water, has taken a few genuinely heavy fish this week, including at least one report of something well into the 40-pound class worked out of that white water on a slow outgoing tide. That's the kind of fish that reminds you why this island pulls stripers from three states away every summer — the cold, clean, well-oxygenated water coming up over that ledge is simply a different animal than what most of the coast is dealing with right now.

Bluefish have been the dependable piece of the puzzle, same as everywhere else in the region this year. Cocktail blues in the 3- to 6-pound range are mixing right in with the bass on the Ledge and off Southeast Light, and they've been the saving grace on days when the stripers wanted nothing to do with anything moving fast. Bucktails and diamond jigs worked with a jigging stroke through the deeper water have both bait and blues stacked up, and if the bass shut down, the blues usually haven't.

And here's the news worth circling — bonito have shown up. Not thick yet, but real, with the first solid reports coming off the rips on the north and south sides of the island and off Point Judith as well, right where we said they'd turn up as the water settled into that low-70s sweet spot. Small epoxy jigs and half-ounce Deceivers worked fast on top, right in the white water where the tide is ripping bait against structure, have accounted for the first fish. This is a moving-tide, moving-boat fishery — find the nervous water, make the cast into the seam, strip it fast, and don't stop to admire it. Expect this to build over the next week or two as it typically does once it starts, spreading toward Newport and the eastern approaches to Narragansett Bay as bait continues to tighten along the beaches.

Fluke have been the soft spot of the week, same story as the rest of southern New England — warm, thin water has scattered them across the flats rather than holding them tight, and the better catches have come from deeper structure and channel edges rather than the open bottom. If fluke is the target, work the deeper edges around the island rather than the shallow sand.

Looking ahead, expect the pattern to hold through the next several days — southwest breeze, warm cap on top, cold water underneath, and a building tide that should keep sharpening the bite windows at the Ledge and the Rip as the moon fills. If there's only one window to fish this weekend, it's the last two hours of the ebb into dark at the Southwest Ledge, eel or bunker chunk down deep, with a rod rigged light for bonito on the side in case the rip lights up on top. The real turn toward fall bass fishing is still a few weeks off — this is the in-between stretch, and it rewards patience and the right tide more than raw effort.
