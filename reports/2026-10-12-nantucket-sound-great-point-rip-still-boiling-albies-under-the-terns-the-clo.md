# Great Point rip still boiling: albies under the terns, the clock's the real story
*Bonito and false albacore are still working the Sound's rips, but cooling water is closing the door fast — here's what's biting while it lasts.*

And the numbers back up what the birds are showing. Water across the broader region keeps sliding — Kings Point reading 70.9°F, New London at 69.4°F, Montauk down to 69.1°F — a steady west-to-east, open-water-to-inshore cooling trend that's been squeezing warm-water species into smaller and smaller pockets for two weeks straight. Nantucket Sound and the Vineyard are sitting right at the edge of that pocket right now, which is exactly why the albie and bonito bite here is still alive while Montauk and Block Island have gone largely quiet on both species. We flagged this trend last week — said the albie/bonito window would keep closing fast while Nantucket Sound held on as one of the last real pockets — and that's precisely what the Shoals are showing now. It's not a guess anymore, it's the pattern playing out in real time.

The fishing itself has been genuinely good where the rips are working, but it's not an all-day proposition. Albies are keying on tight schools of bay anchovies and rain bait getting pinned against the current seams at Great Point and off Monomoy, and the bite has been concentrated in short, explosive windows — usually the last two hours of the ebb when bait gets funneled hard against structure. Small profile is winning: half-ounce to one-ounce epoxy jigs, Deadly Dicks in chrome/blue, and 2-inch Albie Snax on light leader have all been producing when the fish are up, but the second the surface goes quiet, the lure choice stops mattering. Bonito are mixed into the same schools but thinner in numbers than peak summer — small Clousers and slim profile metal in white/olive have taken a few when anglers have gotten in front of a moving school rather than chasing the splash after it's already broken.

Striped bass are the more dependable story right now, and that tracks with the bigger coastal picture — this is peak fall migration, with bass pushing hard along every funnel point from Montauk through the Canal and down into the bays. Nantucket Shoals and the rips off Great Point and Sankaty are holding fish tight to the current edges, and reports describe decent numbers of schoolie-to-mid-teens bass mixed with the albie action, taking swim shads and 1 to 1.5-ounce bucktails worked along the bottom edge of the rip rather than up in the surface chaos. Bluefish are still mixed through both the striper and albie activity wherever bait concentrates, chopping up topwater plugs and providing the kind of aggressive, line-cutting fun that reminds everyone to drop the wire leader back on before the next session.

Fluke are winding down across most of the region as water drops out of their comfort range, but the deeper structure around the Sound and Horseshoe Shoal has reportedly stayed one of the more consistent producers left on the board — Gulp and bucktail combos worked slow along the bottom on the last of the drift have still turned a few keepers, though effort has clearly shifted toward the migratory species as the calendar pushes into mid-October.

Looking ahead, expect the current pattern to hold for another day or two before the next cooling push starts narrowing things further. The blitz-style activity at the classic funnel points — the Race, Block Island's North Rip, the Canal — should stay strong through the short term as that migration keeps rolling, and the same logic applies here: the rips at Great Point and Monomoy should keep producing short, sharp windows of albie and bonito activity through this week, but that window is measured in days now, not weeks. If there's one day to make, early morning on the dropping tide with birds working is the bet — once the next front moves cooler water through, the smart money says the albies and bonito finish their run east toward the Vineyard before vacating the Sound entirely, leaving striped bass and bluefish to carry the rips into November.
