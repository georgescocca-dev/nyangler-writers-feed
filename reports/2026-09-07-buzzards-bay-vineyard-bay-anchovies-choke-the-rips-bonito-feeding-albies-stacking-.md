# Bay Anchovies Choke the Rips: Bonito Feeding, Albies Stacking Behind Them
*Micro bait is running thick through Robinson's and Quick's Hole, and the first real albie push of the fall is right on its heels.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — September 7** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

The bait tells the story before anything else does, and right now the bay anchovies are so thick in Robinson's Hole and Quick's Hole that the surface looks nervous even without a fish touching it. Small, silvery, moving in tight sheets — that's the signature this time of year, and it's exactly what's been pulling bonito hard into Buzzards Bay all week. The push has been building out of Rhode Island and is now sitting right on our doorstep, with fish showing consistently on the west side of the Elizabeth Islands wherever that current squeezes bait against structure.

The full moon that came through the night of the 6th and 7th did what we said it would — it tightened those bait schools up. We flagged this last week, that the building moon would concentrate bunker and forage further and set up the first real fall feeding activity, and that's exactly what's playing out now, just a few days ahead of the new moon on the 10th. Bigger tide swings mean more water moving through the holes, and more water moving through the holes means bait gets squeezed into predictable seams. That's when bonito and albies stop being ghosts and start being catchable.

Right now it's more bonito than albies, but that ratio is shifting fast. Reports out of the Vineyard side — Menemsha rip, the drop-off east of the jetty, and the stretch running out toward Noman's — have both species mixed in on the same pushes of bait, sometimes in the same five minutes. The albies that have shown are keying tight on that anchovy-sized profile, which means standard-size Deadly Dicks and half-ounce Point Judes are getting looked at and refused while smaller epoxy jigs — the quarter-ounce and three-eighths stuff in olive/white or bone — are getting bit. If you're only carrying the usual 40-gram tins, add something smaller to the box before you leave the dock. Same goes for albie snacks — downsize to the smaller profile ones rather than the standard 3-inch bodies.

Bonito are less picky but still respond best to speed. A fast, erratic retrieve on those same small epoxy jigs, cast into the whitewater where the rip breaks over the ledge at Robinson's, has been the most consistent producer. Best window has been the last two hours of the ebb into the start of the flood, when the current compresses through the hole and the bait has nowhere to go but bunch up. Early morning, before the wind builds, is still the calmest water and the easiest sight-fishing — you can see the nervous water and the occasional greyhounding fish well before you're in casting range.

Striped bass are the other piece moving into position. Bait concentrating around the Elizabeth Islands has stripers starting to organize rather than just pick at scraps, and that's the pattern we expect to sharpen through the coming week as the tide builds toward the new moon. Cuttyhunk's western side and the rips off Nashawena have both had bass mixed in with bluefish, working bunker pods tight to the rocks on the last of the outgoing. Bucktails in the 1- to 1.5-ounce range, white or chartreuse, worked slow along bottom through the deeper cuts, have been the better producer over swimming plugs so far — the fish seem to want something crawling rather than something skipping on top, at least for now. That could flip fast once the blitz activity properly turns on.

Bluefish have been the dependable player all week, chopping through bunker schools throughout the bay and giving anglers something to lean on when the bonito and albies go quiet mid-day. Poppers and metal-lip swimmers worked over visible bluefish surface activity near Woods Hole and the Oak Bluffs side have kept rods bent even when the tuna-family fish were sulking.

Fluke are winding down but not done. There's still a respectable bite on the flats inside Buzzards Bay and around the Vineyard sound side on Gulp/bucktail combinations and live killifish, particularly on the last of the incoming tide when water temperatures are at their highest for the day. Don't expect the numbers of July, but a mixed bag with a few keepers is realistic if fluke is part of the plan.

Looking at the next few days, the tide is the whole story. As we build toward the new moon on the 10th, expect current speed through Robinson's, Quick's, and the Menemsha rip to increase, which should tighten bait even further and pull albie numbers up to match the bonito that are already here. If that bait stays concentrated the way it's been, the window from first light through the early ebb should be the most productive stretch, with a second look worth taking on the tail of the outgoing before the tide goes dead. Watch the wind — anything out of the southwest under 10 knots keeps these rips fishable and the water clean enough to sight-cast; anything harder and it turns to chop that makes spotting breaking fish tough. Best case, the new moon tide brings the first real blitz-style activity to the islands. Worst case, it stays a mixed, technical bite where downsizing lures is the difference between a bent rod and a long cast into empty water.
