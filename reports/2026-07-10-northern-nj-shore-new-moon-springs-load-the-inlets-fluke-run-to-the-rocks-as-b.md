# New moon springs load the inlets — fluke run to the rocks as bass play it coy
*Bigger tides and cooler upwelled water are pushing fluke out of the bays and into Manasquan and Shark River, while the bass bite stays a dawn-only proposition.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — July 10** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Twenty-five years of standing on these rocks and I still get excited when the tide chart tells me something's coming. This week it's telling me the moon's going dark — new moon lands right in this window — and that means spring tides building through the weekend, peaking around the 11th to 13th. Bigger swings, stronger current, more water moving through Manasquan and Shark River inlets on both ends of the cycle. That's the kind of setup that either turns a jetty on or blows it out entirely, depending on how much push you get and whether the wind fights the tide or rides with it.

Last week the story was heat. The back bays — Shark River especially, up past the Route 35 bridge — pushed into the high 70s, and that's too warm for fluke to sit comfortable. They did what they always do when the bay gets soupy: they ran for the inlets and the ocean front, where a little NE breeze has been upwelling cooler water along the beach, holding in the low 70s. That's a clean, predictable pattern, and it's the one I'd build a week around. The bass, on the other hand, have been more stubborn — some fish around at first light, but nothing you'd call consistent, and I'm not going to stand here and tell you otherwise.

Start with the fluke, because that's where the sure money is right now. Manasquan Inlet on the outgoing, from the Route 71 bridge down through the channel to the jetty mouth, has been holding fish in 15 to 25 feet. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom, tipped with a strip of squid if you want insurance — that combo has been taking keepers in the 19 to 22-inch range, with a few better fish mixed in on the last hour of the drop. Shark River Inlet is doing the same thing on a smaller scale — work the deeper hole off the Belmar side, tight to the rocks, same bucktail-and-Gulp approach, chartreuse if the water's got any color to it from the runoff. The key is timing: you want the last two hours of outgoing, when the bait gets pinned against the structure and the fluke stack up to ambush it. Don't waste your morning on the flood — that's when they scatter back toward the bay edges.

Stripers are the harder conversation. The jetty rocks from Sea Bright down through Belmar have given up bass at dawn, but it's a short window and you need to be standing there when it opens, not showing up after the sun's already up over the water tower. Sea Bright rocks, the stretch north of the Rumson-Sea Bright bridge, has thrown a few schoolies to guys throwing white Super Strike Zig-Zag needlefish and Gibbs Danny plugs on the first light of the outgoing, right as the current starts pulling bait past the tip of the jetty. Manasquan Inlet's north jetty has done the same on occasion — bunker have been thin but not absent, and when a pod pushes through the inlet mouth on that outgoing new-moon surge, the bass show up underneath them for ten, fifteen minutes and then it's over. I had one morning last week where three fish came in twenty minutes and then nothing for two hours. That's the read right now — short, sharp windows, not a sustained bite. If you're going to fish it, fish the tide change hard and don't linger past it.

Bluefish have been the wildcard filling in the gaps — cocktail blues, mostly, blitzing bunker pods off Asbury Park and Spring Lake on calm mornings, chewing through metal lips and poppers fast enough that you'll lose a few to the teeth if you're not running wire or heavy mono leader. Not glamorous, but it's action, and it keeps the rod bent while you wait to see if the stripers show.

On the albie front — I'm watching, not promising. The pelagic window that's had bonito and Spanish mackerel showing up on Long Island's south side is closing out over the next few days, and the water temperature signatures that usually precede our first false albacore push have been building. Nothing confirmed yet at Manasquan or Shark River, but the outer rocks at both inlets are worth a look with small Deadly Dick or Albie Snax on light tackle if you're already out there for the fluke. I wouldn't build a trip around it yet — it's a maybe, not a bet.

Looking at the week ahead: the new moon spring tide peak lines up Saturday into Monday, which means the strongest current push of the month at both inlets. If the wind stays out of the northeast and light, that's when I'd put my time in — dawn outgoing at Sea Bright and Manasquan for the bass, with the fluke bucktail as backup once the sun's up and the bite window closes. If the wind swings onshore and stiffens, the surf's going to get sloppy and the jetty game turns miserable fast — that's when I'd shift entirely to the inlet channels and fish the fluke on the last of the outgoing, working the deeper cuts where the current stays manageable. Either way, don't sleep past first light this weekend. The tide's doing the work for you — you just have to be standing on the rock when it does.
