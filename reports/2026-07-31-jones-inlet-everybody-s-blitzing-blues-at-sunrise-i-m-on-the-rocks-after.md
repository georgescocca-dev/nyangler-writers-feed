# Everybody's Blitzing Blues at Sunrise — I'm on the Rocks After Dark for Bass
*Warm ocean water has the bass shut down all day, but the inlet rocks at night are holding up their end of the deal.*

**JONES BEACH, NY — July 31** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Half the guys I talk to at the bait shop this week are up at 5 a.m. chasing the blitz on the beach, and I get why — peanut bunker and mullet getting hammered right off the sand is a hell of a sight. But I'm not fighting that crowd. I'm out at the jetty rocks off Short Beach two hours after sundown with a bunker chunk soaking on the bottom, and that's where the real fish have been.

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear in July: the ocean's too warm for stripers to play the daytime game right now. We're sitting up around 71, 72 degrees off the beachfront, and that's above the comfort zone for a big bass. So they're doing what they always do when the water gets like this — they go nocturnal, they slide onto the harder structure, and they wait for the tide change to push bait through the inlet before they commit to feeding. I've said it before and I'll say it again — this pattern isn't going anywhere as long as this weather stays put, and this week proved it out again. Same story, three nights running.

The program at the rocks has been simple. I fish the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide, right at the mouth of Jones Inlet where the current sweeps bait past the jetty tip. Fresh bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig, 3 ounces of weight to hold bottom against that current, cast up-current and let it swing down into the pocket behind the rocks. Landed a 34-incher Tuesday night and lost something bigger that just leaned on the drag and took me into the rocks — that's the kind of fish that's around right now, you just have to fish the dark hours to find it. Eels work too if you can get your hands on live ones, snap-cast them out and let them swim naturally on a slow retrieve past the tip.

During the day, the bass program basically shuts off at the inlet mouth, but that's fine, because bluefish don't care one bit about warm water — matter of fact they love it. We flagged this pattern last week and it's still running exactly the same: bluefish are the beneficiary of this whole warm-water setup, and the blitz action along the beachfront has kept spreading, not slowing down. Jones Beach surf, Field 6 through Field 10, incoming tide has had choppers in the 3 to 6 pound range crashing bait right in the wash. Metal — Deadly Dicks, Hopkins jigs — thrown into the melee has been about as close to a sure thing as this sport gets right now. If you want a slower, more controlled bite, work the rip off the inlet itself on the same incoming water with bucktails or poppers; the current edge there stacks blues tight enough that you can pick through them for the bigger models instead of just grabbing whatever hits first.

Fluke has been the steady, unspectacular money-maker all month, and this week didn't change that. Sloop Channel is still doing it — drift the drop-offs on the edges where the channel falls off from 8 feet down into 18, 20 feet, dragging a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, tipped with a squid strip if the current's really moving. Keepers mixed with plenty of shorts, nothing to write home about but enough to keep a rod bent on most drifts. The bridge structure at Wantagh has been holding some too, right along the pilings on the last of the outgoing when the current slackens enough to fish a lighter jig head.

Now the fish I actually get excited to talk about — weakfish are legitimately showing back up in the bay, and I don't say that lightly because for years I wouldn't even bother mentioning them. Guys working South Oyster Bay on soft plastics, 4-inch pearl or bunker-colored paddle tails on light jig heads, have been picking up weakfish in that 18 to 22-inch class working the deeper holes off the marsh edges on the last two hours of the outgoing. It's not a blitz, it's a handful of fish here and there, but five years ago you wouldn't catch a single one, so I'll take it as a sign this fishery's finally coming back to life in this system.

Blackfish and porgies are basically an afterthought this time of year — water's too warm and the tog won't turn on again until the fall push, though you can still scratch out porgies around the Freeport bridge pilings on sandworms if you're bored on a slow tide.

Looking ahead, I don't expect much to change through the weekend. Tides are easing off the full moon toward neap, which should actually help the night bass bite settle into a more predictable rhythm around the tide changes instead of the harder push we've had. If a thunderstorm rolls through midweek like they usually do in early August, it might stir things up for a day, but the underlying pattern — blues up top in daylight, bass down deep after dark — should hold right through the build toward the mid-August new moon. That's when I expect the bait to really stack up tight in the inlet again, and that's when this thing gets interesting.
