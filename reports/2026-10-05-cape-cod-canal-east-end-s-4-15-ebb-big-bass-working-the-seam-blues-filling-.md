I can't tell you what it felt like to have a rod doubled over on the rip, but I can tell you why the current is doing what it's doing and where the fish are using it.

The short version: the Canal is in its October groove. Herring stragglers and the last of the summer's bunker are getting squeezed through the cut on every tide change, and striped bass have figured out that the current seams on both ends are the cheapest way to eat without working for it. We flagged this building two weeks ago — said the bite would stay patchy but improve as more bass funneled through on their way south — and that's exactly the shape it's taking now. It's not a nonstop blitz from bridge to bridge, but the windows are getting longer and the fish showing up in them are bigger.

The east-to-west tide stage has been the moneymaker, and that's worth understanding rather than just repeating. When the tide runs east to west, bait gets pushed from Cape Cod Bay into the Canal and compressed against the current seams on the Sandwich side before it funnels out toward Buzzards Bay. Stripers stack up tight to those seams — not necessarily at the absolute top of the tide, but in that hour-plus window as the flow builds toward max. That's when a swimming presentation outfishes a cast-and-retrieve. Canal regulars working the east end herring run have had good luck swimming white or bone Super Spooks and 6-inch Savage Gear swim shads right along the current break where the fast water meets the slower eddy, letting the push do the work instead of ripping the retrieve.

On the west end, near Hog Island and down toward Mashnee, the pattern flips with the tide — west-to-east flow pulls bait out of Buzzards Bay and past Wings Neck and Phinneys Harbor before it funnels into the ditch. That stretch has been more mixed. Some days it's stacked with schoolies and the occasional mid-30s fish on bucktails bounced along bottom; other days it's dead water for an hour and then five minutes of chaos when a bunker pod gets pinned against the rocks. If you're fishing that end, the smart move is parking on the seam before the push builds rather than chasing surface activity once it starts, because by the time you see birds working it's often already thinning out.

Bluefish have been the Canal's reliable undercard this fall, same as they are everywhere from New Jersey north right now. They're not as size-structured as the bass — mostly schoolies in the 2 to 4 pound range — but they'll hit when the stripers go quiet, and they're not shy about it. Diamond jigs in the 2-to-3-ounce range, worked with a fast vertical retrieve through the deeper parts of the channel near the railroad bridge, have been producing when the current is still building but hasn't hit full speed. Once the flow really gets cranking, most anglers switch to swimming plugs just to keep gear from getting swept into the rocks.

Albies and bonito have made a few late cameos around the east end mouth, but that's a bonus bite at this point, not something to plan a session around. Water temps on this stretch of coast are sliding into the low-to-mid 60s, and that's getting close to the edge of what keeps those speedsters interested. If you're chasing them, treat it as a long shot on an otherwise bass-focused trip, not the main target.

The next few days should keep building rather than fading. The full moon on October 6th is stacking spring tides on top of an already strong seasonal current, which means faster water, tighter bait concentration, and — if the pattern holds — some of the better blitz windows of the fall right around the bridges. A cooling trend behind this is expected to push water temps down another degree or so, which historically tightens up the bait even more against the current seams rather than scattering it. If you only have one window this week, I'd take the building ebb on the east end in the late afternoon, with a swimming plug worked slow along the seam, and treat the west end as the backup if that session goes quiet. Keep an eye on the wind too — anything with real push out of the southwest will mess with the surface presentation on the east end seam faster than the tide will.

Bottom line for anglers planning around this: the Canal isn't in full blitz mode yet, but the current is doing its part, the bait is getting squeezed harder by the day, and the bass are using the structure exactly the way they're supposed to this time of year. That's not hype — that's just what four knots of moving water and a full moon tend to produce.
