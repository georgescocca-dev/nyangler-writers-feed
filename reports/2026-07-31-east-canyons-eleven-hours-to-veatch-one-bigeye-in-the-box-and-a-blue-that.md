# Eleven Hours to Veatch, One Bigeye in the Box, and a Blue That Wouldn't Come Tight
*Warm water finally pushed up onto the shelf break this week — the run got shorter, the bite got real, but you still have to earn it.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 31** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the Montauk inlet at 4:15 Tuesday morning, dark enough that the outer buoy light was the only thing worth looking at, and didn't see the east wall of Veatch until almost nine. That's the math out here — you don't fish these canyons, you commute to them. Call it 108 nautical miles from the inlet to the drop at 39°56'N, 69°42'W, and at a cruise of 20 knots that's five and a half hours one way, burning somewhere around 16 gallons an hour on the mains. Add the troll time, add the run home, and you've spent eleven hours of engine time to buy four hours of actual fishing. I don't complain about it. I just make sure the fuel log adds up before I leave the dock, because there's no gas station out past the 100-fathom curve.

What we found when we got there made the number worth it, mostly. The warm filament that's been pushing north off the Stream all month has finally set up tight against the canyon wall — we marked a clean break from 71 up into the mid-70s right along the edge, which is about two weeks early for water that warm to be sitting this far north in late July. We flagged this pattern building last week, and here it is holding: yellowfin stacked on that break, working bait right up against the thermocline in 900 to 1,100 feet of water. First fish came on a naked ballyhoo off the shotgun position around 8:40, and from there it was a solid two hours of yellowfin in the 40 to 60 pound class, six for eleven on a spread of Green Machines, a couple of Ilander/ballyhoo combos in dark colors, and one Joe Shute rigged bare that outfished everything else for reasons I still can't explain.

The bigeye came later, and it came hard. We'd shut the engines down around noon to drift and jig a temp break where the sounder showed bait stacked from 200 down to 400 feet, and on the second drop with a butterfish-tipped Nomad jig, something ate it at 280 feet and didn't come up easy. Twenty-five minutes on 50-pound braid, and we got a 165-pound bigeye to color before he made one more run we almost didn't stop. That's the fish that makes the fuel bill make sense.

We had a white marlin come up on the long rigger teaser around 1:30, ate a ballyhoo, and threw the hook on the second jump. That's how it's gone for marlin all month out here — plenty of shots, not a lot of fish actually stuck. If you're running for billfish specifically right now, I'd fish smaller circle hooks and slow your drop-back, because these fish are eating and not committing the way they will once that water settles in through August.

Hydrographer fished similarly when a buddy of mine ran out of Point Judith Wednesday — he's closer to that piece than we are from Montauk, maybe 95 nautical miles from the breakwater to 39°57'N, 69°00'W, and he had a clean yellowfin bite early with a couple of mahi mixed in around a weed line he found drifting south of the canyon. Mahi have been the bonus fish all month — anytime you cross a good weed line or a piece of floating junk out there, stop and pitch a bucktail or a small popper, because there's almost always a school sitting under it, even if it's just schoolies in the 5 to 10 pound range.

Oceanographer is the one I haven't gotten a good read on this week — it's the longest run from our ports, and with the wind holding out of the southwest and light, most of the RI and Block Island boats have been shading toward Veatch and Hydrographer where the fuel math is more forgiving. I'd expect Oceanographer to be fishing similarly given the same warm push, but I can't vouch for it firsthand yet, and I'm not going to sell you water I haven't run to.

Bluefin have been scattered on the inshore edges closer to the Sound side and up toward Stellwagen tied to whale and bait-ball activity, but that's not really our program out here — the canyon bluefin bite has been quieter than the yellowfin, mostly incidental fish mixed in with the tuna schools rather than a targeted push. Wahoo showed up as a couple of incidental fish on the high-speed lines rigged with Ilanders in blue/white, nothing to build a trip around yet, but worth having a rod rigged for if you're already trolling that spread.

Looking ahead, the warm filament shows no sign of sliding back south, and if it holds through the week I'd expect the yellowfin bite to stay steady on both Veatch and Hydrographer, with mahi as a reliable bonus around any structure you cross offshore. The marlin bite should firm up once this water sits in place a little longer — right now the fish are there but green, eating and not always sticking. If I had one day to burn fuel this week, I'd run for that east wall at Veatch on the first light of a calm morning and fish the temp break hard before the wind builds in the afternoon, because that's where the bait's holding and that's where the tuna are working it.
