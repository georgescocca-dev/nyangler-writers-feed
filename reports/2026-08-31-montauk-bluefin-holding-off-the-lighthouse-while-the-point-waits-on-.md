# Bluefin Holding Off the Lighthouse While the Point Waits on Its Bass
*Blues are running hard on bunker, fluke has thinned to the channel edges, and the real fall trigger is still a few tides away.*

**MONTAUK, NY — August 31** — _by Rich Martin, Zone Writer_

Late August at the Point is a waiting game, and anyone who's fished here more than a few seasons knows exactly what that feels like. The water off the Lighthouse is sitting around 69 degrees, still warm enough to hold summer patterns but cool enough that you can feel the calendar turning underneath it. The moon is building toward full, which means more water moving through the rips every tide, and that's the single biggest thing to watch this week — because more current means more bait getting pushed and pinned, and that's the trigger this whole system has been waiting on.

We flagged the bluefin holding off the Point last week, and the boats are still finding them close enough that it doesn't take a long run to get bit. That school hasn't broken up yet, and as long as the bait stays put out there, there's no reason to think it will. Worth a call to the local charter docks before you commit a day offshore, but right now Montauk is one of the few places on the whole Island where you can leave the harbor, catch a bluefin, and be back in time for the evening bass tide.

Striped bass are still in that in-between stage — present, catchable, but not yet doing what October Montauk does. Surfcasters working the boulder field on the ocean side of the Point are picking up fish on the building tide, mostly on bucktails and darters worked slow through the wash, with the better fish coming right at the edge of light — first gray of dawn and the last of dusk. North Bar has been giving up a mix of schoolies and a few respectable fish in the 20s on the incoming, especially when there's a push of sand eels or peanut bunker getting corralled against the bar. Nobody's talking about blitz numbers yet. The bunker schools further west haven't concentrated enough to pull big bass into a feeding frenzy at the Point, but that's the pattern building, not one that's arrived — give it the better part of the next two tide cycles as the moon fills and the water keeps moving.

Bluefish are the fish putting bends in rods right now, and that part of the season is not soft at all. Turtle Cove and the stretch of south side beach running toward the Point have had steady morning blitzes on bunker pods, blues in the 8-to-12-pound class crashing bait right in the wash, sometimes close enough to hit with a topwater popper on the first cast off the beach. Metal — Deadly Dicks, Hopkins — worked fast through the froth is still the simplest way to connect, and there's no shortage of fish willing to eat it. This bite tracks bait, plain and simple, and as long as bunker keep working the beach, the blues will keep showing up behind them.

Fluke have thinned out, which is normal for this point in the season and not a reason to panic. The Frisbee grounds and the drift off Shagwong Reef are still producing, but the better fish have slid into deeper water and channel edges chasing cooler, better-oxygenated flow — 25 to 40 feet is where the boats are finding keepers now instead of the shallower flats that were productive in July. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the last of the ebb, are still the go-to combo, but it's a fair bite rather than a fast one. Expect this window to hold through most of September before those fish push offshore for good.

Porgies remain one of the more dependable meals of the week for anyone fishing the harbor or the inshore reef structure — sandworms and squid strips on the bottom in 20 to 30 feet, no complicated rig required, just patience and a good chum slick. Weakfish are around in scattered numbers but nothing worth planning a trip around specifically; if you're working the same grass edges as the porgies, don't be surprised if one shows up in the mix. Blackfish season hasn't opened yet, so leave the tog gear at home for now.

The next few days should hold mostly steady — winds look manageable, the tide push will keep building with the moon, and that combination usually starts concentrating bait tighter against structure like North Bar and the boulder field. If there's one thing to watch closely, it's the eastern approach: bonito have been working westward out of Rhode Island waters, and Montauk is typically next in line once that push reaches Block Island Sound. It hasn't shown here yet, but the conditions setting up — clean water, building current, squid and bait concentrating in the rips — are exactly what turns it on fast once it starts. Keep a bag of small metal or a bonito-weight bucktail rigged and ready, because when that bite arrives at the Point, it doesn't give much warning.

For anyone with one day to spend this week, the smart play is still bluefish in the morning off the south side beaches on the building tide, then a swing out to the boulder field at last light for bass. The bigger fall push isn't here yet — but every tide from here to the full moon is one step closer, and Montauk in early September is about as good a place as there is to be standing when it finally breaks loose.
