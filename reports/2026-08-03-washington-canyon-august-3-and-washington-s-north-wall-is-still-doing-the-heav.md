# August 3 and Washington's North Wall Is Still Doing the Heavy Lifting
*Two months into the season the chunk bite is fading with the tides, but the troll is picking up the slack right on schedule.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON, NY — August 3** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

By the first week of August I've usually got a pretty good read on how the season's going to shake out, and this year Washington did exactly what it always does — showed up early and settled into a rhythm before the other canyons even got their spreads wet. We were seeing yellowfin on the temperature breaks back in June when boats running out of the other inlets were still soaking bait for bluefin off the beach. That head start matters. It means by early August, when everybody else is just figuring out where the fish are, we've already got three patterns dialed in and we're watching for the fourth.

Right now that fourth pattern is the shift from chunk to troll, and it's happening on schedule. We flagged this last week — said the overnight chunk bite would peak Wednesday through Friday inside the 100-fathom curve on the north wall, and that's exactly how it played out. Guys anchored up there Thursday night into Friday morning had some of the better yellowfin counts of the month, mixed with a few bigeye showing up on the bite windows around 2 and 4 a.m. The moon was still working for us then. Now we're sliding into the neap — tide range narrowing through the 5th and 6th — and that current isn't pushing bait against the wall with the same authority. When the drift softens, the tuna spread out, and chunking loses its edge. That's when the troll starts carrying the weight, and I'd bet on that trend holding through the weekend.

The bite itself has been honest work, not a gimme. We're not lighting up six-packs of yellowfin in an afternoon — this is a fifty-fifty proposition most days, four or five fish in the boat if you found the right piece of structure and the color change lined up. The stable high pressure that's been sitting over the whole mid-Atlantic has kept run conditions about as good as they get — flat enough to troll a nine-line spread comfortably, seas running two to three feet with a long period out at the edge of the shelf, nothing that's going to beat up a canyon run. But calm water and light current also mean the bait isn't getting pinned the way it does when there's some push behind it, so you're covering ground to find pockets instead of parking on one spot and letting it come to you.

Mahi have been the bright spot for the trollers working the color edges and any floating structure — a few nice pods in the 8 to 15 pound class coming off small green machines and skirted ballyhoo pulled just off the paddocks of grass lines. If you see even a single mat of sargassum out there this week, slow down and work it — those fish have been holding tight to any bit of cover they can find. White marlin sightings have been spreading week over week just like we expected, mostly on naked ballyhoo and small blue/white Islanders run flat lines, and a couple boats have gotten multiple bites in a day without necessarily converting all of them — typical August white marlin behavior, they'll play with a bait longer than they commit to it.

Wahoo have been a bonus fish more than a target — a few high-speed trollers pulling cedar plugs and small Ilanders at 12-15 knots along the drop-off have picked off one or two on days when the water clarity out past the 100-fathom line has gone that deep cobalt blue that tells you you're sitting in real Gulf Stream water, not just warm coastal runoff. That blue water pushing up against the canyon edge is the whole reason Washington runs ahead of schedule every year — we're closer to that current than Hudson or the canyons north of us, and it shows in how early the tropical species show up and how long they stick around into the fall.

Bluefin have been mixed in but not the headline story here the way they've been up north on Stellwagen and Jeffrey's Ledge, where the cooler Gulf of Maine water is holding bigger concentrations. Down here they're incidental — a fish or two crashing the chunk slick at first light, usually smaller schoolies mixed with the yellowfin, nothing you'd plan a whole trip around.

Looking at the week ahead, I'd play the neap tide the way I always do out here — lean harder into the troll through midweek while current is soft, working the eddy edge and any temperature break you can find on the meter, then swing back toward the chunk as the tide starts building again into the weekend. If the eddy holds its position off the north wall like it has all month, that's still the high-percentage water. If the post-blow satellite shots this week show it sliding east — and there's some chatter that it might drift with this stretch of settled weather — I'd shift effort toward the canyon's eastern flank and give up a little bit of the wall advantage for fresher water. Either way, the fish are here and the conditions are cooperative. You still have to find the color change and put a bait in the right piece of water, but that's always been true out here, early season or late.
