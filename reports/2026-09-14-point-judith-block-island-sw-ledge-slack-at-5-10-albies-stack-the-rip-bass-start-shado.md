# SW Ledge Slack at 5:10: Albies Stack the Rip, Bass Start Shadowing Bunker
*The albie run we flagged last week has pushed hard into the Block, and the first real bunker-run stripers are showing off the point right behind it.*

**GALILEE, RI — September 14** — _by Ava Thomas, Zone Writer_

SW Ledge's last two hours of ebb this week have been running out around 5:10 in the afternoon, and that window has been the difference between a good drift and a wasted one. When the current pulls hard off that rockpile in 35 to 45 feet, it stacks bait against the structure and the rip lines up clean off the southwest corner — that's when the bonito and false albacore have been showing consistently, not just flashing on top for a few minutes and vanishing. Slack water there has been soft and short, maybe twenty minutes on either side, so boats that time the last hour of the ebb into the first hour of the new flood are getting two bites out of one tide instead of chasing a single window.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the albie run building off Rhode Island would keep extending west toward Montauk and eastern Long Island, and that's exactly what's playing out. The bonito and albies that the Block in early September have held their numbers and are now spreading tighter against structure as the water starts easing off summer highs. Off Sandy Point and down toward Southwest Ledge, small metals — half-ounce Deadly Dicks and green machine tins — are getting hit hard on a fast, erratic retrieve when the bait balls tighten up on the last of the ebb. Epoxy jigs in olive/white have worked when the fish get finicky and start refusing straight metal, particularly in the early morning glass-off before the wind fills in. The pattern isn't universal yet — some drifts are stone dead for forty minutes and then the whole rip erupts for ten — but when it's on, it's a real bite, not just a flash in the pan.

Striped bass are the other half of this week's story, and they're starting to do what they do every September: shadow the bunker schools instead of holding on structure and waiting for bait to come to them. The peanut bunker pushing out of the Narrow River and up toward Point Judith are dragging fish with them, and the better bass — some pushing 20 to 30 pounds, with a few reports of fish well past that off the deeper water near the Ledge — are sitting under and behind those pods rather than smashing through the top of them. Trolling umbrella rigs and bunker spoons through the deeper edges off SW Ledge in 40 to 60 feet has been more productive than blind-casting the surface schools, especially once the sun's up and the bait gets nervous and balls up tight. Live-lining a bunker on a fish-finder rig, dropped just outside the edge of a visible pod, has also been producing better-quality fish than snag-and-drop once the blitzes get picked over by mid-morning.

Block Island's fluke fishing is genuinely the best it's been all season, and it's worth a mention because a lot of boats are so locked onto albies right now that they're walking past a strong secondary bite. The drift off North Light and down along Clay Head in 30 to 50 feet has been holding good numbers of fluke to 6 and 7 pounds on the tail end of a moving tide — white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow along the bottom contour. Don't work it too fast; the fish have been eating on the pause more than the retrieve this week.

Bluefish are the wildcard worth watching closely. Montauk turned up an 18-pound-plus blue on the bunker schools this week, and that kind of size class signals a real push of big fall blues working the bait-rich rips, not just the usual harbor-choppers. That same bunker pattern is starting to bleed east toward the Block, and boats working the deeper water off the point should expect to start seeing bigger, meaner blues mixed in with the bass on those bunker pods — a welcome change from the schoolie-sized fish that dominated most of the summer.

Sea bass have settled into a steady bycatch role on the structure drops around the Ledge and the deeper reef pockets off the southwest corner, nothing to build a trip around but a reliable fill for the cooler when the albie bite goes quiet between tide changes.

Looking at the next few days, the setup favors staying on the tide more than the calendar. If the wind stays out of the southwest and holds under 15 knots, the rips off SW Ledge and Sandy Point should keep firming up as bait continues funneling past the Block on its way south. The bass pattern should keep building steadily as more bunker gets pushed out of the back bays over the next week — that's the safer long-term bet. The albie window is the more urgent one; these runs can hold for a stretch and then slide east or offshore fast once the surface water starts dropping hard. If there's only one trip to make this week, make it an early one on the last of the ebb at the Ledge, with a Plan B ready on the bunker schools off the point if the metal-eaters go quiet.
