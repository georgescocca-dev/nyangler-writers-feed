Here's what the numbers say. The NY Bight buoy is reading 64.6°F with seas running 3.6 feet at an 8-second period out of the east — not flat, not ugly, but enough chop that the open beachfront has been a grind for shore casters this week while the sheltered water inside Fire Island Inlet keeps fishing clean. That's the pattern the dock has been talking about: ocean water cooling faster than the bay, creating a thermal edge that funnels bait and predators into the inlet's current seams rather than spreading them thin along the open sand. We flagged this exact mechanism a few days back — said the cooling nearshore water would push bait tight to the beach as the moon built toward full, and that's precisely what's showing up in the reports coming off Democrat Point and the Robert Moses jetties this week.

That full moon lands October 6, and the spring tides stacking on top of it are doing real work. Bigger tidal swings mean more water moving through the inlet on both the flood and the ebb, which concentrates bait against structure and current breaks instead of letting it wander. The late-stage ebb has been the window most anglers keep circling back to — bass stacked on the current seam off Democrat Point, working the edge where the outflow meets the ocean swell, with bluefish mixed in and occasionally taking over entirely when a mullet pod gets caught in the wash.

Tactics are straightforward right now because the fish aren't being subtle about it. Bucktails in the 1.5 to 2-ounce range, white or chartreuse, cast across the seam and worked with a slow bottom hop, are producing slot and keeper-class bass for the surf crowd at Robert Moses and the inlet rocks. Swimming plugs in mullet patterns — bone, bunker, that silvery flash — are drawing blow-ups on the retrieve when the blitzes get visible, which they have been doing at dawn and again in the last hour of daylight. For the bluefish riding shotgun, metal lures and poppers worked fast through the surface commotion are about as complicated as it needs to get; these fish aren't picky when they're committed to a bait school. Anyone fishing live or chunked mullet off the jetties has reported steady pickups on the outgoing, tight to the rocks where the current slows just enough for a bait to sit.

A word of caution on fluke — state waters remain closed right now, so if you're drifting the bay edges and marking summer flounder on the sounder, that's not a season to target or keep fish from. Weakfish are around in modest numbers but nothing to build a trip around; a few have shown up mixed into bass catches on bucktails worked slow along channel edges in the bay, but it's incidental, not a target bite. Porgies and the first blackfish of the fall are starting to show on the harder structure off the inlet as the water cools, though those reports are thin and early — worth a look if you're already rigged for it, not worth a dedicated trip yet.

Looking ahead, the cold front forecast for the back half of this week is the thing to watch. Falling pressure ahead of a front typically triggers a strong feeding push — that pre-frontal window, likely Wednesday into Thursday, is when the dock expects the blitzes to get loud, with bass and blues both leaning hard into the bait before conditions shift. Behind the front, northwest wind should clean up the water and knock the temperature down another degree, which historically sets up the most explosive beachfront action of the fall as bait gets pinned against the surf line with nowhere to go. If you can only fish one window this week, the pre-frontal push on the mullet schools is the one worth planning around — just keep an eye on that building swell, because 3 to 4 feet at the inlet mouth can turn uncomfortable fast once the wind backs around.
