# West End on the 6:14 Flood: Bass Hold the Seam, Blues Crash Late
*With the tide range shrinking into the weekend neap, the Canal's bite is compressing into shorter, sharper windows — and you'd better be standing there when they open.*

**BOURNE, MA — August 3** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

6:14 flood, west end, Tuesday morning — I had a rod bent inside four minutes of the current turning at the Mashnee cut, a school bass maybe 26 inches coming off the seam where the flood pushes hard against that rock pile off Hog Island. That's the tell right now. The water's mid-to-upper 70s, thick and warm on top, and these fish don't want to work for a meal in that kind of heat. They want the current to bring it to them, and they want it fast, in the first hour of a strong stage, before the seam gets sloppy and spreads out.

That's the whole story of the Canal this week — it's a current clock, and the window's getting shorter by the day. We're sliding into the neap around the 5th and 6th, which means peak flow at both ends is easing off, and anybody who's fished this ditch for more than a season knows what that does. The rips get lazier, the seams get mushier, and the fish that were willing to sit right in the teeth of a 4-knot flood start backing off into deeper water where it's cooler and less work. We flagged this pattern last week — said the softening current would push the better fish onto the deepest, coolest structure, and that's exactly what's setting up now. Night eels at the Bourne and Sagamore Bridge pilings, and down at the Route 6 Gut where the water stacks up against the abutments, are turning into the more consistent play after dark than daylight jigging anywhere along the ditch.

Daytime, if you want a bass, you're fishing deep and you're fishing the drop-off edges. West end guys working 2-3 ounce diamond jigs and white/chartreuse Ava-style jigs tight to the bottom off the Mashnee flats and along the Hog Island channel edge have been picking at fish in that mid-20s to low-30-inch range, nothing giant, but real, and enough to make a morning worthwhile if you're willing to grind bottom on the last two hours of the ebb when the current's still got enough push to hold bait against structure.

East end's been the bluefish end of the equation. Bunker pods have been rolling through on the last of the night tides and again around first light, and where they concentrate — off Scusset Beach and out toward the rip line at the canal mouth — the blues have been all over them, blowing up on top hard enough to hear from the beach. This is a bait fishery straight through, no mystery to it: wherever those bunker schools reform after getting pushed around by boat traffic and current, the blues show up behind them within the hour. Topwater poppers and 3-ounce Kastmasters worked fast through the melee have been drawing quick, violent strikes, sometimes two and three fish before the school scatters again. If you're marking bunker balling up tight on your sounder near the east end jetty, don't wait — that window closes as fast as it opens.

Fluke have been a nice side story for the guys willing to work the flats around Mashnee Island and the channel edges by Hog Island on the moving tide. Nothing giant, but keeper-class fish in the 18 to 20-inch range have come on white Gulp paired with 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow across the sand-to-mud transitions on the last two hours of either tide when the current's still got some shape to it but isn't ripping so hard you can't feel bottom.

Now here's what's got my attention for the coming week, and it's not something I can promise you yet — bonito and albies. We haven't had a confirmed fish inside the Canal proper, but Buzzards Bay on the west side has been warming fast, and Wings Neck and the mouth of Phinneys Harbor are exactly the kind of temperature-break water that tends to produce the season's first scouting fish. If those temp breaks hold and squid keep pushing into the Bay the way they've been doing regionally, I wouldn't be shocked to hear about a bonito or two showing at the west end rip in the next week to ten days. Worth having a small epoxy jig or Deadly Dick rigged and ready if you're already out there for bass.

My plan for the week: fish the strongest hour of current on either end at first and last light, don't waste effort on the slack, and if the neap softens things up as much as I expect by the weekend, I'm sliding my best hours to full dark at the bridges with eels. That's where this bite is heading, and it's where I'll be standing.
