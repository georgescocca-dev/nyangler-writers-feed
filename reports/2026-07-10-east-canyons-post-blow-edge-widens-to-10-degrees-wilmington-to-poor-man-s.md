# Post-blow edge widens to 10 degrees — Wilmington to Poor Man's lights up for yellowfin
*The shelf break did what it does best after a blow: sharpened up and stacked bait, and the boats that made the run cashed in.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 10** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

Last week's blow did us a favor, even if it didn't feel that way sitting at the dock Tuesday watching the anemometer spin. That kind of wind stirs the water column, and when it lays down, the shelf-slope break sharpens up instead of smearing out. By Wednesday and Thursday we had a 10.3-degree temperature differential at the edge — the widest seam I've seen all season — running from Wilmington down through Poor Man's, with the Hudson east wall showing the same signature. That's not a subtle line. That's warm water stacked against cold hard enough that bait piles up on the edge and everything with a fin knows it. Boats that ran Wednesday-Thursday found yellowfin sitting right on that 100-fathom contour, and it was the best structural setup we've had since the season opened.

This week the moon's going dark — new moon builds through the weekend — and that matters more offshore than people give it credit for. Darker nights mean better squid jigging on the drift, and the overnight guys working lights over the canyons should see it. Meanwhile the NE upwelling that's been sharpening the 20-30 fathom breaks midshore hasn't let up, and the Coimbra corridor at 20 fathoms is holding a real concentration of bluefin. That's not canyon fish — that's the midshore program, closer to the beach, and it's worth a look if you don't want to burn the fuel to Veatch. Georges Bank threw a second warming surge into the mix too, which re-energized the temperature differential between Veatch and Hydrographer — that gradient had gone soft for a couple weeks and now it's back to strong. When that differential firms up, the canyon walls hold bait tighter instead of letting it spread out over open water, and that's when the marks get thick instead of scattered.

Catch-wise: yellowfin have been the story, mostly 40 to 70 pounds with a few bigger mixed in, coming on the troll early — ballyhoo behind Green/Yellow Machines and small spreader bars worked the warm seam at Wilmington and Poor Man's before the sun got high, then it shifted to chunking and jigging once the fish got tuna-shy. Butterflied ballyhoo on a long soak, 60 to 80 feet down, has been drawing bites in the chunk slick when the trolling window closes. Bigeye are mixed in but nobody's dedicating a whole trip to them yet — they're a bonus fish on the overnight drift when the squid show up under the lights. Bluefin at Coimbra have been schoolie-to-mid-size class, 40-70 inch fish, and they're eating small subsurface stickbaits and diamond jigs worked fast through the marks at 20 fathoms — that's a different game than the canyon troll, more run-and-gun, more watching the fishfinder than watching the spread.

Wahoo have been scattered — a few incidental fish on the high-speed lures pulled behind the spreader bars, nothing you'd plan a trip around yet, but the water's warm enough that I expect that to build through the month. Mahi are showing around any floating structure or weed line you can find working the edge — nothing giant, mostly schoolies in the 5-15 pound class, but a good bonus stop if you see grass or a paddy while you're running out. White marlin is still a watch-and-wait situation out here — the warm plume that's supposed to trigger the first flags is pushing toward the banks, but that's more of a mid-Atlantic canyon story right now. I'd give it another week to ten days before we see the first real flag reports out of Veatch or Atlantis. Worth having the pitch bait rigged just in case, but I wouldn't build the trip around it yet.

Fuel math on this run: from Montauk it's roughly 100 nautical miles to Veatch, more like 110-115 to Atlantis depending on where the fish are sitting on the edge — figure a 5-6 hour run each way at cruise, so you're committing to an overnight or a very long day. From Block Island or the Rhode Island ports you're looking at similar numbers to Atlantis but a shorter hop if the midshore bluefin bite at Coimbra holds — that's the play if you want tuna without burning a full tank getting to blue water. I ran the numbers this week and the midshore trip pencils out a lot better on fuel than the full canyon run, especially with diesel where it is.

Looking ahead: if that shelf-edge differential holds through the weekend — and the Georges Bank surge suggests it should — I'd keep working the Wilmington-Poor Man's seam and the Hudson east wall on the troll early, then switch to chunk or jig once the sun's up. The new moon nights this week are made for overnight squid-light trips if you've got the stamina for it — dark skies, good squid sign, and bluefin/yellowfin willing to come up under lights is about as good as canyon fishing gets. If the wahoo bite builds like I expect with this warm water sitting on the edge, by next week I'd have a couple of high-speed lures rigged in the spread no matter what else you're targeting. Best case, the differential holds and Atlantis starts producing the way Veatch did this week. If it slides or softens, I'd shift effort to Coimbra and fish the midshore bluefin instead of forcing a long run for a moving target. Either way, this is the best structural setup we've had all year — worth the trip if you can pick your weather window.
