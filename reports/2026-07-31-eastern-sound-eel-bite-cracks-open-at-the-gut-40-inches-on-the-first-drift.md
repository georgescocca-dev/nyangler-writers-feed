# Eel bite cracks open at the Gut — 40 inches on the first drift
*Current came back to the Race and Plum Gut this week, and the bass that vanished on the neap showed up hungry after dark.*

**ORIENT POINT, NY — July 31** — _by Mike Rizzo, Zone Writer_

Three-eighteen in the morning, tide dead ebb at Plum Gut, and my buddy Sal's rod loads up like someone hooked a mooring ball. Forty inches, maybe 26 pounds, came off a snag-proof eel drifted right off the tip of Plum Island where the current pours into the deep water. That fish ate on the second cast after we hit the seam — not the middle of the rip, the edge of it, where the fast water folds against the slow. That's the tell right now. These bass aren't roaming. They're pinned to current edges because the water on top is too warm to sit in all day.

We called this exact turn last week — said the neap tide had gutted the current in the Race and Plum Gut and the bite would come back as the flow rebuilt. It rebuilt. Kings Point's reading 70.9, New London's sitting at 69.4, and that's warm enough to shove quality bass off the flats and into the current-scrubbed depths where the water stays a few degrees cooler and the oxygen's better. Dawn, dusk, full dark — that's the program now. Daytime bass fishing in the Gut and the Race is mostly dead water unless you're marking fish deep and dropping bucktails on them, and even then it's a grind.

Plum Gut itself is fishing best on the last two hours of the ebb and the first hour of the flood, working the west side drop where it goes from 20 feet to better than 50 in a hurry. Snag-proof eels and 3-ounce bucktails with white or chartreuse tails, dead-drifted and bounced along bottom, are outproducing everything else. If you're on a boat, drift the edge — don't anchor in it, you'll get bounced around and lose the presentation. Bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig off the bottom will pick up stragglers too, especially right at the turn of the tide when the current slacks enough to hold bottom.

The Race is a different animal — bigger water, harder current, and it wants effort. Wire line trolling bunker spoons and umbrella rigs through the pinnacle structure west of the lighthouse is still putting bigger fish on the deck, mostly in that 15- to 25-foot band where the reef breaks up the flow. I had one push 35 pounds come off a spoon on the last of the incoming two nights ago, right where the surface goes from smooth to boiling in about ten feet. That boil is the whole game out there — cast or troll right along the edge of it, not into the white water itself.

Bluefish are the story nobody's complaining about. They love this warm surface layer that's pushing the bass down, and Orient Point and the rips off Fishers Island have had peanut bunker getting hammered on the surface most mornings this week. Poppers and 2-ounce metal — Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — worked fast through the froth are getting hit on nearly every cast when the birds are working. This blitz pattern is the same one that's been running the length of the Jersey and Long Island beaches for a week straight, and there's no reason to think it stops here. If anything it keeps spreading east as bait pushes toward the Gut. Worth running out early before the wind builds and the surface glass goes choppy — that window is short, usually gone by 9 a.m.

Fluke fishing is steady, not spectacular. Structure drifts off Orient Point and along the edges of Plum Island in 25 to 40 feet are giving up keepers mixed with a lot of shorts on white Gulp Swimming Mullets and bucktails, worked slow on the drift with the current, not against it. Nothing blitz-level, but if you cover ground and stay on structure you'll put dinner in the cooler.

Porgies are about the most reliable thing going right now on the reefs around Fishers Island — bloodworms and clam on high-low rigs in 20 to 35 feet, pretty much any stage of the tide. Good action if you want a bend in the rod between bass runs, and a solid backup plan on a slow morning.

Looking ahead, tides are building again toward the August 12 new moon, which should keep rebuilding current strength through the Gut and the Race over the next week — good news for the eel and bucktail program. Watch for the first scouting reports of false albacore starting to show around Fishers Island and the Race as bait concentrations tighten up; the water's in the right range for it and it usually starts as a rumor before it's real. If a August thunderstorm rolls through and muddies things up for a day, don't panic — this pattern's been consistent enough that it should snap right back once the water clears. I'll be on the rocks at 3 a.m. either way.
