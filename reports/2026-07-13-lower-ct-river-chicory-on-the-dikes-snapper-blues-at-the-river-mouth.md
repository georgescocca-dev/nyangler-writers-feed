# Chicory on the Dikes, Snapper Blues at the River Mouth
*The shad are three months gone and the river's settled into its summer rhythm — perch in the coves, young bass on the flats, blues showing at the mouth.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — July 13** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The day lilies are done and gone along Route 156, and what's blooming now on the dikes above Great Island is chicory — that pale blue color you only see in the dog days. I've watched that flower mark the calendar on this river for fifty years now, and it always tells me the same thing: the shad run is a memory, the water's dropped to summer flow, and if I want to catch anything worth talking about, I'd better be on the water before the sun gets up over Essex.

The spring run this year came and went the way it usually does — heavy through the second week of May, tailing off by Memorial Day, a few stragglers of hickory shad hanging around the Salmon River mouth into early June for the guys who like to chase the tail end of things. That's old news now. What we've got in mid-July is a different river altogether. Low, warm, the spartina grass gone thick and green on Great Island, and the current running slack and easy except around the moon tides. The river gauge at Essex has been reading low-summer numbers for weeks, which means less push, less color, and water that warms fast in the shallows by afternoon.

That warmth is exactly why the white perch bite has been the most reliable thing going. Selden Creek, Chester Creek, up in Lord Cove behind Whalebone — anywhere the current slows and the bottom's soft mud, the perch are stacked and feeding hard in that hour before dark when the heat comes off the water. Small stuff does the work: quarter-ounce jigs tipped with a sliver of nightcrawler, or those little curly-tail grubs in white or chartreuse, fished slow along the drop-offs where the creek channel meets the flat. Nothing heroic — a lot of eight and nine inch fish, some pushing eleven — but it's steady, and on a hot evening with the kids in the boat, that's the kind of fishing that keeps people coming back to this river.

Down at the mouth, the story's about young stripers and the first snapper blues of the season. Great Island's shallow flats have been doing what they do every July — holding juvenile bass in that skinny, warm water where the grass shrimp and mummichogs are thick. I've been working those flats on the last two hours of the incoming, poling quietly and casting small paddletails — three-inch white or bone colored — up onto the edges where the marsh grass meets open water. Most of what's coming to hand is schoolie-sized, eighteen to twenty-two inches, but they're aggressive and it's good practice for anyone teaching a kid to cast.

The bigger resident bass are still working the harder current around Saybrook Breakwater, Lynde Point, and the rock piles off the DEP Marina, but you have to fish the right window. With the river running low and warm, those fish want the cooler push of the outgoing tide, especially in that gray hour right at first light before the sun heats the flats. An eel fished slow along the breakwater rocks, or a bucktail bounced through the seam where the river current meets the Sound swell, has been the play. It's not been a banner week out there — I had two solid bass over 28 inches on Tuesday's dawn ebb and then nothing but short fish and missed hits the two mornings after. That's July on this river. You work for it, and the tide stage matters more than the moon phase right now.

Worth noting — the broader Sound has a real temperature split building, running warmer to the west around Kings Point and noticeably cooler out toward New London and the eastern approaches. That kind of gradient tends to push bait and predator fish around the mouth of the river in fits and starts, which may explain why some mornings the blues show thick around the breakwater chasing silversides, and other mornings there's nothing but gulls working empty water. The snapper blues themselves — those first-of-the-year fish, four to six inches — have started showing in the lower river around Saybrook Point and off the ferry landing in Essex, which is always a sign that their bigger cousins aren't far behind out in the Sound. Small silver spoons or a sliver of bunker on a snap-swivel rig off the town dock will keep a kid busy for an afternoon, and it's the best early warning system we've got for what's coming later this summer.

Looking ahead, I don't expect much to change until we get a real push of cooler water or a stretch of northeast wind to stir the river up. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd take the last of the outgoing at the breakwater before eight a.m., eel or bucktail, and I'd have a backup plan to slide up into Lord Cove for perch once the sun gets high and the bass stop answering. That's how you fish this river in July — patient, tide-driven, and willing to change your plan when the light changes.
