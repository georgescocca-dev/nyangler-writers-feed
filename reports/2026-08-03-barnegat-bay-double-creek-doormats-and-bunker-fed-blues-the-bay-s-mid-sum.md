# Double Creek doormats and bunker-fed blues — the bay's mid-summer rhythm sets in
*Fluke are stacking the channel edges, bluefish are chewing through bunker at the Inlet, and the stripers have gone nocturnal — here's where to put your time this week.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — August 3** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

I remember a August like this one back in the early eighties — flat calm mornings, bunker so thick off the Route 9 bridge you could walk on them, and my old skipper, Nick the Greek — no relation, just what everybody called him — telling me, "Gus, when the bait's this thick, you don't go looking for fish. You go stand where the bait is and wait for them to find you." Forty-some years later that's still the whole game on this bay in August, and this week it's playing out exactly the way he taught me.

The bay's sitting in the mid-to-upper 70s right now, which is warm enough that the big fish have gone smart on us. Striped bass are doing what they do every summer once the water gets like bathwater — they're holding tight to deep structure through the day and only really turning on in that low-light window, dawn, dusk, and after dark. We flagged this coming a week back — said as the tides softened toward the neap the bass would compress onto the strongest current hours and the deepest, coolest structure — and that's exactly what I'm seeing now. Barnegat Inlet itself, the rocks along the north side, and the pilings under the Route 37 and Mathis bridges are holding fish that come alive on the last two hours of the outgoing and the first hour of the incoming. Eels fished on a fish-finder rig, or bunker chunks bounced right along bottom in 15 to 20 feet, are the ticket after dark. Daytime, if you want a shot, you're jigging deep — bucktail or a heavy soft plastic worked slow along the channel drop by the Coast Guard station.

Fluke, though — fluke are the bright spot right now, and Double Creek Channel is doing what it's supposed to do this time of year. The drop-offs on the edges, where the channel falls from six feet down into twelve to fifteen, are stacking bait against the thermocline and the fluke are sitting right on that seam. Drifting the edges on the last of the incoming with a half-ounce to three-quarter-ounce bucktail tipped with a four-inch white or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullet has been putting keepers in the box, with a few pushing four and five pounds mixed into the shorts. Same story working the drop from the ICW channel down toward Tices Shoal — anywhere that current sweeps bait off a flat into deeper water, set up a drift and cover it slow. Squid strips on a bucktail teaser rig are still worth having tied on too, especially when the bite's finicky on an outgoing that's running light with this narrowing tide range.

That bunker business is the real story of the week, though, and it's spreading down from up north the way I figured it would when I saw the schools thickening a couple weeks back — if the bunker keep re-forming after these afternoon thunderstorm blowouts, the blues show up within hours, and that's been happening right on schedule. Big pods of adult bunker are working the bay from the mouth of Oyster Creek down through the Inlet itself, and wherever they bunch up tight against a current line the bluefish are hammering them on top — the kind of surface explosion you can see from the causeway. If you can get out early, before the wind fills in, run up on a nervous bunker school inside the Inlet or along the edge of the Double Creek channel and throw a metal-lip swimmer or a bucktail with a pork rind trailer right into the boil. It's not subtle fishing. It doesn't need to be.

Weakfish have mostly slid off the sedges now — that classic May pattern along the marsh banks north of Good Luck Point has wound down, and what's left are a few holdover fish working deeper water at night, mostly incidental to the fluke and bass bite rather than something you'd target on purpose. If you're set up for stripers after dark on bunker chunk near the Inlet rocks, don't be shocked if a weakfish grabs it instead — I had that happen twice last week.

Blowfish are exactly where they should be for early August — sandy, grassy bottom around Tices Shoal and the flats off Island Beach, small pieces of clam or squid on a snapper rig with a small hook, fished on a light outgoing. It's not glamorous fishing but it's steady, and it's the kind of thing that keeps a kid entertained on the boat while you wait for the tide to turn for the real stuff. Kingfish are showing in ones and twos on bloodworm rigs fished on clean sand bottom near Barnegat Light and along the ocean side troughs off Island Beach — nothing to build a trip around yet, but worth a rod in the sand spike if you're already surfcasting for something else.

Looking ahead, this stable high-pressure stretch should hold the bunker where they are for a few more days, which means the blue bite stays lit off the Inlet and the bass keep their pattern tight to structure after dark. The tide range keeps narrowing into the weekend neap, so current-dependent spots like the Inlet rocks and the bridge pilings will fish a touch softer — lighter jigs, slower presentations, patience on the drift. If I had one evening this week, I'd be anchored on bunker chunk along the north jetty an hour before dark, with a fluke rod rigged and ready for the ride out through Double Creek.
