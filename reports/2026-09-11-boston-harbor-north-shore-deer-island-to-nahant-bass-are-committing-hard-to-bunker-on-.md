# Deer Island to Nahant: Bass Are Committing Hard to Bunker on the New-Moon Push
*The September 10 new moon tightened bait against the harbor islands and North Shore rocks, and the first real fall feeding of the season is showing up on the sounder and in the surf.*

**BOSTON HARBOR, MA — September 11** — _by Sara Whitfield, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one window this week, spend it on the outgoing at Deer Island Light and work north toward Nahant as the tide drops. That's the call right now, and it's built on what the new moon did to this harbor over the last four days.

The September 10 new moon delivered the strongest tidal push we've seen since spring, and it's done exactly what a strong new-moon push is supposed to do — it's squeezed bunker schools tight against the beaches, the harbor islands, and the mouths of the rivers. We flagged this coming last week, when the bait was still staging loose and the bass were picking at stragglers instead of committing. That's changed. The fish are no longer nosing around the edges of bunker pods; they're driving through them, and that's the difference between early-fall scattered activity and the real thing starting to take hold.

President Roads and the flats off Deer Island have been holding pogies stacked thick enough to turn the surface nervous on the last two hours of the ebb, and the bass under them are running better than the shorts we were seeing in August — mid-20s to low-30-inch fish mixed with the occasional bigger cow working the deeper edge where the channel drops off toward the shipping lane. Snag a bunker off the school with a weighted treble and free-line it back into the pod, or if you don't want to bother snagging, a big white or bunker-pattern paddletail on a 2-ounce jighead worked slow along bottom in 15 to 25 feet is getting bit just as well. The key has been working the edges of the bait balls, not the middle — the bass are staging on the flanks where the current pushes stragglers off the school.

Spectacle Island's south side ledge is worth a look on the same tide stage. It's shallower structure than the shipping channel drop at Deer Island, and with bunker holding tighter to the harbor islands than they were even ten days ago, that ledge has been giving up bass in the 20s to slot-sized fish on bucktails and swim shads worked along the rockpile at half-tide dropping.

North Shore has been the more consistent producer for size. Nahant's rocky structure — the ledges off East Point and the boulder fields along the causeway side — are holding bass that are feeding hard on both bunker pods pushing along the shoreline and on mackerel schools that have shown up in decent numbers this week. Live mackerel under a slip float, fished tight to the rocks on the last two hours of the ebb and the first hour of the flood, has been the most reliable presentation for the bigger fish — we're hearing of 30-plus-inch bass coming off that rig consistently, with the occasional fish pushing into the high 30s. If you're not rigging live bait, a 1.5- to 2-ounce bucktail with a white or olive tube trailer, cast tight to the rocks and swum back with a slow, jigging retrieve, is drawing strikes on the same tide.

Up at Cape Ann, the Dogbar Breakwater and the rocks around Eastern Point are starting to see the same pattern — bait tightening, bass committing — though the fleet up there is reporting it's a notch behind the harbor and Nahant in terms of consistency. Give it another few days as the bunker migration continues to push north along the coast; that's usually how it plays out this time of year, with the pattern building from the harbor northward rather than showing everywhere at once. Halfway Rock has had some bluefish activity mixed in with the bass, and that's worth noting because bluefish tend to signal the broader predator response is ramping up. Snapper blues have been thick in the back coves and river mouths all summer, and that's usually the tell that adult blues are about to push in behind them as the water keeps cooling. Watch for that over the next week or two — it's a reliable precursor pattern up here just like it is further south.

Flounder fishing has been a quieter, steadier story. The mud flats in Winthrop and off Deer Island are still giving up decent flatties on the dead-low stand of the tide, worked with clam bellies or sandworms on a simple two-hook flounder rig. It's not a blitz bite, it's a bring-a-chair-and-wait bite, but it's been consistent enough to be worth the trip if bass aren't cooperating.

South toward Plymouth and Duxbury Bay, the pattern is similar but running about a week behind the harbor — the bunker haven't tightened up against the beaches down there the way they have inside Boston Harbor and along the North Shore rocks. Worth keeping an eye on Duxbury Bay and the flats off Kingston as the week progresses; if the same bait-tightening pattern shows up down there, that's usually good for another two to three weeks of solid inshore fishing before things push offshore for the fall.

Looking ahead: expect the harbor and Nahant pattern to hold and likely intensify over the next several days as the tidal energy from this new moon keeps working, before it starts to ease as the moon moves off its peak phase. That's typically a two-week window before the next full moon resets the cycle, and it should be the best stretch of striper fishing the harbor sees all year. If the wind stays out of the northwest and holds the water clean, the outgoing tide at Deer Island and the last two hours of ebb along the Nahant rocks are where the time is best spent right now.
