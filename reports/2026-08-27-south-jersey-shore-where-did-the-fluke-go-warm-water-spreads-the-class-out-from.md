# Where did the fluke go? Warm water spreads the class out from Absecon to Great Egg
*Kingfish and spot are filling the surf bags while the doormats scatter — and the first bonito showed at the inlets right on schedule.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — August 27** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

A reader asked me at the bait shop counter this week why his fluke numbers were down even though the water looked clean and the tide was moving right. Good question, and it's the same one I've been fielding since the middle of August. The answer is warm water, plain and simple. Surf temps have been sitting in the mid-70s from Atlantic City down through Ocean City, and that kind of warmth doesn't concentrate fluke — it spreads them out. Instead of holding tight to structure where you can pattern them, the fish are scattered across broad, thinly-oxygenated flats in the back bays. That's softened catch rates almost everywhere except the places where a cooler bottom layer still hangs on: channel edges, inlet mouths, and the deeper humps in 40 to 60 feet off the beach.

So class, here's the lesson for the week: fish the seams, not the flats. In Absecon Inlet, that means working the deeper channel edge off the Route 30 bridge and down toward the Coast Guard station, 25 to 35 feet, on the last two hours of the outgoing tide when the current is still pulling bait off the sod banks but hasn't gone fully slack. A four-inch white Gulp swimming mullet on a half-ounce bucktail, dragged slow along bottom, has been the most consistent producer. Great Egg Inlet is running the same pattern — the deeper hole just inside the rock jetty on the Ocean City side has been holding better-grade fish than the flats to the north, especially on the second half of the ebb.

While the doormats have gone quiet, the surf has turned into a different kind of classroom entirely. Kingfish, croaker, and spot have moved in thick on the beachfronts from Absecon Island down through Ocean City, a direct result of that same warm-water push riding up from the south. High-low bottom rigs with small pieces of bloodworm or synthetic bloodworm strips, cast just beyond the second bar, have been filling coolers for anglers willing to sit on a spike and work the tide. Stone Harbor and Townsends Inlet have shown the same signs — smaller hooks, size 4 or 6, seem to be outproducing the bigger stuff for kingfish, and croaker have been mixed in on the same rigs during the last of the incoming.

Bluefish remain the one species you can set your watch by. Snapper blues are thick in the back bays behind Ocean City and Sea Isle City, hammering small silver spoons and Gotcha plugs off the docks on any tide. Cocktail-sized blues, a pound and a half to three pounds, are working the tide changes at Absecon and Townsends Inlets as rainfish and peanut bunker begin staging up along the beachfront — an early tell that the seasonal transition is starting even while the surface water stays summer-warm. And here's something worth noting: the first small bonito have started showing at the inlets on those same tide changes, chasing the same bait. We flagged the mullet run coming in this column two weeks back, said it would flip attention toward bluefish and early surf interest, and that's exactly what's beginning to unfold along these beaches now.

Striped bass are in their predictable late-August holding pattern — daytime activity pushed to structure and deeper water, with the better action coming after dark around the inlet jetties and bridge lights at Brigantine and Longport. That said, the moon builds to full on August 28, and I've taught this lesson before: bigger tides mean stronger current, and stronger current concentrates bait against structure. Expect the swing at Absecon and Great Egg Inlets to sharpen over the next several nights as the tidal range grows. If you're fishing after dark, work bunker chunks or a bucktail dressed with Gulp on the leading edge of the current seam, right where the light meets the shadow line under the bridges. That's where the bass have been staging.

Weakfish have been quiet, as expected for this time of year, though a few keepers have come from the deeper holes in Great Egg Inlet on the early morning outgoing, fished with bucktails tipped with a strip of squid. Triggerfish continue to show around the reef sites and wreck structure off Ocean City and Absecon in that 40- to 60-foot range where fluke anglers are also finding their better fish — small pieces of clam or squid on a simple bottom rig will take them if you're marking structure and holding position.

Looking ahead, the next few days should hold mostly steady — southwest winds in the 8- to 12-knot range, warm surface water, stable high pressure. That means the fluke pattern stays the same: work the channel edges and deeper structure, skip the open flats. The building moon should sharpen the inlet current over the coming nights, which is where I'd put my time if bass are the target. And keep an eye on the beachfront — with rainfish and peanut bunker staging at the inlets, the mullet run window is closing in, and that's when this whole zone flips its attention toward blues and the first real surf-bass interest of the fall.
