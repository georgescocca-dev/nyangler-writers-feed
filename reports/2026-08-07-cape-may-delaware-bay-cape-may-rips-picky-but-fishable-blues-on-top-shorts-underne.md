# Cape May Rips picky but fishable: blues on top, shorts underneath, drum long gone
*Warm water and building new-moon tide are sharpening the bite at structure — but you've got to work for it right now.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — August 8** — _by Capt. Kate Brennan, Zone Writer_

Rips grade: picky but fishable. That's the honest read this week, and I'm not going to dress it up for you. We're getting bluefish — good ones, 4 to 8 pounds — chopping bunker on top when the tide's right, and we're getting stripers, but they're mostly schoolies and the occasional low-slot fish, not the kind of numbers that make you forget what month it is. August on the Rips is what it is: warm water, scattered bait, and a bite that depends entirely on you being in the right seam at the right stage of tide.

Here's the why. Delaware Bay water dumping out past Cape May Point is running mid-to-upper 70s right now, and that warm water doesn't hold bait the way a cooler push does — it scatters it. So instead of finding one big wad of peanuts and working it all afternoon, you're hunting pockets. The best window I've had all week has been the last two hours of the outgoing into the start of the flood, right where the bay current collides with the ocean side over the shoal structure. That collision line is where the bait gets pinned and the blues and bass show up to work it. Miss that window and you're just marking bait on the sounder with nothing underneath it.

Tactically, I've been throwing 1.5 to 2-ounce bucktails in white and chartreuse, working them with a slow lift-and-drop right through the seam, and when that's not drawing strikes I've gone to live bunker on a fishfinder rig, freelined back into the current. That's put bluefish in the box consistently and picked up a keeper bass here and there, but I want to be straight with you — it's not a slam dunk. Some drifts you get bit twice, some you get bit zero times. You need the tide stage right or you're wasting gas.

Now, if you're asking about black drum — that ship sailed in May, and it sailed a while ago. Nobody's finding drum in Delaware Bay in August, and if someone tells you different they're either lying or confused about the calendar. That run is done until next spring, full stop.

What is producing steady, honest numbers is weakfish in the bay channels. They're sitting deep — 18 to 25 feet — off Fortescue and down around Brandywine Shoal, and they want bloodworms or shedder crab on a high-low rig fished right on bottom, slow and patient. This isn't a fast bite, but it's a real one. Guys working those channels on the last two hours of the ebb are putting together decent counts of weakfish mixed with kingfish and croaker as bycatch, which tells you the bait's stacking in the deeper water as the shallows heat up past what those fish want to tolerate.

Fluke are the other bright spot, and honestly the most reliable thing I've got for you this week. The mouth of the bay and the back-bay drop-offs off Hereford Inlet are holding fish, and the pattern's simple — drift 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails right along the edges where the deeper channel breaks up into the flats, and time it for the last two hours of the outgoing when bait gets flushed off the shallows into that deeper water. I've had fish to 22 inches doing exactly that, nothing spectacular, but a legitimate keeper mixed into the shorts most drifts.

Offshore of the inlet, on the nearshore reef structure, sea bass and porgy have been steady — not fast, but dependable — on clam and squid fished tight to bottom. Triggerfish are mixed in on those same pieces too, which is normal for this time of year once the water's held warm this long. If you want a bend-the-rod afternoon without burning a tank of fuel chasing bait pockets, that's your play.

We flagged this last week — said the tides would build toward the August 12 new moon and sharpen up the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's happening. The rip current is running harder every day as we approach the new moon, and that extra push is concentrating bait tighter against the shoal edges than it was seven days ago. I expect that trend to keep building right through the weekend and into early next week.

So here's my read going forward: if I've got one shot at the Rips this week, I'm fishing the two hours before the moon and the two hours after, betting the stronger current does what stronger current usually does — pins bait hard against structure and turns picky fish into willing ones. If that doesn't materialize, I've got Plan B sitting right there in the bay — the weakfish and fluke patterns aren't glamorous, but they're honest, and honest beats hopeful every time out here.
