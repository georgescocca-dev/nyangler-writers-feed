# Double Creek doormats and snapper blues everywhere — the bay's running hot but the fluke are still there if you know the bends
*Water's pushing 78 in the shallows, but the channel edges are holding fish for anyone willing to fish the bottom of the tide.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — August 14** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

I remember an August a long time back, mating on a Point Pleasant party boat, when the mate before me — old Charlie, gone now near twenty years — told me the bay in high summer is a lazy man's puzzle. Everything's still there, he said, it's just gone quiet and gone deep. You have to go find it instead of waiting for it to find you. I think about that every August, and this one's no different. The bay's pushing toward 78 degrees in the shallows this week, and that heat has pushed the bait and the fluke both down into the channel edges and the deeper holes where there's a little relief from the sun beating on the flats all day.

Double Creek Channel is doing what it always does for me in mid-August — producing on the bottom third of the ebb, when the current's still got enough push to pull bait off the shoulders and stack it in the bends. I've been running white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails down there, dragging them slow along bottom in 8 to 12 feet, and picking through a mess of shorts for every keeper. That ratio isn't great, but the keepers have been decent — 20 to 22 inches mostly, with one angler I talked to at the ramp in Waretown showing me a fish pushing 5 pounds he took on a killie under a bobber, drifted right along the edge where the channel drops off. Barnegat and Long Beach Island backbays have a reputation this summer for punching well above their weight on big fluke — somebody weighed a 12-pound-plus doormat over in Bayonne a couple weeks back, and I wouldn't be shocked if there's a fish that size sitting in one of these deep bends right now. If you're hunting a wall-hanger instead of dinner, fish live killies or mullet strips on a fish-finder rig right on the bottom where the channel narrows, and be patient. Doormats don't chase.

Oyster Creek's mouth is holding fish too, same story — last two hours of the outgoing, working the drop where the creek dumps into the bay. That's classic August pattern; the water's warm enough now that there's no thermal edge to exploit like you get in the cold months, so it's really about current concentrating bait, not temperature. Bucktail and Gulp combo, or a simple high-low rig with squid strips if the current's ripping too hard for plastics to sit right.

Snapper blues are thick as I've seen them in a few years — every dock, every marina basin from Toms River down to Forked River has snappers hammering silversides in the last hour of daylight. That's a genuinely good sign. Those fish grow fast, and by Labor Day some of them are pushing cocktail-blue size. If you've got a kid with a bamboo pole and a piece of bloody bunker on a small hook, that's your entertainment for the next month, and it's also a preview of the adult bluefish that'll be working through the inlet as September comes on.

Weakfish have thinned out from where they were back in May at the sedges — that bite's mostly a memory for this season, though I had a report of a couple of legal fish taken at dusk on the outgoing near the sedge banks off Tices Shoal, worked with a small swimming plug fished slow. Don't plan a trip around it, but if you're out there for something else and the light's going gold, it's worth a couple of casts.

Blowfish, on the other hand, are exactly where they should be — thick in the shallow, warm flats, especially around Tices Shoal and the skinny water off Cedar Creek. Small hooks, clam strips or squid, snelled two-hook rigs fished right on bottom in 3 to 6 feet. It's not glamorous fishing but it's about the most reliable thing going in the bay right now, and it's a great way to keep a kid or a first-timer bent over a rod all afternoon. Blue claw crabbing has been excellent in that same warm shallow water too — chicken necks on a hand line off any dock along the western shore will keep a pot full.

Striped bass in the bay proper have been scarce, and I'll be honest about that rather than dress it up. This time of year the resident fish push toward the inlet and the ocean side looking for cooler, moving water, and what I'm hearing from up the coast — Sound, Buzzards Bay, even up toward the Cape — is a genuine peanut-bunker-driven blitz pattern building early, which tells me the fall staging is starting to think about happening. It hasn't shown up here yet in any real way, but Barnegat Inlet on the last two hours of the incoming, right around the rocks and the Coast Guard station, is where I'd throw bucktails at dusk if I wanted a shot at a bass this week. Don't expect a blitz. Expect maybe one good fish if the tide and the light line up.

Looking ahead, we're a couple days off the new moon, and the tidal flow's been building nicely into it — that's exactly the kind of push that sharpens the bite at current-driven structure like Double Creek and the Oyster Creek mouth. I'd expect the fluke bite to hold steady to slightly improving through the coming week as that flow keeps concentrating bait in the bends, and I'd keep an eye on the inlet for the first real signs of bunker schools pushing through, because when they show, the blues and maybe a few bass won't be far behind.
