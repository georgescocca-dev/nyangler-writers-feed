# Jeffrey's Ledge, 190 to 220 feet: haddock stacked on the mud, cod scratching the gravel edge
*Mackerel schools pushing onto the north end have the first bluefin sign showing early, but the real giant push is still weeks off.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — July 17** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Two hundred and six feet, soft mud, on the west side of the basin between Jeffrey's and Stellwagen's northern approach — that's where I found the best haddock of the week, and it's the number I keep coming back to when guys ask me where to start. Mud like that in the low 200s is haddock water this time of year. Gravel is a different animal, and the two don't mix on my chart plotter — I mark them in different colors so I don't waste a drift.

The haddock bite has been steady, not explosive. High-low rigs with clam belly, dropped straight down and worked slow off bottom, six inches at a time, are putting keeper haddock in the box in the 3 to 5 pound range, with the occasional 7-pounder mixed in to keep things honest. Best window has been the top two hours of the tide change, when the drift slows enough to hold bottom without a ton of lead. I've been running 10 to 12 ounces depending on the push, more on the bigger tides. When the current really starts moving on the full flood, the fish seem to shut down for a stretch and then turn back on as it slackens toward the top. Patience pays here — chasing marks on a hard drift just burns fuel.

Cod have been tougher, and I'll say that plainly instead of dressing it up. The gravel edge on the east side of Jeffrey's, in that 130 to 160 foot band where the bottom breaks hard from mud to rock, has given up fish, but it's been a scratch bite — two or three keepers a trip in the 8 to 15 pound class, not the kind of numbers we were seeing in past Julys. Diamond jigs, 10 to 14 ounces with a bright green or white tube teaser above the hook, worked with a short lift-and-drop right on the bottom third of the water column, have outfished bait rigs for me two trips running. The cod are sitting tight to structure, so precision on the drop matters more than volume of gear in the water. If you're marking fish on the sounder and not connecting, slow the retrieve down — these cod aren't chasing much right now.

Pollock have been the saving grace for guys who want steady rod-bending action without the grind. Working the humps on Platts Bank, especially the north knoll where the bottom comes up from 180 to about 110 feet, jigging Norwegian jigs or small diamonds through the mid-water column has been putting nice fish in the 6 to 12 pound range in the boat consistently. Pollock don't need you to hit bottom — they're suspended, often 30 to 50 feet up off the structure, and once you find that band it's been fast fishing, a nice change of pace mid-trip when the cod and haddock go quiet.

Hake have shown up better after dark on the mud in Scantum Basin, which isn't a surprise — that's classic hake water once the sun drops and the water column settles. Not many of us are running out there for an overnight soak this time of year with bluefin starting to flicker on the radar, but if you've got the fuel and the patience, whiting-sized hake and a few larger ones have been coming over the rail on squid strips fished right on bottom in 250-plus feet.

Now for the fish everybody's really asking about. Mackerel have moved onto the north end of Jeffrey's in good numbers over the last week and a half, and where there's mackerel piling up, there's usually something bigger paying attention. We've had a handful of bluefin sign — a few boats marking fish and a couple of hookups on trolled spreader bars and green machines run wide off the ledge near Cape Ann's outer approach — but I want to be straight with you: it's early sign, not a pattern yet. This is mid-July. The real giant show on Jeffrey's doesn't typically load up until late September into October, when the sand eels and herring get thick and those fish start committing to the ledge for weeks at a time. Right now what we're seeing is scattered, opportunistic fish following bait, not staged fish holding structure. Worth a trolling pass if you're already out there jigging cod and haddock, but I wouldn't plan a dedicated tuna trip around it yet.

Looking ahead, if the mackerel schools keep building on the north end the way they have been, I'd expect more bluefin sign through late July, especially on calm mornings when the bait balls up tight near the surface and you can actually see it happening. For the bottom fishing, I'd keep working that mud in the low 200s for haddock — it's been the most reliable piece of the puzzle — and treat the gravel cod bite as a bonus rather than the main event until we see the water cool a touch more. Wind's been cooperative most mornings this week, laying down enough by first light to make the run out comfortable, but check the afternoon forecast before you commit to Platts or Tillies — that's a longer run home if it kicks up out of the southwest.
