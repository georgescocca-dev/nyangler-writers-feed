# Fort Trumbull Rips Load Up as Bunker Push Down the Thames
*Cooling nights and Harvest Moon tides are shoving bait toward the river mouth, and the bass are starting to stack on it.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — September 21** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

The current seam off Fort Trumbull has changed character in the last ten days, and that tells you more than any thermometer. Through most of August that seam ran thin and lazy on the ebb — a soft push past the fort wall with a scattering of small bait getting nervous in it. Now it's loaded. Peanut bunker and adult menhaden are stacking against that current line where the harbor channel narrows and the ebb accelerates past the fort, and where there's bait pressed against a seam like that, there are bass working underneath it. New London's reading around 69 degrees at the mouth, which is still warm for the calendar, but the nights have turned, and that's the real driver here — cooler air is pushing the baitfish exodus out of the upper river faster than the water temp alone would suggest. We flagged this pattern building last week, said the adult bite would intensify at the mouth as that exodus accelerated, and this is exactly what's showing up on the water now.

The Harvest Moon tides are doing their part too. Bigger swings mean more water moving through the Thames on each cycle, and that's translating into bait getting flushed through the harbor in bigger, more concentrated pushes rather than a slow trickle. Anglers working the river mouth and the flats off Fort Trumbull on the last two hours of the ebb have been running into these pushes at first light and again toward dusk — not constant, but predictable enough that you can set your morning around it. The bite is genuinely building, not exploding. Some mornings the surface stays quiet until the tide really starts moving, then it goes from nothing to bunker getting nervous and bass punching through in the space of twenty minutes.

Striped bass in the 24 to 34-inch range are the bulk of what's coming over the rail right now, with a scattering of fish pushing past that mixed into bunker schools holding tight to the pilings under the Gold Star Bridge. Live-lining a snagged bunker on a 6/0 circle hook and 30-pound fluorocarbon leader has been the most consistent producer there, fished on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in that current without killing the bait's natural swim. Bucktails in the 1.5 to 2-ounce range, white or bunker-colored, worked with a slow lift-and-drop along the bridge pilings on the last of the ebb, have also been picking off fish holding deep in that current break — 15 to 20 feet down is where most of the takes are coming, right where the pilings break the flow and bass sit waiting for bait to get swept past.

Bluefish are riding along with every one of these bunker pushes, and they're not small. Cocktail blues in the 3 to 5-pound range are mixing into the surface activity at the mouth, especially on the early morning push, and they'll hit anything moving fast — Hopkins jigs, Kastmasters, or diamond jigs worked with a hard, erratic retrieve are drawing the most aggressive strikes. If you're marking bait balls getting nervous off Ocean Beach or Pleasure Beach on the outgoing tide, that's usually blues underneath more than bass, and it's worth a few casts to sort out which is which before committing to a spot.

Porgies are still around in decent numbers on the deeper structure off Pleasure Beach, working sandworms and squid strips on standard hi-lo rigs in 20 to 30 feet, though that bite has cooled slightly from its late-summer peak as fish start their seasonal shift toward deeper winter holding areas. Blackfish anticipation is building in the shop conversations more than on the water right now — the structure bite hasn't fully switched on yet, but with water starting to cool through the low 60s in the weeks ahead, the crab-and-clam crowd is already prepping tackle for the rockpiles and the harbor's deeper structure. That push typically doesn't get serious until October, but it's worth having gear ready.

Looking at the next several days, expect the pattern at the river mouth to keep building rather than fade. The Harvest Moon tides still have a few days of strong push left in them, and each cycle should keep funneling more bait down through the Thames and concentrating it against structure — the fort wall, the bridge pilings, the current seams off Pleasure Beach. If there's only one window worth planning around, it's the last two hours of the ebb at first light, when the bait gets squeezed hardest through the narrows and the bass and blues are waiting on the downstream side of it. If the wind stays manageable, that window should hold up through the week. Where it could shift is if a real cold front comes through and knocks the water temp down sharply — that tends to accelerate the whole migration south and can shut down the mouth bite for a few days while fish reposition. Nothing on the immediate horizon suggests that's imminent, but this time of year it's always the next data point worth watching. For now, work the seams, match the bait size with peanut bunker imitations when the fish are picky, and don't be surprised if the better fish are holding a little deeper than you'd expect for late September — they're already thinking about winter.
