# Napatree on the Push: Albies Find the Breachway, Bunker Stack the Ledges
*Peanut bunker are pinning against the rocks from Sakonnet to Watch Hill, and everything with fins is following them in.*

**WATCH HILL, RI — September 14** — _by Billy Tanaka, Zone Writer_

Walk any rock pile from Second Beach to Napatree right now and you'll see it before you cast a line — peanut bunker balled up tight against the ledges, getting pushed by current and by whatever's underneath them. Silversides are mixed in too, skittering off the rocks on the last of the ebb. That bait isn't wandering. It's pinned, and pinned bait against structure this time of year means one thing on the South Shore: the fall parade has started, and it's using the rocks as a highway.

We flagged this last week — said blitz-style activity would build as bait kept concentrating along the beachfront under the strengthening tide, and that's exactly the shape of things now. The tidal range is building toward the full moon at the end of the month, which means more water moving through the breachways twice a day, and more bait getting squeezed into places where it can't get away. Napatree and Weekapaug are the two breachways doing the most work right now, and both are seeing albies push through on the last two hours of the incoming, when the current is really running and the bait has nowhere to hide but tight to the rocks.

False albacore have been the story of the week along this stretch. They showed first off Sakonnet Point, working the rip where the reef structure breaks up the current, and they've worked their way west through the week — Second Beach, then the mouth of the Sakonnet River, now solidly into the Weekapaug and Napatree breachways on the push. This tracks with what we said was coming: a warm-water edge holding bait tight to structure, and that edge sliding toward eastern Long Island and Montauk over the coming week. RI is getting the leading wave of it right now. Small epoxy jigs — half-ounce, in the peanut bunker pattern — and 3-inch Deceiver-style flies on an intermediate line have both been producing, but only when you can match the speed of the bait. These fish are keying on panicked, erratic peanut bunker, not smooth swimming baitfish, so a jerky retrieve beats a straight crank almost every time.

Bluefish are riding the same bait and the same tide stages, and the size class has been notably better than midsummer — several fish in the 8 to 12-pound range have come off bunker schools working the rocks at Sakonnet Point and off Jamestown's western shore. Bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig, fished on bottom in 15 to 25 feet where the current sweeps past a ledge, have been steady producers on the outgoing. Poppers work too when the blues are up on top chopping bait, which has been happening in the last hour of daylight most evenings this week.

Striped bass are the one piece of this puzzle still coming together rather than fully arrived. The bunker schools funneling out of Narragansett Bay are tightening up the way they do every September, and bass are starting to show real interest rather than just picking at stragglers — which is the pattern we called a week ago. Right now that interest is concentrated at dawn and dusk around the mouth of the Sakonnet River and along the deeper edges off Jamestown, where soft plastics — 6-inch paddle tails in bunker or white, fished slow on a 3/4-ounce jighead — have taken slot fish in the 26 to 32-inch range. It's not a blitz yet. It's a build. Expect that to change fast once the bigger bunker pods start getting pushed out of the upper bay in real numbers, likely within the next two weeks as water continues cooling.

Blackfish season is quietly underway on the ledges, and this is where the South Shore rewards patience over run-and-gun. The classic spots — the rock piles off Newport and the boulder fields around Jamestown's western shore — are holding tog in that 6 to 15-foot zone right on the slack tide, both top and bottom. Green crabs, fished on a simple jig-head rig dropped right into the cracks, are doing the work. This early in the season the fish are still shallow and aggressive; as water cools further into October they'll slide deeper, but for now the slack-tide window on the inshore ledges is producing better than anyone expects for mid-September.

Fluke are fading but not gone. A few keepers are still coming off the deeper water outside Narragansett Bay on bucktail-and-Gulp combos, but the bite has thinned out as bait spreads along the beachfront instead of holding in tight pockets. Scup are still around the rocky structure in decent numbers on squid strips, a reliable fallback if the bigger stuff isn't cooperating.

Looking at the next few days, the pattern holds together as long as the tidal range keeps building and the wind doesn't turn onshore hard enough to muddy the breachways. If the current stays clean, the incoming push at Napatree and Weekapaug should keep producing albies, and the bunker schools moving out of the bay should keep pulling more committed bass into the mix at Sakonnet and Jamestown. Best case, this builds into real blitz conditions by the weekend as more bait gets squeezed through the system. If the wind kicks up out of the southeast and stirs the shoreline, shift toward the more protected water inside Jamestown and work the tog bite on the slack instead — that pattern doesn't care much what the surface is doing.
