# Bunker Push Hits Barnegat Inlet — Blues and Early Bass Show, Fluke Fade at Double Creek
*The mullet and peanut bunker are moving, the blues are chasing them through the inlet, and the fluke bite is winding down for the season.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — September 14** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

Forty years ago my uncle Nick used to say you could set your watch by the bunker in September — not the calendar, the bunker. When those big pods started stacking up off the north jetty at Barnegat Inlet and the gulls started working nervous water instead of just sitting on the beach, that's when he'd tell me to leave the fluke rods in the rack and rig up something heavier. He was right more often than the Farmer's Almanac. That pattern is running again this week, right on cue, and it's the story of the bay right now.

The bunker schools have been funneling through the inlet on the building tide for about ten days, thickest on the last two hours of the incoming when the current pushes them up against the rock pile and the old Coast Guard station. We flagged this coming — said the new-moon tides would push bait through the inlet and pull the first real bass and blue activity with it, and that's exactly what the docks are talking about this week. Snappers and cocktail blues have been mixed in with the peanut pods pushing into Oyster Creek and up into Double Creek Channel on the afternoon tide, and there have been a few legitimate teenage blues — three and four pounds — busting bait right at the mouth of the inlet on the last of the ebb, when the water funnels out fast and pins everything against the rocks.

Fluke, meanwhile, are doing what they do this time of year, which is thinning out. The bite in Double Creek Channel and off the ICWA markers near Waretown has gone from a solid keeper-a-drift pattern in August to a pick — one or two fish for an afternoon's work, mostly shorts, with the occasional 18- to 20-incher mixed in on a Gulp Swimming Mullet in white or pink, three-eighths ounce bucktail, dragged slow along the channel edge in 8 to 12 feet. We said two weeks back that the fluke bite would keep tapering through September as the water eased and the fish started sliding toward the inlet and out to depth, and that's holding true. If you want one more crack at them, fish the last two hours of the incoming tide in Double Creek where the channel drops from six feet to twelve — that ledge is still holding a few stragglers staging before they push out through the inlet themselves.

Striped bass are the ones to watch now. Nothing dramatic yet inside the bay — this isn't Raritan Bay or the bunker schools off Sandy Hook where the big girls are already committing hard — but there's real interest building. A few keeper bass, mostly in that 26- to 30-inch range, have been taken on live bunker snagged right off the inlet rocks and drifted back on a fish-finder rig, weight just heavy enough to hold bottom in the current sweeping past Barnegat Light. Best window has been the top of the tide into the first hour of the ebb, when the bunker get pushed tight against the rocks and the bass slide up out of the deeper water to work them. If you don't want to snag your own bait, a bunker chunk fished on a 6/0 circle hook, fished right on bottom in that same stretch, has been picking up a fish or two as well. Nothing to call a blitz — yet — but the pieces are there: bait, current, cooling water, and a moon that's about to build tidal range again heading into the weekend.

Weakfish have shown up sporadically around the sedge banks off Tices Shoal, which is a little later in the season than the classic May pattern most of us grew up on, but a few 2- and 3-pound fish have come off bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, fished slow along the grass edges on the last of the outgoing when the water clears up and pulls bait off the flats. Blowfish and kingfish have both been slow — kingfish especially, which isn't unusual once the water starts cooling from the surface down, pushing them off the sandy inlet-adjacent bottom they favor in high summer.

Looking at the next few days, the tidal range keeps building toward the weekend, and that's the piece that matters most. Bigger swings mean more water moving through the inlet, which means more bait getting pushed and pulled, which is exactly what triggers the bluefish blitzes everybody's hoping to see spread from the inlet up into Oyster Creek and the mouth of Double Creek. If the wind stays out of the west or northwest and lays down in the mornings, the smart play is first light at the inlet on the tail of the ebb, working topwater plugs or diamond jigs through any nervous water near the rocks, then sliding back into the bay as the sun climbs and the bait scatters into the marsh creeks. Give the fluke one more look in Double Creek if you want a change of pace, but don't build a whole trip around it — that bite is closing out, and the story for the rest of September is going to be written by the bunker and whatever's chasing them.
