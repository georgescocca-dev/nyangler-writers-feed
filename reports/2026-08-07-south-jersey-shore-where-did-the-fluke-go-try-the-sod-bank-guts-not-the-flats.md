# Where did the fluke go? Try the sod bank guts, not the flats
*The shallow bays got too warm for comfort, so the fluke and weakfish slid into the channel edges — and the peanut bunker call from two weeks ago just landed.*

**ABSECON, NJ — August 7** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get this question every August, almost word for word: "Mr. Freeman, we fished the same sod bank we've been killing it on since June and got nothing — where'd they go?" Fair question. Class is in session.

The shallow flats behind Brigantine and up in the Absecon meadows have been sitting in the mid-70s for weeks now, and fluke don't love that. They're not gone, they just moved — off the skinny mud and into the deeper guts, the channel edges, and the drop-offs where moving water keeps the bottom a few degrees cooler and the oxygen fresh. I've had my best luck lately working the deeper bends off Absecon Inlet in 15 to 22 feet, and the cuts along the Intracoastal near Graveling Point in Great Egg Harbor Bay in similar depths. Drop a 3/4-ounce white bucktail tipped with Gulp Swimming Mullet, work it slow along the bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing, and you're in business. I had four keepers to 19 inches out there Tuesday on that exact combo, mixed with a handful of shorts that needed to go back and grow up.

The better bite has actually been on the structure — the Route 30 causeway bridge pilings in Absecon Bay, and the rock piles along Great Egg's channel markers. Fluke are stacking tight to that cover during the day, ambushing bait as it sweeps by on the tide. Chartreuse bucktails with a Berkley Gulp swimming mullet trailer, cast up-current and bounced back with the sweep, has been the difference-maker. Don't be afraid to go heavier than you think you need — 1 ounce if the current's really running — because staying on bottom is everything right now.

Now here's the one I want to flag, because we called this a few weeks back: the peanut bunker pushing into the back bays was going to pull the weakfish with it, and that's exactly what's happening. I've had reports of legal weakfish — 14 to 17 inches, a few better — coming out of the deeper holes in Absecon Bay and around the Route 52 causeway in Great Egg on light jigs and small swimming plugs worked over peanut schools at dusk. If you find the bunker balled up nervous on the surface, get a Storm Shad or a 3-inch Gulp swimming shad down under them on a light jighead. That pattern should only get stronger through the rest of August as more peanuts filter into the estuary.

Bluefish are the easy button right now if you've got kids or first-timers in the boat. Snapper blues are thick in nearly every back bay creek from Brigantine down through Sea Isle — small spoons, Kastmasters, anything shiny thrown on light spinning gear will keep a rod bent all afternoon. The adult blues are still doing their dawn-and-dusk thing along the beachfront, chasing bunker schools that are getting pushed tight to the sand at Ocean City, Sea Isle City, and down around Townsends Inlet. If you see the gulls diving and the water nervous just off the second bar, that's your window — it doesn't last long once the sun gets up and the bait scatters back offshore.

Striped bass have settled into the pattern you'd expect for early August — they want the dark and they want structure. Nighttime bunker chunking around the Absecon Inlet rocks and the Great Egg Inlet jetty has been the more consistent approach than daytime casting. Fresh bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, tight to the rocks on the last of the incoming into the first hour of the outgoing, has produced a few keeper-class fish in the 28 to 32-inch range for guys willing to put in the late hours. Daytime striper fishing in the surf has been mostly a non-event — the water's too warm and too bright for them to want to commit in the wash.

Surf guys working bait rigs at Ocean City and Stone Harbor Point have kingfish and mullet thick in the wash right now, which is exactly where they should be this time of year. Bloodworm or Fishbites on a high-low bait rig, fished in the first thirty yards of surf on the incoming, has been steady if unspectacular — good action for a family outing, not a trophy hunt. I'd also keep an eye on the jetty rocks at Townsends and the wreck structure off Great Egg for triggerfish — they show up sporadically this time of year around anything hard on the bottom, and small pieces of clam or squid on a light jig will find them if they're home.

Looking ahead, the tide cycle is building stronger through the week, which should sharpen up that fluke bite on the channel edges — more current means more bait movement, and more bait movement means more fish committing to feed rather than just holding. If the weather stays settled, I'd spend my time on the last two hours of the outgoing at either inlet, working structure with bucktails, and save the chunk bait for after dark at the rocks. That's where I'd put my own boat this weekend, and I'd have the light jigs rigged and ready in case that weakfish bite keeps building the way I think it will.
