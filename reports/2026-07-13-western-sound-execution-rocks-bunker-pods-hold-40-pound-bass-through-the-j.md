# Execution Rocks bunker pods hold 40-pound bass through the July heat
*A dock-shop debate about summer stripers gets settled by a sonar screen full of bunker off Matinecock and a rod bent double at Execution Rocks.*

**CITY ISLAND, NY — July 13** — _by Frank "The Sound Pro" Marinaro, Zone Writer_

Guy at the bait shop this week told me the bass fishing on the Sound is done for the summer, that everything worth catching has slid east toward the Race and we're left chasing schoolies and snappers till September. I let him finish his coffee before I told him about Tuesday.

Tuesday morning, dead calm, I idled out past Execution Rocks lighthouse right on the tail end of the outgoing and put the sonar over a bunker pod so thick it looked like a shadow on the bottom. Snagged three in about ninety seconds with a weighted treble, hooked one through the nose on a 7/0 circle, and drifted it back along the rockpile in eighteen feet of water. Didn't have to wait long. Rod loaded up, drag started singing, and twelve minutes later I had a bass pushing 40 pounds in the net. That's not a fluke catch — that's a pattern, and it's been holding for about two weeks now.

Here's the why of it. Kings Point is reading 71 degrees, which is warm but not scalding, and it's a good ten degrees north of what they're seeing out at New London. That west-to-east gradient means the bait — and I mean serious bunker biomass — is stacking up on our side of the Sound instead of pushing east like it usually does by mid-July. Matinecock Point has been holding pods since June and they haven't left. Execution Rocks, Stepping Stones, the flats off Hart Island — all of it is loaded with peanut and adult bunker, and where the bunker sits, the big fish sit under them, especially on that last hour of the ebb when the current funnels bait past the rockpiles and gives the bass an ambush point.

The catch — and I'll be straight with you — this isn't an all-day bite. Midday, with the sun beating down and the surface water pushing past 72, the bigger fish go deep and sulky. I've marked them stacked tight to the bottom in 25 to 30 feet off Execution Rocks at noon, completely uninterested in anything. Dawn and dusk are your windows right now. First light on the outgoing at Stepping Stones has been the most reliable — that light has been a bass highway since I started fishing this water thirty years ago, and July doesn't change that, it just shrinks the clock. Same story at Throgs Neck: the rip that forms off the bridge piers on a strong ebb is still throwing up bass in the 20s and low 30s, but you've got maybe ninety minutes of prime water before the sun gets high and the fish drop into the channel.

Fluke have been the more dependable citizen this month. Drifting white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails through the Throgs Neck rip in 30 to 40 feet of water on the last two hours of the outgoing has been putting keepers in the boat consistently, with a few pushing 5 and 6 pounds mixed into a lot of shorts. If the current's really moving, I'll switch to a chartreuse bucktail with a squid strip trailer to give it a little more profile in the faster water. Manhasset Bay's channel edges have also been holding fluke on the incoming — nothing spectacular, but a solid two or three keepers if you work the drift right and stay patient with your bounce.

Bluefish have been the wild card, and when they show up on those Matinecock bunker schools, they show up angry. I watched a blitz two Saturdays back that turned the surface white for about twenty minutes — cocktail blues mostly, a few pushing 8 pounds, absolutely shredding peanut bunker on the surface. If you see the birds working and the water nervous off the point, don't waste time — throw a metal, a Kastmaster or a Hopkins, and hang on. It doesn't last long but it's worth the run.

Porgies are doing what porgies do around the rockpiles at Execution Rocks and off Hart Island — steady, unglamorous, good eating. Sandworms and clam on a hi-lo rig in 20 to 25 feet is still the play, and if you want a mixed bag day, anchor up on one of those humps on a slow tide and you'll pick at porgies while waiting for a bass to wander through.

Weakfish I'll be honest with you about — they're around, but scattered and inconsistent. I've had a few nice ones, 4 to 5 pounds, come out of the mud bottom in Little Neck Bay on soft plastics fished slow near dusk, but it's not the reliable fishery it was fifteen years ago. Don't build a trip around them, but don't be shocked if one shows up either.

Looking at the week ahead, that thermal split between the western and eastern Sound isn't going anywhere soon, which means the bait — and the big bass under it — should keep holding tight to our side. If the wind stays light out of the southwest like it has been, I'd fish the first hour of light on the outgoing at Execution Rocks or Stepping Stones, live-line a bunker, and be patient. If it's blowing and dirty, shift over to the Throgs Neck rip and grind it out with bucktails on the fluke drift. Either way, get out early — this pattern rewards guys willing to be on the water before the coffee at the diner gets cold.
