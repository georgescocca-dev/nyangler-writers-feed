# Squid Showing on the Ledges, Tog Tucked in the Cracks at Fort Wetherill
*Fluke slid out to the reefs right on schedule, bass have gone nocturnal in the warm water, and the first squid of the summer are working the rocks from Jamestown to Sakonnet.*

**JAMESTOWN, RI — July 20** — _by Billy Tanaka, Zone Writer_

First thing I noticed hopping down onto the ledges below Fort Wetherill this week wasn't a fish — it was squid. Small ones, an inch or two, working the wash right up against the rock in the last hour of daylight. That's early for us. Usually that shows mid-August when the water settles into that late-summer green. Squid pushing into the South Shore this soon tells me the whole food chain out here is running a few weeks ahead, and it's worth paying attention to because everything from scup to stripers keys on that bait once it commits to the rocks.

Tog fishing has been the most dependable thing going, and it's a slack-water game right now. I've been fishing the ledges off Fort Wetherill and the rock piles below Beavertail in 18 to 25 feet, green crabs on a standard knocker rig, and timing it so I'm dropping right through the last hour of the ebb into the dead slack. Current ripping through those cracks pulls your bait right out of the strike zone — tog want it sitting still against the structure, not swinging. Same story up at Sakonnet Point, working the boulder field off the point itself on the turn of the tide. Fish have been mixed sizes, plenty of teasers in the 12-14 inch range picking crabs apart, but enough keepers in the 16-20 inch class coming off the deeper cracks to make the trip worthwhile. With the neap tide coming in behind the July 21 first quarter, current's going to slack off across the board for the next several days — that's actually good news for tog guys, because it stretches that workable slack window out longer instead of cramming it into twenty minutes.

Striped bass have gone almost entirely nocturnal on this stretch, which tracks with how warm the water's been running everywhere south of the Cape. Daytime, the bite off the Newport Bridge rips and the east passage off Jamestown has been dead slow — a few short fish and that's it. But drop an eel or swim a Sebile Magic Swimmer through the rocks off Sakonnet Point or along the Second Beach reef line after dark, and you're in a different game. Best window has been the two hours around the bottom of the tide into the first push of the flood, fishing tight to structure, not out in open water. Nothing giant yet — mostly 26 to 32 inch fish — but they're eating, and eating consistently enough that a few guys have made it a regular after-dinner routine instead of a weekend event.

Fluke is where the real story is. We said a week back that back-bay fluke would push out toward the inlet mouths and ocean structure as the water warmed, and that's exactly what's happened. The bite inside Narragansett Bay proper has gone quiet, but out on the reef structure off Sakonnet Point and along the edges of Block Island Sound, it's been genuinely good — not blow-up numbers, but quality. Fish to 5 pounds coming on 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the last of the incoming, right where the sand meets the rock edge. The pattern's been consistent enough that if fluke's your target this week, I'd skip the bay entirely and run straight to structure.

Bluefish have been showing in patches wherever bunker pods push along the beach — Second Beach in Middletown had a solid blitz for about forty minutes one morning last week, blues busting bait right in the wash, choppers to maybe 4 pounds hitting anything metal you threw at them. Matunuck's had similar flashes. It's not a sure thing — bunker schools move, and when they slide offshore or up into the bay the blues go with them — but if you see gulls working tight over bait close to the beach, that's your window, and it doesn't last long.

No albies yet on this stretch, and I want to be straight about that rather than sell you something that isn't here. There's early scouting activity building around Montauk and out toward Buzzards Bay and the Vineyard, which is usually the tell that our push is coming — Sakonnet Point and the mouth of the Sakonnet River are typically the first water in Rhode Island to see them once that happens, ahead of Newport and well ahead of Watch Hill and Napatree. I'd watch for the first false albacore showing at Sakonnet in the next two to three weeks, not this week.

Looking ahead, the softer currents from this neap tide should widen the tog window and make the breachway structure a little more approachable for slower presentations generally. Weather's stable, no fronts pushing through, which keeps the bunker pods predictable and the bluefish blitzes possible along the same beaches. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd spend the morning slack on the Fort Wetherill ledges for tog, then come back after dark and swim an eel through the Sakonnet rocks. That's the pattern that's actually producing right now — not the fastest week we've had, but an honest one.
