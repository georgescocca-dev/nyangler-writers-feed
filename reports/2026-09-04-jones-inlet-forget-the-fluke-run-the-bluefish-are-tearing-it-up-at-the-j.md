# Forget the Fluke Run — the Bluefish Are Tearing It Up at the Jones Rocks
*While the fleet chases scraps in Sloop Channel, the real action is bunker getting squeezed at the inlet mouth on the outgoing tide.*

**JONES BEACH, NY — September 4** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Every boat still limping out to Sloop Channel this week chasing one more fluke is burning gas on a bite that's already halfway out the door. I get it — muscle memory, everybody wants that last doormat before the season turns — but the fluke have been sliding out of the back bays and inlets toward deeper ocean structure for two weeks now, and the numbers out there are thinning fast. If that's all you're fishing right now, you're fishing last month's pattern. The real story this week is happening thirty yards off the jetty rocks, where the bunker schools are stacking up at the inlet mouth and the bluefish are cutting them to ribbons on every outgoing tide.

Here's the setup. Offshore, the ocean's been sloppy — better than three-foot swell on a short five-second period pushing in against the beach, water out there running in the low 70s. That kind of chop doesn't do surfcasters any favors on the open beachfront, but it does something else that matters: it pushes bait and bass off the exposed sand and packs them into the inlet channels and jetty structure where the water stays organized. Jones Inlet, Short Beach, the rocks off the Coast Guard station — that's where the bait is getting funneled and squeezed, and where the bites are actually happening. We flagged this exact pattern last week — said bluefish would keep being the most consistent producer through the weekend while bass activity built behind the bunker — and that's precisely what's playing out at the inlet mouth right now.

The bluefish bite has been the most honest thing going. Peanut bunker are showing thick along the beachfront and getting bottled up against the jetty rocks on the last two hours of the outgoing, and the blues are hitting them so hard the water looks like it's boiling. Guys working the north side of the west jetty at Short Beach with metal — Deadly Dicks, 2-ounce Ava jigs, or a simple diamond jig with a tube teaser — are connecting on cocktail blues from three to six pounds, with some better fish mixed in on the deeper edge of the channel. If you'd rather chuck plugs, a white or bone Danny surface swimmer worked across the current seam right where the rip meets the calmer water inside the jetty has been drawing crushing strikes at first light and again right at dusk. Don't overthink the retrieve — these fish are eating anything that moves with urgency.

Striped bass are a different story right now, and I'll be straight with you: they're not blitzing yet. They're staged on the bait, not committed to it. Water's still holding in the low-to-mid 70s, which is warm enough to keep bass scattered and lazy rather than balled up and aggressive. You're seeing them show — a boil here, a swirl there, a fish or two mixed into the bluefish chaos at the jetty — but not the sustained surface mayhem everybody's waiting for. The building moon toward full around September 6th and 7th should start tightening that bait up, and once tidal flow strengthens behind it, I'd expect the first legitimate blitz activity to break loose sometime in the next week to ten days. That's not a guess pulled out of thin air — that's the same bunker-density trigger that's been building down the whole south shore, and Jones Inlet sits right in the middle of it.

Until that switch flips, the better bass have been coming off the bottom on bait rather than up top on top water. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig in the deeper cuts along the Wantagh Bridge pilings, fished tight to structure on the last of the ebb, have produced some quality fish in the high 20s to low 30s — nothing epic, but solid, honest bass if you're willing to sit on it through a slow tide change. Live eels drifted along the edge of the inlet channel on the incoming have also been worth a mention from a couple of the regulars working the rocks at night.

Don't sleep on the weakfish, either. They've been making a real comeback in South Oyster Bay and the deeper holes off Freeport, and dusk has been the window — light jigheads with a 4-inch chartreuse or pink soft plastic worked slow along the bottom in eight to twelve feet of water on the last two hours of the outgoing tide. Nothing to build a whole trip around yet, but enough fish coming over the rail that it's worth a rod in the spread if you're anchored up back there anyway.

Looking ahead, the next few days should mirror what we've already got — bluefish remaining the most dependable target at the inlet and along the beachfront wherever bunker are getting pinched, with bass activity slowly building underneath it as that moon fills out. If a real cool front finally shows up behind it — and this is early September, so it's not far off — that's when I'd expect this whole thing to tip over into the first real blitz of the fall. Until then, work the outgoing at the jetty, keep your metal in the water, and don't waste your whole weekend looking for doormats that already left the building.
