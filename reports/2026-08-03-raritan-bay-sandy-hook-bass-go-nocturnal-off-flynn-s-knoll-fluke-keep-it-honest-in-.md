# Bass go nocturnal off Flynn's Knoll — fluke keep it honest in the channels
*With bay water pushing into the mid-70s, I moved my whole program to last light and let the fluke bite carry the daylight hours.*

**PERTH AMBOY, NJ — August 3** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday to stop fishing the flats until the sun's off the water, and that decision's paying the bills right now. Keyport Flats and the mouth of the Navesink are sitting warm — I'm talking bathwater by 2pm — and the bass know it. They've slid off the shallow structure and dropped onto the channel edges and humps out toward Flynn's Knoll and Romer Shoal, and they're not coming up to feed until the light backs off. So that's where I'm putting my evenings: anchored or drifting slow right on the lip of the channel drop, soaking chunk bunker on a fish-finder rig, 3/0 circle hook, just enough weight to hold bottom in the current. Last two trips out, the bite didn't start until almost dark, but once it went, it went — 28 to 34-inch fish, a few overslot mixed in, all in that last hour of light through the first couple hours of full dark on the incoming.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the bunker-driven activity would hold through the start of August, and it has. The bunker schools are still balled up thick off Keyport and pushing down toward the Highlands, and as long as that bait stays, the bass will keep working it after dark instead of chasing it in the heat of the day. The tell to watch for is if those bunker pods start thinning out or scattering — that's usually the first sign the whole pattern's about to flip and you'll need to hunt instead of just showing up where the bait is.

During daylight I'm not wasting time trying to force stripers that don't want to eat — I'm running the channel edges for fluke instead, and that bite has been the most honest thing going in the bay all summer. Structure edges along the Reach — the drop where the sand meets the channel mud off Middle Ground and down through the Chapel Hill Channel — are holding keeper-size fish mixed with plenty of shorts, which is exactly the steady, grind-it-out pattern we called last week, not a blitz, but dependable. White and chartreuse 5-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged dead slow on the drift, tipped with a strip of squid if the current's really moving. Depth's been 18 to 28 feet, best on the last two hours of the outgoing when the water's still got some push to it but isn't ripping. I had a stretch Sunday morning where three keepers came in twenty minutes off one short drift, then it went quiet for an hour — that's fluke fishing in August, you put your time in and take what the tide gives you.

Bluefish are the loudest thing happening right now, and they're building fast. That warm surface push that's been stacking blues up and down the Jersey coast is spilling right into the Bight and through the Hook, and I'm seeing bigger fish mixing in with the schoolies at the Rip. If you're out there on the last of the incoming, throw a diamond jig with a tube teaser or a bunker spoon right through the rip line where the current stacks bait against the edge of the shoal — you'll know it's happening when the birds start working tight and the water starts boiling. It's not a subtle bite. I lost a whole bucktail leader to a bluefish that had no business being that thick this early in August, so if you're targeting fluke in the same water, bring extra leader material or you'll be retying all afternoon.

Porgies are stacked up on the reef structure around Romer Shoal and holding tight to the rock piles — small hooks, sandworms or clam on a high-low rig, bottom bounced right on the structure. It's not glamorous fishing but it's steady meat in the cooler if that's what you're after, and it's a good option on a day when the wind's got the open bay chopped up and you want to stay tucked closer to the Hook.

Weakfish have been a ghost this summer — a few incidental fish coming up mixed in with the fluke drift, nothing you can target with confidence, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

Looking ahead, the tides are starting to build again as we push toward the new moon, and that extra current should sharpen up both bites — the bass program at last light and the fluke drift during the day. If the bunker stay put off Keyport, I'd keep working the same evening pattern through the weekend: get to the channel edge by Flynn's Knoll with an hour of daylight left, soak chunks through dark on the incoming, and don't be in a hurry to leave once it gets good. Daytime, stick with the fluke on the channel structure and treat any bluefish blitz at the Hook as a bonus, not a distraction — but watch your leaders. If a front pushes through and knocks the surface temp down even a couple degrees, I'd expect the bass to come back up shallower and feed earlier, and that's the day I'll be back on the flats instead of the channel edge.
