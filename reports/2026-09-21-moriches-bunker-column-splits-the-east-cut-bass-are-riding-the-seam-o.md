# Bunker Column Splits the East Cut — Bass Are Riding the Seam Off Cupsogue
*Harvest Moon tides are stacking bait against the inlet's new sandbar, and the surface bite is starting to show it.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — September 21** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The east cut off Cupsogue has been quietly rebuilding all month, and this week it finally did something worth talking about — it split a bunker school clean in half on the outgoing tide. That's the kind of detail that tells you more than any temperature reading. The sandbar that formed off the east jetty after the spring nor'easters has grown enough shoulder to actually redirect bait, pushing one push of menhaden into the main channel and peeling a second pod off into the shallower flat toward Pikes Beach. Predators have figured that out fast. Stripers are working both lanes, but the heavier fish — the mid-30s to upper-40-inch class — are holding on the channel side where the current runs harder and the bait gets pinched tighter against structure.

The Harvest Moon tides are doing exactly what we said they'd do two weeks back — we flagged this pattern holding steady into this stretch, and the bigger swings this week accelerated it right on schedule. Bigger water movement means bait gets flushed through the inlet faster and concentrated harder at the pinch points: the jetty rocks at Cupsogue, the drop-off at the Coast Guard station cut, and the current seam that forms off Tuthill Point on a strong ebb. That seam has been the most consistent piece of structure in the system this week — bass are sitting just off the edge of it, letting the moving water deliver bait instead of chasing it down.

Bluefish have piled onto this same bait movement, and they're not subtle about it. Cocktail-to-medium blues, 3 to 6 pounds, are running the surface any time bunker gets pushed tight against the jetty rocks on the last two hours of the ebb. The blitzes aren't constant — more like 20-to-30-minute windows that flare up and shut down as the bait ducks into the channel or gets pushed out past the bar — but when they go off, they go off hard enough to empty a bucktail bag fast. Poppers and metal — Kastmasters, Ava jigs in white — are getting hit on the first cast into a boil and ignored ten minutes later once the fish move.

Striper action inside the bay itself has been more workmanlike than explosive. The mud flats off East Moriches and the deeper holes around Senix Creek are holding fish that slid off the bunker schools once they got full, and those are eating better on presentation than on speed. Guys working bucktails — 1 to 1.5 ounce bucktails in white or chartreuse, tipped with a strip of fresh bunker — dragged slow along bottom on the last of the outgoing have done better than anyone throwing plugs blind. The take is usually subtle, more of a heavy pickup than a slam, which tells you these fish are eating leftovers, not chasing.

Out at the inlet mouth itself, the deeper water on the ocean side of the bar — that 8-to-12-foot cut that runs parallel to the beach off Cupsogue — has been the most reliable window for bigger bass. First light and the last hour of the ebb are producing best, with fish holding tight to the drop where the bar falls into that channel. Live bunker on a fish-finder rig, or a fresh chunk fished right on bottom, is out-producing artificials there simply because the current is moving too much water to keep a bucktail in the strike zone long enough.

Fluke have essentially wrapped up their season in the bay proper — this isn't news, it's just where we are on the calendar. A few stragglers are still coming off the deeper holes near the Route 27 bridge on Gulp in white or pink, but the effort-to-reward ratio has flipped hard toward the bass and blues. Weakfish have shown flickers of activity on the same current seams as the bass, mostly at dusk, but numbers remain thin enough that nobody's targeting them specifically. Porgies are still biting steady around the rock piles inside the inlet and off Smith Point, small stuff mostly, and blackfish season hasn't turned on yet — worth noting for anyone planning ahead, but not yet worth a dedicated trip.

Water clarity has stayed reasonable through the week — not gin clear, but clean enough that the bass aren't shy about working shallow on the flood when bait pushes up into the marsh creeks. Wind has been the bigger variable than temperature; the days with a light southwest breeze under 10 knots have fished dramatically better than the days with anything harder out of the east, which just muddies the inlet mouth and pushes the bait schools deeper than the current can move them efficiently.

Looking at the next few days, the bigger moon tides aren't done working yet. If the wind stays manageable through the weekend, expect the bait volume moving through the inlet to keep building, which should sharpen the blitz windows at Cupsogue and extend the workable bass window along that ocean-side cut. The smart play is timing effort to the tide change rather than the clock — the last two hours of the ebb has been the most productive window all week, both for the blitz-style blues-and-bass action on the surface and for the bigger fish holding deep on bunker chunks. If a cold front does slide through late in the week, expect the bite to compress rather than disappear — bait and bass alike will tighten up around the inlet structure instead of spreading through the open bay, which honestly makes them easier to find, if not always easier to catch.
