# Appledore ledges holding cod to 6 pounds, bass stacked on Piscataqua mackerel
*Thirteen miles of coast, but the ledges off the Shoals and the current lines on the river are still doing all the work.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 27** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

Thirteen miles of coastline sounds like nothing until you've spent enough dawns idling out past Whaleback with a chart of the Shoals memorized cold. Guys drive two hours to fish structure that's a twenty-minute run from Rye Harbor State Marina. The water off Appledore and White Island doesn't care how big your bay is — it holds cod, it holds bass, and this week it held both, which is more than most of the mid-Atlantic can say right now.

The pattern that's been running since mid-August hasn't budged. Surface water along the coast is sitting in that comfortable, slightly-too-warm-for-daytime-aggression range that pushes stripers and blues onto structure and into the current lines rather than roaming open water. High pressure has held, winds have stayed out of the southwest in the 8-12 knot range most days, and that's kept the ocean off the Shoals fishable — not glassy, but workable, two-to-three-foot swell with a long enough period that a 20-foot boat can sit on the ledges without getting knocked around. We flagged this pattern last week, said mackerel schools close to shore would keep driving the predator activity, and that's exactly what's held. The bait is thick enough around the Shoals and off Great Boars Head that nothing has needed to travel far to eat.

On the groundfish side, the Appledore ledges and the drop east of Star Island have been the honest producers. Depths in the 45 to 65-foot range, fished right on the slack or the first hour of the new tide, have given up cod in the 4 to 6-pound class with a few better fish pushing 8. Diamond jigs — the 4 and 6-ounce sizes — tipped with a strip of squid or fished bare with a fast lift-and-drop have outfished bait rigs this week, which tells you the fish are chasing, not scavenging. Haddock have been mixed in but thinner than a July report would show; the ones coming over the rail are running 18 to 20 inches, mostly on the same jigs worked a little slower off bottom. If you're marking bait balls on the sounder around 50 feet and the current's easing off, that's the window — don't waste time on max flood or max ebb out there, the drift gets unmanageable and the fish stop feeding as hard anyway.

Stripers have been the better story inshore, and the Piscataqua River is the reason. Mackerel pushing up through Portsmouth Harbor on the last two hours of the flood have pulled bass right up into the current seams below the Memorial Bridge and around the moorings off Peirce Island. Live mackerel on a fish-finder rig, or a 3-ounce bucktail with a chunk of mackerel belly dressed on the hook, worked slow along bottom in 15 to 25 feet, has been the move. Fish in the 28 to 34-inch range are common there right now, with the occasional overslot showing on the harder tide changes. Hampton Harbor has been more of a dusk program — the inlet by the Route 1A bridge stacks bait on the outgoing, and bass have been sliding through there in the last two hours of ebb chasing peanut bunker and silversides, best worked with white or chartreuse soft-plastic swim shads on light jig heads so they stay in the strike zone without snagging bottom.

Bluefish have done exactly what bluefish do this time of year — shown up everywhere and annoyed everyone trying to fish for something else. Snapper blues are thick in Rye and Hampton Harbors, small enough to be more nuisance than target, but cocktail blues in the 2 to 4-pound range have been mixing into the striper bite off Great Boars Head and around the outer edge of the Shoals, hitting the same mackerel-imitating baits meant for bass. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod for a kid on the boat, a small silver Kastmaster worked fast through a surface blitz off the Boars Head rocks will get eaten every time right now.

Flounder fishing has stayed quiet but steady for the handful of guys still bothering with it — Rye Harbor and the mud flats inside Portsmouth Harbor near the Route 1B causeway have given up winter flounder and a few blackback keepers on bloodworms and clam bellies, fished on a simple bottom rig in 10 to 15 feet on the last of the ebb when the current slows enough for a bait to sit still.

Looking at the next several days, expect more of the same rather than a turn. The moon's building toward full, which should sharpen the tide swings and tighten up the bite windows at the river mouths and around the Shoals ledges — instead of a long lazy bite you'll get a harder, shorter push right around the tide change, so timing matters more than usual. Southwest wind in the 8 to 12-knot range looks likely to hold, keeping the run out to Appledore and White Island manageable most mornings. The real shift — the one that sends bass moving with more urgency and cools that surface layer enough to open up daytime activity closer to the beach — is still probably two to three weeks out, once overnight temps start dropping consistently into September. Until then, the smart money stays on the same program: ledges on the slack for cod and haddock, river current seams on the mackerel-fed tides for bass, and the harbors at last light for anything willing to chase a blitz.
