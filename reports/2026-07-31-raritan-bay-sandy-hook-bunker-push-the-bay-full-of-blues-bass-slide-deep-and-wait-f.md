# Bunker Push the Bay Full of Blues, Bass Slide Deep and Wait for Dark
*With Raritan Bay running warm and bunker schools thick from Keyport to the Hook, blues own the daylight and stripers only show once the sun's down.*

**PERTH AMBOY, NJ — July 31** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

Here's the call I made Tuesday morning standing on the bulkhead in Perth Amboy watching bunker pods roll past thick as a carpet: mornings on the flats for fluke, evenings working the bunker schools for blues, and save the bass program for full dark. That's the split right now, and if you try to force stripers during the day in this water temp, you're wasting bait.

We flagged this last week — said the bunker-bluefish push coming up from the south would hit this zone hard by the weekend, and it did. The bay's holding bunker in numbers I haven't seen stacked this heavy since early June, rolling thick off Keyport, working up past Flynn's Knoll, and pushing into the mouth of the Raritan. Where the bunker go, the blues follow, and right now they're everywhere in that school — snapping at the edges, blowing up on top mid-morning off Romer Shoal, and again in the last hour of daylight along the Hook side of the channel. Nothing subtle about it. If you see gulls diving and mullet-colored water churning white, put the boat on it and throw metal — half-ounce to one-ounce Deadly Dicks, Ava jigs with a green tube, anything that flashes through that ball fast. You don't need finesse for these blues, you need to get a bait in the strike zone before the school moves off, because they're covering ground fast chasing bunker pushed by the tide.

Stripers are a different animal right now and this is the part guys keep getting wrong. Bay surface temps have been sitting in that mid-70s range, pushing toward 80 in the shallower flats by afternoon, and that's above where bass want to be. They're not gone — they're just smart. They're sitting deep, either suspended under the bunker schools or holed up on structure — the wrecks off Romer Shoal, the deeper cuts through the Chapel Hill Channel, the drop along the edge of Flynn's Knoll where the bottom falls from eight feet to eighteen. During the day those fish are basically shut down. Wait for the last hour of light and through the night and it flips completely. I've had my best bass action this week drifting bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig, 2-3 ounces of lead depending on current, right on the bottom edge of that Flynn's Knoll drop starting an hour before dark and running through the first part of the incoming tide. Fresh bunker beats frozen right now — cut a fresh one you snagged out of the school an hour earlier and it's a different bite entirely. Eels fished the same way, slow-trolled or drifted along the Highlands side of the channel after dark, have also been putting bigger fish in the boat — nothing huge, mostly mid-20s to low 30-inch fish, but they're there if you commit to the night program.

Fluke are the honest, dependable piece of this whole puzzle and they haven't cared one bit about the warm surface water because they're living on the bottom where it's cooler. The Keyport Flats have been steady — not blitz numbers, but a solid keeper mixed in with shorts on almost every drift if you're covering the channel edges right. I've been working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, drifting the drop-offs where the flats fall into the channel in 12 to 20 feet. The Chapel Hill Channel edge and the deeper water off Great Beds Light have both given up keepers in the 18 to 22-inch range on the outgoing tide, when current pulls bait off the flats and concentrates it right on that edge. Slow drift, bounce bottom, don't overwork it — these fish are eating, they're just not chasing far.

The Navesink hasn't been anything special this week — warm, slow-moving water, and I'd rather burn gas running to the Hook side than waste a tide up there right now. If you want a change of pace, weakfish have shown in small numbers mixed with the bluefish schools on bucktails tipped with squid strips, fished slow near bottom on the edges of the bunker pods — nothing to build a trip around, but a nice surprise if one grabs.

Looking ahead, I don't expect this pattern to break unless we get a real shift in weather. The tides are easing off the full moon push toward neap over the next several days, which should slow the current a touch and might let those bunker schools spread out rather than staying balled tight — if that happens, the blitz-style blue fishing could thin out even as individual blues stay around. Bass will stay married to the night program as long as this warm stretch holds; the only thing that changes that is a real cool-down or a string of northeast wind stirring the bottom. If I had one evening this week, I'd be on the bunker schools off Flynn's Knoll right at last light, working blues on top until the sun's down, then switching to chunk bait on bottom for an hour of dark before calling it. That's where the bay's telling me to be right now.
