# A Fresh Cut South of Holgate Is Rewriting the Night Bite
*New structure from last week's south swell is holding stripers after dark while doormat fluke stack the inlet edges on the drop.*

**SHIP BOTTOM, NJ — July 17** — _by Tommy Nguyen, Zone Writer_

I walked the Holgate stretch at dead low Tuesday morning with a cup of dock coffee going cold in my hand, and the beach had changed shape on me again. There's a new cut about three hundred yards south of the old Rodanthe-style break — a legit trough that wasn't there two weeks ago, carved out by the south groundswell that's been rolling through at fifteen-second periods. It's maybe forty yards wide, drops to four or five feet at the bar, and the inside edge has that dark green color that tells you there's a bait highway running through it. I marked it on my phone, walked it twice, and told the guys at the shop before I even had my second cup — this is the spot for the next few weeks, until the next blow reshuffles the deck.

Here's the thing about mid-July on this island: the ocean side is running warm — buoy data off the Bight has water pushing 73 degrees — and that's pushed the bulk of the keeper-class bass into a strict after-dark program. Daytime you might see a swirl or two around the jetty rocks at Barnegat Light on the last of the outgoing, but the real work is happening from an hour before high tide through the top of the flood, once the sun's down and the water in these new cuts cools a couple degrees. I've been throwing a black Danny-style swimmer and a 3-ounce darter through that Holgate cut on the incoming, working it slow across the trough, and connecting with stripers mostly in the upper 20s to low 30-inch class — a few slot fish mixed in, nothing giant, but consistent enough that I've made three trips down there this week and come home with fish twice. That's a decent ratio for July surf fishing, but it's not a gimme — you need the tide right and you need to be fishing structure, not just chucking plugs at open beach.

Barnegat Inlet itself has been the fluke story. The channel edges on the north side, that stretch running from the Coast Guard station drift down toward the rip, are holding doormat-class fish in 25 to 35 feet. I've had my best luck dragging a 3/4-ounce white bucktail tipped with a 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet, bounced slow along bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing when the current really starts ripping bait off the flats and funneling it through the cut. Squid strips on the hook point add scent when the water's got any color to it after a blow. The bigger fish — I had one push six pounds Thursday — are coming off the deeper structure where the bottom breaks hard, not the flats themselves. Little Egg Inlet is fishing similarly, though it's been more of a numbers game than a big-fish game this week; lots of 16 to 18-inch fish mixed with the occasional keeper on the same bucktail-and-Gulp combo, drifted from the Holgate side across toward Beach Haven Inlet.

Great Bay and the Mullica system are doing their July thing — weakfish showing up on the evening bite around Graveling Point, mostly on the last two hours of daylight into dusk. These aren't the springtime schoolies; a few honest 20-inch fish have come out of there on peeler crab fished on a fish-finder rig, dead-sticked over the mussel bottom, with a couple more falling for a small bucktail tipped with a Fin-S worked slow along the channel edge where it drops from four feet to eight. The bay water's warmed up fast in the afternoon sun the way it does this time of year — that shallow mud and grass bottom heats quicker than the open inlet, and it's pulling bait and predators into tighter windows right around the tide change. If you're fishing Graveling, I'd rather be there on the last of the outgoing when that warm water's draining out of the marsh creeks and concentrating everything on the current seams, than sitting there at high water with nothing moving.

Kingfish have been steady but not thick on the ocean beaches — Ship Bottom and Beach Haven have both given up decent counts on bloodworm and Fishbites rigs fished in the troughs at low tide, small sinkers, light leaders, nothing fancy. It's a good bet if you want steady action for the kids or you're just killing time waiting for the evening striper bite to turn on.

Bluefish have been the wild card. Bunker schools have been moving up and down the beachfront from Barnegat Light down past Ship Bottom, and when the blues find them it's fast and violent for twenty minutes and then gone — topwater poppers and metal-lip swimmers thrown into the melee will get bit, but you need to be in the right spot at the right time, and that's mostly a function of watching for diving birds working bait balls just outside the surf line.

Looking ahead, that new Holgate cut is the thing I'm watching closest. If the south swell holds through this weekend, I expect it to hold its shape and keep producing on the night flood. If a bigger blow comes through and reshapes it again, I'll be back out there at first light re-reading the beach — that's the job. For the inlet, I'd keep working the outgoing on Barnegat and Little Egg as long as the fluke keep showing on the drop, and I'd have a bucktail rigged and ready the moment those bunker schools push back into range.
