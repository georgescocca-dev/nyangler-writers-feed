# New moon springs load up Shinnecock Inlet as bay fluke push out to cooler water
*Bigger outgoing tides and a 71-degree ocean seam are stacking bait at the jetty — but the weakfish still haven't shown at Ponquogue.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — July 10** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay ran warm and slow. Wind sat out of the south for four straight days, which is never my favorite setup — it pushes a chop against the outgoing current at the inlet and muddies up what should be a clean drain. Shinnecock Bay itself climbed into the high 70s in the shallows off Tiana and up toward the canal, and that heat did exactly what warm bay water always does this time of year: it pushed the fluke out. I was seeing bait get nervous and scatter off the flats by mid-week, and by Thursday the real activity had shifted hard toward the inlet channel and the ocean side, where upwelled water is running a good seven degrees cooler than the bay. That kind of gap — call it 71 on the ocean side versus 78 back in the shallows — is a magnet. Fluke don't like to work that hard to stay comfortable, and neither do the bass.

This week the moon's doing the heavy lifting. We're building toward new moon, and that means bigger tides, more water moving, more current ripping through the inlet on the outgoing. That's my kind of week. The stronger the drain, the more bait gets flushed out of the bay and funneled past the jetty rocks, and the stripers know it. Peak spring tide alignment lands right around this weekend, so I'd have my night trips penciled in for Friday through Monday when the outgoing really loads up. Wind looks like it's backing more westerly by the weekend too, which should clean up that inlet water after a week of chop. If that holds, the combination of maximum flow and clean water is about as good as this spot gets in July.

Here's what's actually been happening. Striped bass fishing at the inlet on the night outgoing has been the most consistent thing on my log this week — not a blowup bite, but steady. I've been working the north jetty rocks and the deeper scour hole on the inside bend, live eels and chunk bunker on a fish-finder rig, weighted just enough to hold bottom in that current without getting swept into the rocks. Best window has been the two hours either side of the tide really getting up to speed, an hour or so after the turn. Fish have mostly been schoolies to mid-20s, with a few better ones in the 30-inch class coming off bunker chunks fished tight to the base of the jetty where the eddy forms. Nothing that's going to make headlines, but it's a bite you can set your watch to if you're willing to be out there at 11 p.m. instead of noon.

Fluke have followed the cool water out to the inlet and the ocean side rips, same story I mentioned above. The drift along the edge of the inlet channel, working the deeper cuts in 15 to 25 feet on the last of the outgoing, has been giving up decent numbers on white Gulp Swimming Mullets rigged on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow right along bottom. A few boats working just outside the inlet on the ocean side, dropping into that 71-degree seam, have done better than the bay boats — fish to 4 and 5 pounds mixed in with a lot of shorts, so it's a pick-through-the-count kind of bite rather than a limit-in-an-hour situation.

Now the honest part. Weakfish at Ponquogue Bridge have been quiet. The water's plenty warm enough — that threshold's been crossed for weeks now — but warm water alone isn't pulling them up under the lights like it used to. I've made four trips to the bridge on the night tide this month and only put two weakfish in the boat, both small. I'm not writing them off, but I'd stop treating warm water as a promise. If you're going to fish for them, do it on the slower tide stages under the bridge lights with bucktails tipped in squid strips, but go in with your expectations calibrated — this isn't the bridge bite of ten years ago.

On the bonus side, this is about the last stretch of a window I've had my eye on — early-season bonito and Spanish mackerel have been showing on the ocean side near the inlet mouth on clean incoming tides, the kind of fish you stumble into while working small metal for blues. That pattern's closing out over the next few days, so if you want a shot at one, fish the incoming near the outer rocks with a Deadly Dick or similar and keep it moving fast.

Looking ahead, I'm building my whole week around that new moon spring tide. If the wind actually swings west like it's supposed to, Friday through Monday night should give the cleanest, strongest outgoing water we've had in weeks — that's when I'd be at the jetty with an eel in the water. Plan B if the bass don't cooperate: shift out to the ocean-side fluke drift where that cold seam is holding. Between the two, there's enough happening to make a night on the water worth it, even if the weakfish stay a no-show a little longer.
