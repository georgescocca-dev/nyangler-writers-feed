# White Island Rockpile on the Slack: Cod and Haddock Mixed, Stripers Working the Piscataqua Seam
*Thirteen miles of coastline doesn't mean thirteen miles of options — it means you'd better know every rock, and this week the Shoals paid for that knowledge.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 8** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People hear thirteen miles of coastline and figure I'm running out of places to fish by Wednesday. I've got more water inside that thirteen miles than most guys work in a season. The Shoals alone are nine islands and about as many distinct pieces of bottom, and I fish it like it's a hundred and thirty miles of Gulf of Maine, because structurally that's what it is. This week that mindset paid rent.

I was off White Island before 5 AM Tuesday, tide bottoming out around 5:40, and I sat on the rockpile on the northeast corner in about 70 feet waiting for the slack to set up clean. That first twenty minutes of dead water is when the cod come off the structure and feed loose instead of holding tight in the current — and it showed. Jigging diamond jigs, the 4-ounce chrome ones, tipped with a strip of mackerel belly, and I had cod to 8 pounds and a couple of haddock in the mix, which is the part worth mentioning. Haddock have been thin around the Shoals all summer — this was the first day in a few weeks I saw more than one or two. Whether that's a real push of fish or just a good morning on a good piece of bottom, I can't say yet. One trip isn't a pattern. But if I had to bet where I'd put my next haddock trip, it's back on that same rock on the next slack tide, not blind-drifting somewhere new.

Gosport Harbor and the back side of Appledore have been quieter than I'd like for cod — water's cold and green out there the way it usually runs in August, which is normally good news for groundfish, but the fish have been scattered rather than stacked. I marked bait on the sounder in 55 feet off Duck Island Wednesday and never got more than a bump. Sometimes the mackerel schools pull the cod's attention off the bottom entirely, and that's what I think happened — the macks have been thick enough around the Shoals this week that jigging a bare Swedish Pimple with three or four hooks, no bait needed, filled a bucket in fifteen minutes some mornings. Good news if you want table fare and a break from bottom fishing. Bad news if you were counting on those cod being predictable.

Inshore, the story's at the river mouths, same as it always is this time of year. The Piscataqua is running strong current through Portsmouth Harbor with the building tide range — we flagged this last week, said the sharpening current into the new moon would turn on the structure-oriented bite, and that's exactly what's happening at Henderson Point and off the end of Pierces Island. Stripers are stacked on the seam where the river current meets the harbor eddy, especially on the last two hours of the ebb when bait gets pushed out of the back channels. I've been throwing swimming plugs — a Slug-Go rigged weightless, white, worked slow through the seam — and picking up schoolies to about 26 inches with the occasional better fish mixed in. Live mackerel on a fish-finder rig, drifted right down the current line off Fort Point, has been the better bet for the bigger bass, but you need to be anchored right, not just close.

Hampton Harbor is doing its usual August thing on the incoming tide — bass and blues pushing up through the inlet on the flood chasing bait out of the marsh, then dropping back out on the ebb. I've had decent luck working the channel edge off Great Boars Head with a bucktail, half-ounce white, bounced slow along bottom as the tide starts to push in. Bluefish have been the wildcard — quiet most of July, and now starting to show again in the harbor mouth and off Odiorne Point, chopping up schools of peanut bunker close to the rocks. Nothing gator-sized, mostly 2 to 4 pounds, but they hit hard enough on topwater poppers to wake you up at 6 AM. If that bluefish comeback keeps building the way it has the last few days south of here, I'd expect more of that action at the river mouths through the rest of August.

Flounder have been steady but unspectacular in the Piscataqua's mud channels and the back of Hampton Harbor — small hooks, sandworms or clam bellies, fished right on bottom on the slower part of the tide. Not a trip to plan a whole day around, but a solid backup if the wind kills the Shoals run.

Looking ahead: the new moon on the 12th is going to push tidal range up noticeably, and that should sharpen every current-driven spot I've mentioned — the Piscataqua seam, the Hampton inlet, the rockpiles at the Shoals where slack water timing matters. If the wind stays reasonable out of the west, I'd run out to White Island again on the next good slack and see if that haddock bump was real or a fluke. If it blows, I'm working the river mouths on the bigger tides and letting the current do the work for me. Either way, thirteen miles is plenty if you know which rocks to sit on.
