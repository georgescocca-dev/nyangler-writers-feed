# Weakfish crash Fire Island surf as bay water hits 68 degrees
*Trophy weakfish and keeper fluke stack up as thermal gradient pulls bait through the inlet.*

**CAPTREE, NY — June 24** — _by Mariana "Mari" Reyes, Zone Writer_

The water's telling a story this week, and it's one I've been waiting all season to hear. Bay temperatures spiked to 68 degrees while the ocean side sits at 64 — that four-degree gradient is like a magnet for bait, and everything with fins is taking notice.

The big news came from the Fire Island Inlet surf, where a 26-inch, 7.3-pound weakfish hit a 9-inch Gravity Tackle Sand Eel. That's not just a nice fish — that's the kind of weakfish that makes you remember why we chase these ghosts in the first place. The sand eel pattern is key right now. These weakfish are staging in the inlet wash, ambushing bait as it gets swept out on the ebb. Fish the last two hours of the outgoing, work those 9-inch sand eels slow along the bottom, and be ready for that telltale head shake.

The Captree fleet is cashing in on keeper fluke in the ocean. The Laura Lee boats are finding them shallow, working 20 to 30 feet along the beach drops. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails are the ticket — drag them slow on the drift, let the current do the work. The Robert Moses bridge pilings are holding fish on the outgoing tide, especially around the deeper holes where the current carves out the bottom.

What's driving this action is that thermal layer. The bay water hitting 68 degrees is pulling massive amounts of bait through the inlet system. Spearing, sand eels, and juvenile bunker are getting flushed out with every tide change, and the predators know it. I'm seeing the classic Fire Island summer pattern setting up — warm bay water creating a conveyor belt of food that everything from fluke to weakfish to bass can't resist.

The party boats out of Captree are loading up on sea bass and ling. Diamond jigs in gold are producing limits, but don't overlook clams once you've got your bass. The ling are stacked up in 40 to 60 feet, and they'll hammer a fresh clam on a high-low rig. Sabiki rigs are deadly for the mixed bag — sea bass, mackerel, and porgies are all feeding in the same water column.

Tide timing is everything right now. The new moon spring tides are creating serious current flow through the inlet. Fish the last three hours of the outgoing for the best action — that's when the bait gets concentrated and the fish turn aggressive. The incoming can be productive too, but you need to position yourself where the warm bay water meets the cooler ocean flow.

Surf fishing the Fire Island beaches is picking up as bait moves along the shoreline. Early morning and late evening are prime time. Work the cuts and troughs with bucktails and soft plastics. The bass are there, but they're being selective — match the hatch with whatever bait you're seeing in the wash.

Looking ahead, this thermal gradient should hold through the week. Water temperatures are stable, and the forecast shows light winds that won't blow out the bite. The full moon on Friday means even bigger tides — I'm watching for that first real push of weakfish as the inlet drains at sunset. The conditions are lining up for what could be the best week of fishing we've seen all season. Get out there and make it count.
