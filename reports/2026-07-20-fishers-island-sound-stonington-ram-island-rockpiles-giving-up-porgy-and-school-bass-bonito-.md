# Ram Island rockpiles giving up porgy and school bass — bonito are next
*The neap has gone soft on the rips, the bass have gone nocturnal, and the water's finally in range for the first bonito of the year.*

**STONINGTON, CT — July 20** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

Poled out to Ram Island Tuesday morning on the last of the ebb and the boulders were doing what they do this time of year — holding shadow and bait in equal measure. Silversides tucked so tight against the granite you'd swim right past them if you weren't looking for the nervous water. A few terns working nothing, just checking. That's the tell. When the terns are checking instead of diving, the bait's still down in the rocks, not pushed up and panicked. Means the bass are around but not stacked yet, not blitzing. Patient water.

We're coming off the first-quarter neap now, and anybody who's fished the Race or Fishers Island Sound long enough knows what that does to this water. The current goes soft. Instead of that six-knot freight train ripping over the boulder fields at Wicopesset and the Sugar Reef humps, you get a manageable push — two, three knots at peak — and that widens the window. You don't have to time your drift to the fifteen-minute peak of the tide anymore. I had a good hour and a half of clean presentation through the Fishers Island rips Tuesday, working a black bucktail with a white curly tail down along bottom in eighteen to twenty-two feet, letting it stall and lift off the rock edges instead of ripping it through. Picked up two schoolies and lost a better fish that wrapped me around a boulder I know by name at this point.

The real bass right now are a night fish. Water's warm — New London's reading 70, and shallow water inside Little Narragansett Bay and around Mystic River mouth is running warmer than that by afternoon — so the keeper-class fish have pulled back into a dawn-dusk-and-dark pattern same as everywhere south of the Cape. If you want a shot at something over the slot, fish the last hour of light into full dark on the Stonington Breakwater, working a swimming plug or a live eel along the rock face on the outgoing. Wamphassuc Point does the same thing — current sweeps bait off that point on the ebb and the bigger fish sit right on the seam waiting. Eels fished slow, just enough weight to hold bottom, have out-produced everything else there the last two weeks.

Daytime, the fluke have been the honest bite. Channel edges off Esker Point, the flats inside Little Narragansett Bay, and the deeper cuts around Wilderness Point are holding fish in the eighteen-to-twenty-eight-foot range on the last two hours of the incoming, when the water's still moving but not screaming. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged dead slow along bottom — that combination has kept us in fish most mornings. Nothing giant, mostly seventeen to twenty inches with a scattering of keepers, but steady enough that a couple hours drifting the edges will put dinner in the cooler.

On the rock structure itself — Ram Island, the boulder fields off the Fishers Island side, the rips off North Dumpling — porgy and scup fishing has been about as good as I've seen it in years. Small hooks, squid strips or clam, right on bottom in fifteen to twenty-five feet, and you can load a bucket in an hour on the slack tide when the bass and blues aren't around to bully them off the structure. It's not glamorous but it's honest fishing, and it's the kind of thing that keeps a family in fresh fish through August.

Now here's the one I've been waiting to write. We said last week the water was getting close, and it's close. Block Island's pushing 68 and change, New London's sitting at 70, and that's the range where bonito start showing on this stretch of coast. I haven't seen one yet with my own eyes, and I want to be straight about that — no albies, no bones, nothing confirmed inshore of Fishers Island as of this week. But the temperature is there now, and the pattern from Rhode Island through Buzzards Bay says they're coming. I'd be watching the temp breaks off Napatree Point and East Point hard over the next ten days, small epoxy jigs and Deceiver-style flies ready in the boat bag, because when that first push of bonito or albies shows here it shows fast and it shows in a hurry — one morning nothing, next morning the water's exploding off Watch Hill Reef.

Look-ahead: with the current staying soft through the neap, I'd keep working the rips with patience rather than power — slower retrieves, more time on each drift. Fluke should stay consistent on the moving water as long as this clean weather holds. Bass will keep favoring the dark hours until we get a real cool-down. And I'll be out there most mornings this week with binoculars up, watching for that first sign of albie foam off the Fishers Island side. When it happens, you'll hear about it from me before anybody.
