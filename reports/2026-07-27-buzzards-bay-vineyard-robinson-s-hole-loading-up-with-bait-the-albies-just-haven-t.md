# Robinson's Hole Loading Up With Bait — The Albies Just Haven't Shown Yet
*Sand eels and silversides are stacking in the rips between Pasque and Nashawena, water's crossed the threshold, but the first false albacore of the season still hasn't punched a card.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — July 27** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

I stood on the rocks at Robinson's Hole Tuesday evening with the tide just starting to run and watched a ball of silversides get pushed into a tight knot against the Pasque side, nervous, flashing, doing that thing they do right before something eats them. Nothing hit. I watched it for twenty minutes. That's where we are right now — the table's set, the bait's here, and the guest of honor hasn't walked in yet.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the water had crossed the seasonal threshold and the building current off the full moon should be enough to trigger the first albie or bonito scouting reports within a few days. The current part is right on schedule. Tides through Robinson's and Quick's Hole are running noticeably harder than they were two weeks back, that classic mid-summer rip starting to snap on the last two hours of the ebb. But as of this week I haven't had a confirmed albie or bonito in hand or heard one from a boat I trust. Close, not there. If you're itching to be first, I get it — I am too — but don't burn a Saturday convinced they're already stacked up. They're not, not yet.

What IS happening, and happening well, is the striped bass game, just not in the way you'd want at noon. These fish have gone nocturnal on us with the inshore water sitting warm — I'm seeing surface temps in the low-to-mid 70s from Quick's Hole down through the Cuttyhunk side, and that pushes stripers deep onto structure during daylight and up onto bait only after dark. The move right now is eels and bunker chunks fished on the down-current side of the rips at Cuttyhunk and around the backside of Nashawena after sundown, into the first couple hours of the incoming. Guys working the west end of Cuttyhunk with a whole bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig, weighted just enough to hold bottom in that current, have been finding slot fish and the occasional overslot in that 15-25 foot depth range where the rip dumps into deeper water. Daytime, if you want a shot, fish the thermocline — deep bucktails, 2 to 3 ounces, dropped down along the drop-offs on the Woods Hole side where the current keeps things cooler, worked slow on the bottom third of the water column.

Bluefish have been the reliable show for anyone who wants action without waiting for dark. They're riding the same bait schools pushing through Woods Hole and along the backside of the Vineyard toward Oak Bluffs, and when they find bunker pinned against structure it turns into a proper blitz — poppers and single-hook Hopkins get bit the second they land. This matches what's been building the whole coast — the bunker-driven blitz pattern that's been marching north, and Buzzards Bay is squarely in that lane right now. If you see gulls working tight and diving hard off the Elizabeth Islands, don't wait, that window closes fast.

Fluke have softened up some in the shallower stretches of the bay as the heat's pushed them toward deeper structure — I'm hearing better numbers out of the deeper bends off Robinson's Hole and the channel edges around Quick's Hole than off the open sand flats that were producing in June. White Gulp on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the last of the outgoing, has been the ticket, with the better fish coming right at the current edge where it dumps into deeper water.

The bright spot with zero complications is bottom fishing. Scup and black sea bass have been about as steady as this zone gets — the classic structure around Middle Ground, the ledges off Menemsha, and the rock piles between Cuttyhunk and Nashawena are stacked with keeper-class sea bass and scup thick enough that a bucket limit isn't a stretch on a good drift. Squid strips or clam on a double-hook bottom rig, 2-3 ounces of lead depending on current, right on the structure. This is your Plan B while we wait on the albies — reliable, fast, and it keeps the rod bent while you scout for the real deal.

Looking ahead: the moon goes full in the next few days and that current is only going to build from here. If the pattern holds the way it usually does, I'd expect the first legitimate albie or bonito sighting somewhere in this zone within the week — Robinson's Hole and the mouth of Quick's Hole are where I'd have my eyes first, followed by the rip off Menemsha once the bait gets pushed that direction. I'll be out there checking the bait balls every evening tide change, and the second something eats one, you'll hear about it here first. Until then, fish the dark for stripers, chase the gulls for blues, and hit the bottom structure for a guaranteed bend in the rod while the real show finishes loading.
