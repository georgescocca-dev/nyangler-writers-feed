# 71.2°F at the Bight Buoy, Warmer Still on the Break — Yellowfin Working the Hudson's Edge
*A lumpy inshore signal and a stubborn warm filament are keeping the mid-September canyon bite honest — yellowfin steady, bluefin scattered, mahi and marlin riding the same structure.*

**BRIELLE, NJ — September 14** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Buoy 44025, sitting inside the 30-fathom curve in the Bight, read 71.2°F this week with seas running 3.9 feet at a 5-second period. That's a short, wind-driven chop, not a groundswell, and it tells you the sea state offshore has been more about breeze than swell — bumpy but workable for boats running the extra hour out past the 100-fathom line. More importantly, that inshore number is a proxy for what's been pushing offshore all week: warmer surface water still stacked up ahead of the first real fall cold fronts, and that's exactly what's kept the warm filament along the canyon wall intact rather than folding up and pushing out to sea.

We flagged this last week — said any shift in that filament's position was the single biggest variable to watch for this zone, and it's held its ground rather than moving, which is the best-case outcome for late-season yellowfin. The edge is still sitting where the fleet found it, running along the canyon's western wall and spilling bait up onto the shoulders near the Claw and the Dip. That stability is why the yellowfin bite has stayed the most dependable thing out there — not a blow-up bite, but a grind-it-out, know-where-to-look bite that's rewarded boats willing to run the extra few miles to stay on the warm side of the line.

Tactically, that means trolled spreads of green-and-yellow machines and small ballyhoo dredges working the temp break itself, with the better fish coming off the warm-water side rather than the cold. Boats jigging butterfly-style metal — 150 to 200 gram Shimano Orca or similar — into the top of the water column where the bait is holding have been picking off yellowfin in the 25 to 45 pound class on the drop and the first few cranks, especially when the sonar shows bait balled up tight rather than spread thin. Mahi are mixed into the same structure, coming on the troll and on chunked butterfish drifted behind the boat once a stop is made, with fish running smaller than midsummer — schoolies in the 5 to 10 pound range mostly, with the occasional bull mixed in around any floating debris or a weedline that's held together in the chop.

Bluefin have been the frustrating piece of the puzzle, and that pattern isn't new — it's held for weeks now. They're present on the same grounds as the yellowfin, showing on the machine and occasionally busting on top, but they've been slow to commit to the same trolled spreads that are drawing strikes from the yellowfin. The better play has been smaller profile baits — cedar plugs and smaller spreader bars fished further back in the pattern — or working diamond jigs and butterfly jigs deeper in the column where the bluefin seem to be holding tighter to bait than sitting up top. It's not a bite you can count on, but it's worth a rod or two in the spread if the marks are there.

White marlin have continued to respond to live-lined peanut bunker fished right along the temperature break, flat-lined off the corners of the spread rather than buried in the middle of it. That's been a consistent producer through the warm-water stretch, and with the filament holding steady rather than breaking down, there's no obvious reason for that pattern to shut off in the next several days. Wahoo have started showing on the same structure as the mahi and yellowfin, a sign that the warm intrusion pushing this filament isn't a one-week fluke — high-speed trolled minnow-profile lures like a Braid Marauder or a small Ilander behind wire have accounted for the occasional wahoo mixed into an otherwise tuna-focused spread, and that's worth a line if the boat's rigged for it.

Looking at the next few days, the read is cautiously stable rather than explosive. The chop offshore should ease some as the wind pattern settles, which will make the run out past the canyon wall more comfortable and put more boats on the grounds to test the edge. As long as the filament holds its current position along the western wall near the Claw and the Dip, the yellowfin bite should stay consistent — not a blitz, but a reliable grind for boats that put in the time on the troll and stay disciplined about working the warm side of the break rather than drifting back onto the cold water. If that filament starts sliding or breaking apart — and this time of year, with the first real cold fronts starting to stack up behind each other, that's a real possibility inside the next week or two — expect the bite to get patchier and the fleet to have to hunt harder for the edge rather than finding it parked in one place. For now, it's holding, and that's the best news out of the canyon this week.
