# Water's split down the middle at Plum Gut — and the bass know it
*Warm water pushing from the west, cold water holding the Gut — the fish are stacking right where the two meet.*

**ORIENT, NY — August 3** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

Fella pulled up to the ramp at Mattituck Inlet Tuesday morning, boat still had the dealer sticker on the windshield, asked me where the bass were biting. I told him the same thing I've told every man who's asked me that question in sixty years on this water — depends which bass you mean, and depends what time of day you're willing to go looking for them. He didn't much care for that answer. Went out anyway, ran the shoreline at noon with topwater plugs, came back skunked and a little sunburned. That's not bad luck. That's just August.

Here's what's actually going on out there. The Sound's got a real temperature seam running through it right now — Kings Point way west is sitting near 73, and by the time you get out to New London it's dropped to under 70. That gradient doesn't stop at some invisible line on a chart, it drags right along our stretch, and the fish feel it before we do. Water this warm on the surface pushes the bass down and back, holding tight to structure through the daylight hours, and they don't come up to feed proper until the light goes soft. I've watched this pattern my whole life and it never changes with the calendar, only with the thermometer.

So the bass bite here has become a dusk-and-dark proposition, same as it always does this time of year. Best water's been the rip off Orient Point and the push into Plum Gut itself, where the current keeps things stirred and a few degrees cooler than the flat water either side of it. Guys eeling there after sundown are doing right by themselves — a whole or half snake eel, weighted just enough to hold near bottom on the drift, worked slow through the seam where the Gut current meets the slower Sound water. We said last week the tides would soften up heading toward this neap and that it'd push the bass onto the strongest current hours and the deepest, coolest structure — night eels at the Gut and working the bridge-adjacent rock — and that's exactly the program now. Nothing mysterious about it, the fish just go where the water's comfortable and the bait's easy.

Daytime, if you want bass, you're jigging deep over structure — the rock piles off Horton Point, the drop past Rocky Point — bouncing a bucktail or a diamond jig right on the bottom where they're holding tucked out of the sun. It's not fast fishing. You might work a spot forty-five minutes for two fish. But two keeper stripers off structure in the middle of the day beats an empty box, and it's honest work for honest fish.

Porgy's your bread and butter right now, no argument. Scup fishing on the reef structure off Horton Point and out toward Plum Gut is about as good as it gets all year — small hooks, squid strips or clam, bounced right on bottom, and you'll fill a bucket without much fuss. This is the fishery I send the kids and the first-timers to when the bass are being difficult, because it just about always produces.

Fluke's holding up nicely too. The channel edges and current-swept flats between Mattituck and Rocky Point have been giving up decent-sized fish on the last two hours of the outgoing — 3-inch white Gulp on a bucktail, or a strip of squid trailing a spearing, dragged slow along bottom where the current's cutting a seam. Nothing I'd call a doormat bite, but a few fish in the 20-to-24-inch range most trips out, which is plenty for a summer dinner.

Bluefish are around but scattered, and they're chasing bait, not tide charts. Wherever the bunker pods bunch up tight along the shoreline you'll get a flurry — poppers and metal will get hit hard for twenty minutes and then it goes quiet as the bunker move off. That's the whole story with blues this year. Find the bait, find the fish, and don't expect them to stay put.

Weakfish I've got nothing good to tell you. A few reports of small ones taken incidentally on bass gear near the inlet, nothing worth planning a trip around. That fishery's been thin for years out here and this week's no different.

Tide's easing toward the neap around the 5th and 6th, which means the Gut and the Race will run slower than they have — lighter jigs, slower retrieves, less brute force needed to hold bottom. That's not a bad thing. Slower current can actually concentrate bait in the seams rather than blow it through, and some of my better Plum Gut nights have come on the softer tides, not the hard ones.

Worth keeping an eye on the temperature break right at the Gut approach over the next week or two — that's historically where the first false albacore or bonito show up scouting through, once the water settles into that mid-to-upper 60s range on the cooler side of the seam. Too early to call it, but I'd rather tell you to watch for it now than have you surprised when someone hooks one off Orient and nobody believed the leader that came back.
