# Wood Island Ledge Gave Up a 28-Pound Bass on the Incoming — Then Went Quiet
*Biddeford Pool's morning flood is still the best hour in Southern Maine, but you've got to be there for it — the rest of the tide is a coin flip.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — August 3** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Tuesday, half-tide incoming, fog still sitting on the water off Wood Island. I put a black Danny plug on the outside edge of the ledge where the current starts to stack against the rock, and on the third cast something hit it like it meant to end my week. Twenty-eight pounds, fat as a football, full of mackerel. That's the Pool doing what it does best — bass staging on the bar waiting for the tide to shove bait over the top, and for about ninety minutes it was as good as this beat gets all summer. Then the tide filled in, the current went soft, and it was over. That's the honest version. One great hour, and I worked the rest of the morning for a follower and nothing else.

That's basically the story of the whole zone right now — short, sharp windows, and a lot of dead water in between. We're sliding toward the neap around the 5th and 6th, and you can feel the current losing its shoulders already. Less push means less bait getting flushed out of the marshes and over the structure, which means bass aren't being forced to feed — they're doing it on their own schedule, and that schedule is dawn, dusk, and full dark. We flagged this coming — said the fish would tighten up onto the hardest current hours and the deepest structure once the tides went slack, and that's exactly what's happening at the Pool channel and around Wood Island Harbor. If you're fishing the middle of the day right now, you're fishing for company, not fish.

Mackerel are still thick off the Cape Elizabeth ledges and out past Prouts Neck, which is good news because that's the bait pushing bass up tight to the rocks. Snag a few on a mackerel tree, drop one back on a fishfinder rig into the deeper cuts off Prouts on a falling tide, and you'll find fish holding right on the bottom in fifteen to twenty-five feet, especially when there's a little southwest wind blowing bait against that shoreline. That wind direction is the one to watch down there — it piles water and forage right into the rocks at Prouts Neck and makes the whole stretch from the Neck down toward Bald Head fishable with big swimmers and pencil poppers worked slow along the edges.

Higgins Beach had a real moment of it Thursday evening — pogies got pushed up tight to the sandbar by a body of bluefish that came through like a chainsaw. Birds diving, blues busting bait on top, the whole show for maybe forty minutes right at last light. Anybody throwing a silver Kastmaster or a Hopkins through the melee got bit, some fish pushing six, seven pounds with teeth that'll take a chunk out of a soft plastic if you're not paying attention. That's a bait-driven bite, pure and simple — wherever those pogy schools reform after getting scattered, the blues show up within hours. If the bunker stay put off Higgins, that pattern should run again this week, but bluefish blitzes up here come and go with the bait schools, and I wouldn't plan a whole trip around one location without a backup.

Pine Point's been quieter for stripers but solid for flounder — the channel edges off the Nonesuch and Scarborough River mouths are holding fish on the bottom, bloodworms or clam on a simple hi-lo rig, fished right on the last of the outgoing when the current's carrying the flats water out through the cut. Nothing to write home about size-wise, but steady action if you want dinner and don't mind soaking bait for a couple hours.

Night fishing is where the real bass are right now. Cape Elizabeth's rocky points, the mouth of the Pool, and the deeper water around Wood Island Harbor are all holding bigger fish that are laying up on structure through the heat of the day and coming up to feed after dark. An eel on a slow swim past the ledges, or a big darter worked just under the surface in that first hour of full dark, has been the move for anyone willing to put in the hours. It's not fast fishing. It's a couple of good bites a trip if you're in the right spot on the right stage of tide, but the fish that come are usually the better ones.

Offshore, if you've got a boat that'll run out into the cooler Gulf of Maine water, cod and pollock have stayed steady on the deeper structure — nothing dramatic, just consistent, which this time of year I'll take.

Looking ahead: with the tides softening into the neap, I'd expect the bite to keep compressing into those tight windows — dawn and dusk at Higgins if the bait holds, the incoming hour at the Pool for anyone who can time it right, and night eels for the ledge-huggers at Wood Island and Prouts Neck. If the southwest wind holds through the weekend, Prouts Neck is where I'd put my time. If it swings around and kicks up a lot of chop, I'd rather work the calmer water inside the Pool and let the tide do the sorting for me.
