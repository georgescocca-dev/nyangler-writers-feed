# Shoals Cod Hold Deep, Bass Sneak Into Hampton on the Flood
*Thirteen miles of coastline doesn't mean thirteen miles of fishing — the Isles of Shoals still stack fish nobody bothers to find.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 3** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People hear thirteen miles of coastline and figure that's the whole story for New Hampshire. It's not. Run out past White Island, work the backside of Appledore, drop a line off Duck Island Ledge in a hundred and fifty feet — you're fishing water that behaves like it's off Chatham or Monhegan, not tucked between two states that barely claim a coastline at all. I trailer a 20-foot Lund out of Rye Harbor most mornings before the sun's up good, and by the time the tourist boats are loading up for their nine o'clock run to the Shoals, I've already got a box going.

This week the pattern out there has been steady if you respect it. Cod are still down on the deep structure off the Shoals — 130 to 180 feet, sometimes deeper on the humps southeast of Duck Island. Diamond jigs, the heavier ones, 8 to 12 ounces depending on drift speed, worked with a short hop off bottom, have been the most consistent producer. A couple boats out of Rye have done well soaking clam bellies on a simple two-hook bottom rig in the same water when the current's light enough to hold bottom without a ton of weight. Fish have run mostly in the 5 to 10 pound class, with the occasional double-digit fish coming up when someone finds a pocket that hasn't been worked over. Haddock have been spottier — you'll mark them on the sounder in loose clusters rather than the tight schools you want, and it's taking more moves to put a limit together than it did back in June. If you're haddock-specific right now, plan on burning gas checking three or four spots rather than parking on one.

The tide's about to soften up on us. We're sliding into a neap around the 5th and 6th, and that's going to knock the current down noticeably out at the Shoals and in the Piscataqua both. When that happens, drop jig weight and slow the retrieve — the fish don't move as far to feed when the water's barely moving, so you need to put the jig right on their nose rather than count on drift to cover ground. I've seen this exact stretch before in early August: current goes soft, cod tighten up right on the structure instead of spreading over it, and the guys who keep fishing like it's still ripping end up working way too fast over fish that would've eaten a slower presentation.

Stripers have been the more interesting story this week, honestly. Mackerel schools are working the coast in good numbers right now, and wherever those pods bunch up near structure, bass and blues are showing up to hammer them. Rye Harbor breakwater and the water off Great Boars Head have both given up bluefish blitzes in the last week — nothing you need a boat for, casting metal, Deadly Dicks and Kastmasters, into visible surface activity has been enough. The stripers have settled into that classic mid-summer rhythm: they're holding deep on structure through the heat of the day and coming up hard at first light, at dusk, and after dark. If you're fishing daylight hours, you're jigging deep or live-lining a mackerel over structure and waiting. If you can fish the edges of the day, that's when the real bite happens.

Hampton Harbor keeps doing what it's known for — bass sliding in on the incoming tide to work the bait pushed up into the back channels, then dropping back out with the ebb. I've had my best luck there fishing the last two hours of the flood, right as the current starts to slow near the top, with a live mackerel or a chunk of fresh bunker fished on a fish-finder rig tight to the deeper channel edges near the inlet. After dark, that same stretch has been worth an eel swim for anyone willing to stay out past the tourist traffic. Seabrook side of the same system has produced similarly — the bait doesn't care which side of the channel it's on, and neither do the bass.

Flounder have been quiet, which isn't a surprise for early August — that's a spring and fall fishery here more than a summer one, and the deeper holes in the Piscataqua near Portsmouth Harbor are about the only place worth a drift right now if you're set on flatfish. I wouldn't build a trip around it this week.

Looking ahead: expect the softening tide to keep pushing cod tighter to structure through the weekend, which actually makes them easier to find once you commit to slowing down. Bass and blues should keep riding the mackerel schools along the open coast as long as that bait stays put, and Hampton's harbor mouth should keep producing on the flood as long as the bait keeps funneling through. If I had one trip to make this week, I'd run the Shoals early on a light-current morning for cod, then swing back toward Rye Harbor in the evening to see if the blues are still working the breakwater. Small water, but it never runs out of things to show you if you're willing to look.
