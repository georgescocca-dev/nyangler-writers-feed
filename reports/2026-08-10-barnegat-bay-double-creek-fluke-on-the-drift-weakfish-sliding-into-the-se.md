# Double Creek Fluke on the Drift, Weakfish Sliding Into the Sedges on the Peanut Run
*The shallows are pushing past 80 and shoving every fluke, weakfish, and blue claw crab into the channels — here's where to find them.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — August 10** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

I was maybe fourteen the first August I mated on a Point Pleasant boat, and the skipper — old Sal Renna, God rest him — told me something I never forgot. He said, "Gus, in August the bay tells you where it's comfortable, and the fish just follow along." Forty years later I still think about that every time the mercury climbs and the shallows go from bath-warm to downright uncomfortable for anything with fins. That's exactly where we are right now, and it's why the last week of fishing has funneled itself into a handful of very specific places instead of spreading out across the whole bay the way it does in June.

The flats off Tices Shoal and the shallow sod banks up toward Berkeley are pushing near 80 degrees by midafternoon, and that's simply too warm for a self-respecting fluke or weakfish to sit in comfortably all day. What that's done is stack both species into the deeper, current-scrubbed structure — Double Creek Channel most of all. I've had three good drifts through there this week on the last two hours of the outgoing, working 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with a 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet along the drop where the channel falls from six feet down to twelve. Keeper fluke to five pounds have come off that edge, with the better fish holding right where the sandy slope meets the harder mud bottom — that's the spot where the bait gets funneled and pinned by the tide, and the doormats know it.

Weakfish have been the pleasant surprise. We flagged this trend back at the start of the month — figured the peanut bunker would push south and pull weakfish right into the bay with them through August — and that's exactly what's happening now. I've been finding schools of weakfish holding along the sedge banks south of Oyster Creek, particularly where the marsh grass gives way to a slightly deeper cut, on the last of the incoming tide. Small bucktails in pink or chartreuse, or a plain Kahle hook with a live killie under a slip float, worked slow along the edge of the grass has been the ticket. These aren't the 5-pound tiderunners we see in May off the same water, more 16 to 19 inches, but they're eating with some confidence in the low light of early morning and again right at dusk.

Speaking of low light — that's become the whole game for striped bass right now. Daytime bay temperatures have shut down the bite almost everywhere except right around the inlet structure and the cooler water pushing out of Oyster Creek's mouth on a strong ebb. Barnegat Inlet itself has been holding some bass around the rocks on the jetty side, particularly at first light when there's still a little chill in the air and bunker pods are working the surface. Live bunker on a fish-finder rig, or a bunker chunk fished tight to the rocks on the bottom half of the outgoing, has produced fish in the 20-to-28-inch range with the occasional better one mixed in after dark. If you want a shot at a bigger bass, I'd rather be out there at 5 a.m. or after 9 p.m. than fighting the heat at noon — the fish simply aren't interested in daytime feeding right now, not in the bay, not with this water temperature.

Snapper blues are doing exactly what they do every August — thick as fleas in the shallows off Good Luck Point, up in the Toms River mouth, and along the docks in Forked River, chasing bay anchovies and rain bait in schools you can see dimpling the surface on a calm evening. Small Kastmasters, or a 1/16-ounce jig under a bobber for the kids, will get bit on nearly every cast. It's not glamorous fishing but it's the best thing going for teaching a kid to cast and catch something that fights back, and I'd argue it's the backbone of how most of us on this bay got started in the first place.

The adult bluefish have stayed mostly offshore and out toward the inlet edges, mixing into bait schools when the bunker push through — nothing like a blitz, but worth a look if you're running out past Barnegat Light on the right tide.

Blue claw crabbing has stayed strong right through this heat, which tells you something about how comfortable the crabs are with warm, slightly brackish water even when the fish are struggling with it. Bunker heads and chicken necks off any dock along the Metedeconk or up in Silver Bay have been producing full baskets most evenings.

The full moon this week has put some extra push into the tides, and I've noticed the bite windows at the channel edges — Double Creek especially — sharpening up right around the peak of the current rather than spreading out lazily like they do on a neap tide. If you're planning your week around one trip, I'd put myself on Double Creek an hour before the top of the outgoing, working that channel edge with bucktail and Gulp, then slide over to the Oyster Creek sedges as the tide bottoms out and starts to turn, right when the weakfish seem most willing to commit. As the moon works past full over the next few days, expect that exaggerated current to ease back to something more normal — which usually means the bite windows widen out again, even if they don't hit quite as hard in the moment. Keep an eye on the wind, too; a stiff afternoon southwesterly can turn the open bay chalky by midday, and that's when I'd rather be tucked into the channels anyway.
