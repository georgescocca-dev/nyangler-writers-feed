# Outgoing After Midnight: Bass Hold the Deep Hole, Weakfish Working the Bridge Lights
*Warm bay water is pushing the real bite into the dark hours — here's where I'm putting my time before the full moon rebuilds the tide.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — August 17** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

I made a call Tuesday night I've been making all month: skip the sunset crowd at the inlet and wait for the 11 p.m. slack to turn. Bay water's sitting warm right now, upper 70s pushing 80 on the flats by Tiana, and when it's like that the stripers don't want anything to do with daylight. They stack up in the deep hole on the ocean side of the jetty and wait for the outgoing to pull cooler water and bait past them. That's where I want to be — not at dusk when everybody else is casting into the pressure, but two hours into the ebb when the current's ripping and the boils start showing up tight against the rocks.

The pattern held again this week. Working the north jetty rocks on the outgoing, I've been getting slot bass and a few overslots on bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, weight just heavy enough to hold bottom in that current — usually 3 to 4 ounces depending on how hard the water's running. Eels work too if you want to cast and swim them through the seam where the deep hole meets the sandbar, but I've had better luck staying still and letting the chunk do the work. Best window has been that hour and a half after the tide really gets moving, before it goes slack again around 2 or 3 a.m. This is a stealth game — no lights on the deck, quiet with the anchor, and let the fish come to the smell, not the noise.

Ponquogue Bridge has been the other half of my nights. Weakfish are holding under the lights on the down-current side of the pilings, and they want it small and subtle — a bucktail, quarter to three-eighths ounce, tipped with a strip of squid or a small piece of bunker, hopped slow along bottom in 15 to 20 feet. The bite's been better on the last two hours of the outgoing than on the incoming lately, which tracks with how warm the bay's running — that current pulls cooler, cleaner water out from the back bay and the weakfish key on it. Sizes have been mixed, some short fish mixed with a few keepers in the 16 to 18 inch range, so it's not a bucket-fill night, but it's a real bite if you're patient and quiet about it.

Daytime fluke has been a mixed bag, and I'll be straight about it — the bay's mostly throwing back shorts right now. Warm, stagnant flats away from current don't hold quality fish this time of year, so I've been running the drift tight to the inlet mouth and along the edge of the channel where the tide keeps things moving and a few degrees cooler. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, drifted right along that channel edge in 12 to 20 feet on the last of the incoming, have put a few keeper-class fish in the box, but you're working for them. If you want numbers over size, the back bay flats off Tiana still hold plenty of shorts to keep rods bent while you wait on the tide to turn.

Bluefish have been the one thing you can count on without much thinking. Snapper blues are thick as ever up in the canal and around the back bay creeks off Hampton Bays — we flagged this building a couple weeks back, said the adult class would follow as the season pushed into late August, and that's exactly what's happening now. Bigger blues, 3 to 5 pounds with a few better, are mixing into the bait around the inlet on the outgoing, blowing up on the surface when the bunker get pinned against the rocks. Poppers and metal — Kastmasters, half-ounce, worked fast — have been getting hit hard right at first light before the sun gets high, which lines up with that dawn window everyone's seeing region-wide right now.

Porgies are still around the reef structure inside the inlet and off Ponquogue in 15 to 25 feet, worm rigs with sandworm or squid strips doing the job on the bottom stages of the tide when the current slows enough to hold bottom without a ton of weight. Blackfish are still a few weeks off from being worth a dedicated trip, but I'd start scouting structure now for when that opens up.

Looking ahead, the tide's building toward the August 28 full moon, and that's going to sharpen everything at current-driven structure — the inlet jetty, the bridge pilings, the canal mouth. Bigger tidal swings mean stronger current, which means more bait getting pushed and more predictable feeding windows after dark. If I had one night to fish this week, I'd take the outgoing starting an hour before midnight at the inlet, work the deep hole quiet, and swing over to the bridge on the tail end of the tide for weakfish. That's the play until the moon gets fatter and the current really starts moving.
