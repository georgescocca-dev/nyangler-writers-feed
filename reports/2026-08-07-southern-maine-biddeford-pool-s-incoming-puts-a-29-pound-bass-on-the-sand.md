# Biddeford Pool's Incoming Puts a 29-Pound Bass on the Sand
*Building tide into the new moon is stacking bait at the river mouths, but the bluefish are still playing hard to find.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — August 8** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Thursday evening, forty minutes into the incoming at the Pool, a guy I've fished alongside for fifteen years hooked a striper that buried the rod tip into the rocks and didn't let up for what felt like ten minutes. Twenty-nine pounds, caught on a black Gibbs pencil popper worked slow and choppy off the outer bar where the current pours in against the rocks. That's the Pool doing what it does best — it's the single best hour of striper fishing in Southern Maine, that first push of green water sliding over the flats and funneling bait and bass together into a tight seam you can cover with two dozen well-placed casts.

I've been saying for a couple weeks now that the building tide into this new moon would sharpen up the current-driven spots, and here we are — Biddeford Pool, Wells Harbor, the mouth of the Kennebunk River, all fishing better on the last two days than they did the whole back half of July. The moon's pulling harder water through those inlets now, more current means more bait getting swept and confused, and a confused school of mackerel or pogies in three feet of moving water is exactly what a striper wants to see. If you fish the Pool, get there on the bottom of the tide and stay through the first two hours of the flood. That's your window. After that the current slackens, the fish spread out over the flats, and the bite gets a lot more patient.

Up the beach at Kennebunk, the river mouth has been holding fish right where the outflow meets the surf line — schoolies mostly, 18 to 24 inches, but mixed in with the occasional slot fish pushing 30. Live mackerel under a slip float, or a swimming plug like a Yo-Zuri Mag Darter worked with a stop-start retrieve through the current seam, has been the ticket there on the outgoing. The river's pushing warmer water out into cooler ocean water and that edge is holding bait tight.

Mackerel have been thick off the Cape Elizabeth ledges and around the back side of Prouts Neck, and that's been a blessing for anybody willing to run out and jig a few before hitting the beach. Small diamond jigs, no bait needed, and you can load a cooler in twenty minutes some mornings. Fresh mackerel filleted into strips has been outfishing anything artificial for the bigger bass holding deeper around the Prouts Neck boulders — fish it on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in that rocky wash, and be ready for the rod to load up hard because the fish down there this time of year tend to run heavier.

Bluefish are the frustrating part of the report this week. I keep hearing guys ask about Higgins Beach blitzes and I have to be honest — it hasn't happened yet, not the kind of surface-churning, birds-diving event we all want to see. What I am seeing is scattered pods, three or four fish busting bait for a few minutes and then going quiet, mostly early morning on the outgoing tide with a light southwest wind pushing bait tight to the beach. If you're throwing metal — a Hopkins or a Kastmaster — keep one rigged and ready when you're plugging for bass at Higgins, because those blues show up without much warning and vanish just as fast. The Prouts Neck side, with that southwest wind funneling bait around the point, has produced a similar pattern — brief windows, worth being patient for, but nothing you can plan a whole trip around yet.

Flounder guys have had a quieter but more dependable week. Wells Harbor has been giving up decent flounder on bloodworms and clam strips fished on a simple two-hook bottom rig, best on the last of the ebb when the water's clean and slack enough to hold bottom without dragging. It's not glamorous fishing but it's steady, and it's a good option on the days the wind kicks up and makes the open beaches unfishable.

Cod remain mostly a deeper-water story right now — the ledges off Cape Elizabeth still hold fish for the boat guys working jigs and bait near bottom, but inshore action from the rocks has been slow, which is normal for August. That fishery really wakes up as the water starts cooling into September and October, so if cod are your target, patience is the play for another month or so.

Looking ahead to the new moon on the 12th, I expect the tidal range to keep building and the current-driven spots to keep sharpening right along with it. The Pool should keep producing on that incoming push, and I'd watch Kennebunk and the York River mouths closely too as the extra flow stirs bait loose. Southern Maine's still sitting well behind the warmer water to our south — no albies, no bonito up here, and I don't expect that to change — but the stripers don't seem to mind the cold Gulf of Maine water one bit. If the wind stays out of the southwest and the tides keep building like they have been, I'd put my money on the Pool again next week, timed right on that bottom-of-the-tide push, pencil popper in hand.
