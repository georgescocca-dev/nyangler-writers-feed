# Monhegan Ledges on the Ebb: Bass Push the Structure While Pollock Work the Top
*Mackerel are thick enough to feed the whole bay, and that's dragging stripers and groundfish both into range this week.*

**BOOTHBAY HARBOR, ME — August 3** — _by Rusty Pelletier, Zone Writer_

I was hauling gear off the backside of Monhegan Tuesday morning, southwest ledge, and had to stop the boat twice because the mackerel were showering so thick they looked like rain hitting the water sideways. Forty years I've watched that happen out there, and it never fails to mean something. Where the mackerel pile up this time of year, the bigger fish aren't far behind, and this week they weren't.

Striped bass have settled into the pattern I'd expect for the first week of August — holding tight to structure through the heat of the day and coming up on the mackerel schools at first light and again toward dusk. The river mouths are the place to start. Damariscotta, Medomak, and the St. George have all been giving up fish on the last of the incoming and the first hour of the ebb, when the current pulls bait out of the marsh grass and stacks it against the ledge rock. Live mackerel on a fish-finder rig, or a big white or chartreuse bucktail worked slow along bottom in twelve to twenty feet, has been the ticket. Nothing fancy. The bass don't need convincing when the bait's this thick — they need you in the right spot at the right minute of tide, and that's the whole game up here.

Eastern Egg Rock has fished the way it usually does in early August — a little bit of everything if you're willing to work for it. Bass around the edges of the rock on the push tide, mackerel stacked over the deeper water to the south and west of it thick enough that you can jig a rod bent double in under a minute, and the puffins doing their business overhead like they always do this time of year. If you want mackerel for bait or the pan, that's your spot. Diamond jigs, no bait needed, straight up and down in fifteen to thirty feet.

Groundfish are where the real story is for anybody running out past the islands. Penobscot Bay's deeper channels — the water off Two Bush, the drop-offs east of Vinalhaven, the ledges running down toward Isle au Haut — have been holding cod and haddock in the classic pattern, working the bottom in a hundred to a hundred-eighty feet where the water stays cool no matter what the surface is doing. Jigging has outfished bait this week, diamond jigs and heavier soft-plastic tails worked with a short hop off bottom. Pollock, meanwhile, have been coming up on top over structure around Matinicus and the outer ledges off Stonington, chasing the same mackerel that are drawing everything else in. If you see birds working and pollock rolling on the surface, that's your cue to put down the bottom rig and throw something shiny on top — a Deadly Dick or a light diamond jig worked fast through the melee will get bit fast.

We flagged conditions holding steady through this stretch last week, and that's exactly what's played out — stable high pressure, clean water, bait thick enough to organize the whole food chain from mackerel up through bass and groundfish. That kind of stability is a gift this time of year, and it's why the fishing's been dependable rather than spectacular. Nobody's filling a cooler in an hour, but a patient boat working the right tide stage has been finding fish pretty consistently.

Bluefish remain the exception up here — a few showing around the outer islands and in Boothbay's outer harbor when the water's warmed enough, but this isn't Jersey or the Sound. If you connect on a blue up this way in early August, treat it as a bonus, not a target species.

Looking ahead, the moon's sliding toward neap around the fifth and sixth, and that's going to soften the current everywhere from the Muscle Ridge Channel out to Monhegan. When that current lays down, the bass tend to spread off the hard structure and settle into the deeper eddies where the bait pools up rather than getting pushed past — that's a pattern I've watched play out plenty of Augusts. It doesn't mean the fish disappear, it means you slow down and fish the troughs and the back sides of ledges instead of the fast water. Groundfish trips should hold up fine regardless, since the deep channels don't care much what the tide's doing on the surface, but the pollock topwater bite will likely go quieter for a few days until the current builds back.

If I had one day this week, I'd run out to Monhegan on the last two hours of the ebb, work the southwest ledge with bucktails first, then switch to jigs on the pollock if I see them showing. Bring mackerel gear regardless — you'll want bait either way, and right now there's no shortage of it out there.
