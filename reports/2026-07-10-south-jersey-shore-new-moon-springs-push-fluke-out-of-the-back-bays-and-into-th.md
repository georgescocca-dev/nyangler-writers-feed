# New moon springs push fluke out of the back bays and into the inlets
*Warm bay water is doing the work for us this week — the fish are stacking where the current still runs cold and clean.*

**OCEAN CITY, NJ — July 10** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

Class is in session, and the lesson this week is thermal geography. Last week the back bays from Absecon to Great Egg cooked — sod bank flats pushing into the high 70s on the afternoon tides, which is about ten degrees warmer than a fluke wants to work in. That's not a guess, that's basic biology: doormats are ambush predators, and ambush predators don't like being lethargic in warm water any more than we like running wind sprints in August. So the fluke did what they always do when the shallow water gets soupy — they slid to where the current still moves fast enough to stay cool. That means Absecon Inlet, Great Egg Inlet, the deeper reaches of Townsends, and the channel edges just inside Stone Harbor. If you were still working the grass flats behind Brigantine last week wondering where everybody went, that's your answer.

This week the tide chart does the fish a favor. We're coming off a new moon, which means spring tides — bigger swings, stronger current, more water moving through those inlets on both the flood and the drain. Peak alignment lands right around Saturday through Monday, July 11-13. Bigger tides mean the inlets flush harder, which pulls more bait — spearing, mullet, small peanut bunker — out of the bay and stacks it against the jetty rocks and the edges of the channel. That's exactly where you want to be dragging a bucktail. Wind's been mild out of the southwest most days, nothing that's torn the water up, so clarity at the inlets has stayed respectable — not gin clear, but fishable, which matters more than people think when you're trying to get a fluke to commit in six feet of moving water.

The fluke program right now is an inlet program, full stop. Absecon has been the most consistent — working the outgoing tide along the rock line on the Brigantine side, three-quarter-ounce white bucktails tipped with four-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet in pearl or white, dragged slow along the bottom where the current slackens behind the rocks. Fish in the 17 to 20-inch range have been steady, with enough 20-22 inch keepers mixed in to make it worth your gas money. Great Egg has been similar — the channel edge off Longport and the drop just inside the inlet on the ocean side holding fish on the last two hours of the outgoing, when the water funnels hardest. Chartreuse and white combos have both worked; I haven't seen one color running away from the other, which tells me it's more about presentation than paint job right now. Keep that bucktail ticking bottom, don't overwork it — a fluke in moving water doesn't need much convincing once the bait's in the strike zone.

Bass fishing has been a mixed bag, and I'll be honest about that instead of dressing it up. A few schoolies and some slot fish have been coming out of the surf at night on bunker chunks fished on fish-finder rigs off Ocean City and Sea Isle, mostly the first two hours of the incoming when bait gets pushed up against the bar. But it's not consistent — some nights you get two or three fish, some nights you get sand fleas and nothing else. The bigger stripers everybody's hoping for with the mullet run are still weeks off; that push doesn't usually build until late August into September down here, so patience is the word.

Weakfish, I have to be straight with you — still nothing. The bay's been above 70 degrees for over two weeks now, which used to be the trigger everybody watched for, but warm water alone clearly isn't enough anymore. I haven't heard a credible weakfish report out of the Great Egg or Absecon systems all month. Whatever's driving that decline, it's not just temperature — I'd stop planning trips around a thermometer reading and start looking at it as one ingredient in a recipe we don't fully have yet.

Kingfish have been a nice bright spot for the surf guys working the troughs off Ocean City and Stone Harbor, small pieces of bloodworm or Fishbites on a high-low rig in three to six feet of water right off the beach — good action for anybody bringing kids or just wanting steady bites between the bigger targets. Triggerfish are starting to show around the inshore wrecks and reef sites off Great Egg, which is right on seasonal schedule; small pieces of clam or squid on a light jig head near structure will find them, though they're more of a bonus fish than something to build a whole trip around yet.

Looking offshore for a second, because it matters to guys running out of Absecon and Great Egg for tuna — the warm-water edge that set up midweek out toward the canyons was about as good a structural setup as we've seen all year, and boats working the shelf reported solid yellowfin activity on that seam. That window's closing, but if a similar edge reforms, it's worth the run.

For this coming week, I'd fish the inlets on the outgoing with bucktails, keep an eye on the new moon springs peaking through Monday for the strongest current windows, and check the eastern inlets on a clean incoming tide for any stray Spanish mackerel or bonito — the pelagic window's been open along the coast and South Jersey inlets have occasionally seen strays slip in behind bait pushes. Nothing guaranteed there, but the conditions are right enough to keep a spoon rod rigged just in case.
