# Forget the noon fluke drift — the jetty bite doesn't start till the sun goes down
*With the ocean running warm and lumpy, Jones Inlet's best striper action is happening after dark against the rocks, not on the midday flats.*

**WANTAGH, NY — August 10** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Every boat ramp conversation this week is the same: guys lining up at first light to hit the Sloop Channel drift for fluke, burning gas by 9 a.m., back at the dock with a cooler of shorts by lunch. I get it — the drift bite is real, and I'll get into it. But if you want to know where I've been putting my time, it's not out there in the noon sun. It's at the Jones Inlet jetty rocks, starting an hour before sunset and running into full dark, because that's when the stripers actually show up to eat.

Here's the thing nobody wants to admit in August: the ocean's running 78.8 degrees off the Bight buoy, which is bathwater. Stripers don't want anything to do with that kind of heat during the day. They tuck into the current-swept structure and cooler pockets and wait for the sun to drop before they'll commit to feeding. That's exactly the pattern I'm seeing at the inlet — dead quiet at 2 p.m., and then the last hour of light the bunker start getting nervous against the rocks and the bass show up underneath them like clockwork. I've been throwing bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig, 3-ounce egg sinker, right into the wash where the jetty meets the channel, and hooking fish from the last hour of daylight through the first two hours of dark. Nothing giant — mostly 24 to 30 inches, with the occasional keeper mixed in — but it's steady, and it's a lot better than standing out there at noon getting nothing.

If you want to fish it legit after hours, Field 1 East Bathhouse has the night access again this year — Friday, Saturday, Sunday, dusk to 6 a.m., and it's a good launch point for the eastern surf that runs current west toward the inlet. You need the Night Sport Fishing Permit and the DEC saltwater registry, no way around it, but it's worth the paperwork. That stretch holds sandbars better than the west end of the beach, and on the outgoing tide the current sweeps bait right along that structure.

Now, the fluke — I'm not going to pretend it's bad, because it's not. The drift through Sloop Channel and the deeper edges off the inlet is still the most dependable thing going in this zone right now. Guys are picking through a lot of shorts to find keepers in the 20 to 30 foot depths, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow right along the channel drop-offs where the water stays a few degrees cooler than the flats. The full moon has the tide running harder than it has in weeks, and that stronger push is sharpening the bite right at the tide changes — first hour of the outgoing and the last hour of the incoming are when I'm hearing about the better fish, doormats up to 5 and 6 pounds mixed in with the boat-load of shorts. If you're going to fish it, go at the tide change, not the middle of the tide when everything's just laying there.

Bluefish are doing what bluefish do in August — riding shotgun on whatever bait's around. Snapper blues are thick in the back bay creeks off Freeport and Wantagh, great for kids with a bobber and a piece of spearing, and there are cocktail blues mixing into the bunker schools at the inlet mouth during that same dusk window where I'm finding the bass. Don't be shocked if you hook a chopper blue on a bunker chunk meant for a striper — happens more nights than not right now.

Weakfish are still a bonus fish, not a target, but I'm hearing more legit reports out of South Oyster Bay than I have in a couple seasons — fish in the 16 to 20 inch range coming on soft plastics worked slow along the grass edges in the low-light hours. If the comeback's real, this is the kind of summer where it starts showing up in guys' coolers instead of just talk at the bait shop.

Porgies are exactly where you'd expect — jetty rock piles and the nearby bay structure, sandworms and clam on a hi-lo rig, and they'll keep you busy between bass bites if the action's slow.

We flagged a couple weeks back that the first peanut bunker scouts were showing up in southern New England and that the bay peanuts here were due inside two weeks — I haven't seen them stack up in the back creeks yet, but the timing lines up close, and when they do show, that back-bay food chain flips fast. Worth watching the marsh creek mouths on the outgoing tide over the next week or so.

As the moon slides past full, that exaggerated tidal push will ease back to normal, which usually settles the bite windows into something more predictable rather than the jacked-up current we've had. My plan doesn't change much — I'll keep working the jetty from last light into dark as long as the ocean stays warm, because that's where the fish want to be right now. If you're set on daytime fishing, hit the fluke drift right on the tide change and don't waste your morning fishing dead water in between.
