# Outgoing After Midnight: Bass Hold Deep at the Inlet While Bonito Work the Jetty by Day
*Weakfish are still showing at the Ponquogue Bridge on the night tide, but the real story this week is bait — and who shows up to eat it first.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — September 4** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one window this week, take the outgoing after dark at the inlet over anything the daylight fleet is doing on the ocean side. Water's still sitting in the low-to-mid 70s, which is warm enough to keep striped bass lazy and non-committal during the day, but once the sun drops and the tide starts pulling hard out of the bay, those fish tuck into the current seams along the east and west jetties and feed with more purpose. That's been the pattern all week, and there's no reason to think it changes before the weekend.

The bigger story setting up is bait. Bunker schools are thickening up and down the south shore corridor, and while Shinnecock hasn't seen a true blitz yet, the pieces are lining up. The moon builds toward full on the 6th and 7th, tidal flow strengthens with it, and that's usually the trigger that tightens up loosely scattered bunker into the kind of dense balls that get stripers committing instead of just picking at the edges. We flagged this zone last week as one to watch over the next few days, and the arrival of bonito working bait around the inlet mouth is exactly the kind of confirmation that tells you the water's turning over the way it should this time of year. Don't be surprised if the first real blitz activity on this stretch breaks within the next week to ten days rather than tomorrow.

At the inlet itself, the night bass bite has been steady if not spectacular. Working the outgoing starting about two hours before dead low, boats and surfcasters working the rocks on the ocean side of the east jetty have been picking at slot bass in the 26 to 34 inch range on bucktails — 1 to 1.5 ounce white or chartreuse, bounced slow along bottom in 8 to 15 feet where the current rips past the rock piles. Live eels fished on a fish-finder rig near the deep hole inside the inlet, the one that drops to around 20 feet on the bay side of the Coast Guard station, have also been producing after midnight, especially on the darker nights around the new-to-first-quarter stretch. Once that moon fills in this week, expect the bite to shift earlier in the tide as the extra light pushes fish tighter to structure and shadow lines under the jetty rocks.

Daytime around the inlet mouth and outside the jetties has belonged to bluefish and now bonito. The blues have been the most dependable bite in the whole system right now — peanut bunker and rain bait pushing through the inlet on the ebb are getting jumped by choppers in the 3 to 6 pound range, and small metal like a half-ounce Deadly Dick or a Kastmaster in silver has been drawing both blues and the first wave of bonito working the same bait schools just outside the rocks. That bonito push has been building westward out of Block Island and Point Judith all summer, and its arrival here right on the calendar is worth paying attention to — fast retrieves with light wire or straight 20-pound fluoro leader, cast into the surface nervousness rather than blind-casting open water, has been the difference for the boats connecting.

The Ponquogue Bridge weakfish bite is still there but it's a tighter window than it was a month ago. Working the pilings on the last two hours of the outgoing, after the sun's well down, bucktail-and-Gulp combinations — a 3/8-ounce jig with a pearl or chartreuse 4-inch swimming mullet — dropped tight to the concrete and worked with a slow lift-and-drop through 12 to 18 feet has been drawing keeper weakfish along with the usual bycatch of small bluefish. It's not a numbers bite, more a patience bite, and the boats staying out past midnight are doing better than the sunset crowd.

Fluke are doing exactly what fluke do in early September — thinning out fast. The doormat-class fish that were holding in the deeper holes of the bay through August are starting to slide out toward ocean-side structure ahead of the fall migration, and the drift inside the bay has gotten noticeably quieter. There's still some action to be had drifting white Gulp Swimming Mullets over sand-to-mud transitions between Tiana Beach and the canal entrance on the last of the incoming, but don't expect the volume from earlier in the summer. If fluke is the target, this is the week to fish the ocean-side structure outside the inlet rather than the bay.

Porgies remain a solid bread-and-butter option for anyone wanting steady action without chasing tides too precisely — bay-side structure and the rocks along the canal have been holding fish on bloodworms and clam on light tackle, a good option for the daylight hours when the bass and weakfish bite is dead. Blackfish season is still ahead, but the canal itself remains the connector worth remembering — that stretch to the Peconic keeps moving bait and fish through on both tide changes, and it's worth a look if the ocean side gets blown out.

Looking ahead, watch the moon and watch the wind. If nights stay calm through the weekend, the outgoing tide at the inlet after dark is still the highest-percentage play for bass, and the bonito ought to keep pushing in daylight around the jetties as that westward migration continues. If a real cool front finally shows up behind this building moon, that's when the fall bass trigger could actually break loose — and when it does, the smart money says it happens on a night tide, same as it always does out here.
