# New moon springs load up the Sound as stripers push the flood at sunset
*Charles Island and Stratford Shoal turn on with the tide while fluke slide out of the warm harbors and porgies keep the rockpiles honest.*

**MILFORD, CT — July 10** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound did that thing it does in early July — it warmed up fast in the harbors and shallow flats, and the fish voted with their fins. New Haven Harbor and the back of Milford Gulf were running warm enough that you could feel it through your waders at low tide, and the fluke that had been stacked in there all June started sliding out toward the mouths and the deeper channel edges. Wind was mostly light out of the south and southwest through the middle of the week, which kept the surface glassy for the early risers but didn't do much to mix the water column. We're heading into new moon now — peak alignment falls Saturday through Monday — and that means the spring tides are loading up fast. Bigger swings, more water moving, and a PM flood that's timing up almost perfectly with sunset from Stratford down through Madison. That's the window I've been circling on the calendar since June, and it's here.

Here's the why: when the moon goes new and the tides get big, current speed picks up everywhere — over the reefs, through the harbor mouths, across the bar at Charles Island. Bait gets pushed and disoriented, structure fish get aggressive because their feeding windows get compressed into shorter, harder pushes of water, and predator fish start timing their moves to that current instead of just cruising. A PM flood stacking up right at last light is about as good as it gets for bass looking to ambush bait coming off the sandbar. If you've only got one evening this week, that's the one to take.

Stripers have been the story at Charles Island, and it's been honest fishing — not a blitz, but consistent if you're willing to work the tide correctly. The bar off the southwest corner is holding fish on the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide, right as the light starts going flat gold. Guys drifting live eels along the drop-off in eight to fourteen feet have been connecting with schoolies mixed with a few fish pushing thirty inches. If you're throwing plugs, a black Danny or a bone-colored Gibbs pencil worked slow across the bar right at dusk has drawn strikes, especially in that last half hour before it goes fully dark. Stratford Shoal has also been quietly productive — the west side rockpile in fifteen to twenty feet is holding bass that are using the current break to ambush bait getting swept off the shoal. Bucktails with a white or chartreuse Fin-S trailer, bounced slow along bottom on the drift, have out-produced straight plugging out there this week.

Fluke are the mixed bag right now, and I want to be straight about it — the bay bite softened as that water climbed into the upper 70s, and the fish that were holding shallow in Milford Gulf and up in the harbor have thinned out. What's replaced them is a better bite right at the harbor mouths and in the channel edges where cooler water is getting pulled in on the outgoing. Drifting the New Haven Harbor entrance channel in eighteen to twenty-two feet on the last three hours of the ebb has been the move — pink or white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter ounce bucktails, dragged just off bottom, slow enough to feel the structure. Nothing huge — most fish are running fourteen to seventeen inches with the occasional keeper mixed in — but it's steady enough to keep rods bent if you're willing to hunt the deeper edges instead of the flats.

Porgies are doing exactly what porgies do in July, which is save the day when everything else gets fussy. Falkner Island's rockpile on the north side and the rubble field off the New Haven breakwalls have both been holding good numbers in fifteen to twenty-five feet. Green crab and clam on a simple hi-lo rig, fished on the bottom during the slower current around slack tide, has been about as close to a guarantee as this beat offers. Sea bass are mixed in on the same structure — a few nice ones in the fourteen to sixteen inch range coming off Falkner and the rockpile at the east end of Charles Island, biting best on squid strips right as the tide starts to build after slack. Tog fishing has quieted down as the fish spread out and the water's warmed past their comfort zone on the shallower structure — I'd expect that bite to sharpen back up once we get into fall, but for now it's a bonus fish, not a target.

Bluefish have been running through in pockets — nothing like a blitz, but enough that if you're marking bait on the meter around the Milford Gulf edges or off Hammonasset, it's worth a few casts with a diamond jig or a topwater popper before you move on. They've been more of an afternoon fish this week, showing when the sun's high and the bait gets pushed tight against structure.

Looking ahead: if the wind stays out of the south and light like the forecast suggests, I'd put my Saturday evening into the PM flood at Charles Island — that new moon push converging with sunset is the kind of setup you don't get every week. Plan B if the bass don't show is working the fluke drift at the New Haven Harbor mouth on the same tide change, since that bite's been reliable even when the striper bite is off. Either way, watch that spring tide build through the weekend — bigger water usually means better fishing on this beat, as long as you're standing in the right spot when it turns.
