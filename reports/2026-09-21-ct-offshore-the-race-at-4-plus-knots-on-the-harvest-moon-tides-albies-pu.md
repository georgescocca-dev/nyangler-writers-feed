# The Race at 4-plus knots on the Harvest Moon tides: albies push into Plum Gut, bass stack Valiant Rock
*Bigger water is moving bait harder through the rips, and the false albacore invasion everyone's been talking about in Rhode Island just found its way into Connecticut's front door.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — September 21** — _by Capt. Sabrina Cole, Zone Writer_

The Race is running better than four knots on the peak of these Harvest Moon tides, and that kind of current does exactly what it's supposed to do this time of year — it grinds bait through the rip so hard that everything with a mouth stacks up to eat. New London is reading 69.4 degrees, Kings Point over on the western end of the Sound is up at 70.9, and that gradient matters. Warmer water to the west, cleaner and slightly cooler water pushing through the eastern gates at Plum Gut and the Sluiceway — that's the kind of thermal seam that concentrates forage right where the current squeezes down between Orient Point and Plum Island.

We flagged this two weeks back — said to watch for any warm-water intrusion that could nudge pelagics closer to the Connecticut coast — and here it is. The false albacore and bonito push that's been tearing up Block Island and Fishers Island Sound all month has officially found the Gut. Boats working Plum Gut and the water off Great Gull Island and Little Gull Island are into albies now, not just marking them on the sounder. That's a real westward creep, and if the pattern holds through this week's tides, expect it to keep working into the eastern Race and toward Bartlett Reef before it's done.

The albie bite is a slack-water and current-edge game, same as everything else out here. The window right around the top of the flood and the last hour of the ebb — when the rip settles from screaming current down to a manageable push — is when the fish stop chasing bait in every direction and start feeding in a lane you can actually cast into. Epoxy jigs in the 1- to 1.5-ounce range, olive-over-white or that holographic bunker pattern, cast into the boils off the Gut and worked with a fast, erratic retrieve, are getting bit. Small profile matters — these fish are on bay anchovies and rain bait, not big peanuts yet, so if the standard albie jig isn't drawing strikes, downsizing to a Deadly Dick or a smaller epoxy minnow has been the difference for a few boats this week.

Striped bass are the other half of this story, and the bunker migration that's been building from the Jersey grounds up through Montauk is starting to show its face in the Race itself. Race Rock and Valiant Rock are still the spots — wire line trolled with bucktails or umbrella rigs through the deeper scour on the west side of Race Rock on the last two hours of the ebb has been the most consistent producer, with fish in the mid-20s to low 30s mixed in with a few better ones pushing 40. The slack window at Valiant Rock — that stretch where the current drops from a hard push down to barely a knot before it reverses — is still the money time for jigging bucktails vertically along bottom in 40 to 60 feet. Get there fifteen minutes ahead of the actual slack, because the bite often peaks on the last of the dying current, not the dead stop.

Bluefish are riding shotgun on this whole system, and we called that intensifying pattern last week too — more bait getting flushed south and east translating into bigger, meaner blues mixed into the bunker schools. That's holding up. The Race and the water off Race Rock have blues in the 4- to 8-pound class hitting the same bucktails and umbrella rigs meant for bass, and topwater poppers thrown into visible surface activity near Little Gull Island have been drawing violent strikes in the mornings before the current builds.

Fluke are still around but this is the tail end of it. A few boats working the deeper edges of the Sluiceway and the mud bottom west of the Race on the last of the ebb are picking at keepers on white Gulp swimming mullets and bucktail combos, but it's not the numbers game it was a month ago — this bite fades fast once the water starts cooling in earnest, and with the Harvest Moon tides moving so much water, that cooling could accelerate quickly. Black sea bass remain the steady mixed-bag option on the rockpiles and structure around Race Rock and the western edge of Fishers Island — nothing spectacular, but dependable on squid strips and Gulp bait rigged on a two-hook bottom setup in 40 to 70 feet.

Looking at the next few days, the moon is still working in the fleet's favor. Bigger tides mean stronger current, which means better-defined slack windows and more predictable bait movement — that's the whole game out here. If the albie push keeps tracking west the way it has, expect the Sluiceway and the water between Plum Gut and Orient Point to keep improving through the weekend. The bass bite at Race Rock and Valiant Rock should keep building too as more bunker gets pushed into the system from the west, though the fish are still somewhat scattered — this isn't a full-on blitz yet, it's a building pattern. The one thing worth watching is any early-fall cold front. If temps drop hard, the fluke bite will shut down fast and the albies could just as easily blow back east as they came. For now, if there's only one day to fish this week, the slack window at Plum Gut on the incoming tide is the play — albies first, then work the last of the moving water for bass on the drop-off toward the Race.
