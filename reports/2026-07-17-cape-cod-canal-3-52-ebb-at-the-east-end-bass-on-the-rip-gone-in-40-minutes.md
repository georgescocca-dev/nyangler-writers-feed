# 3:52 Ebb at the East End: Bass on the Rip, Gone in 40 Minutes
*Three days past the new moon and the Canal's still running spring-tide hot, but the window at Scusset is shrinking fast and you need to be standing there when it opens.*

**SANDWICH, MA — July 17** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

3.8 knots. That's what max ebb read off the east end this morning at 6:47, three days past the new moon and still riding that spring tide push before we slide into neaps by the weekend. I stood on the rocks below the Sagamore Bridge watching the water pour out of that ditch like someone pulled a plug, and for about forty minutes — from roughly 5:50 to 6:30 — the rip off Scusset stacked up bait and bent rods. After that the current eased, the rip flattened out, and the bite went with it. That's the Canal in July. You don't fish the tide, you fish the forty minutes inside the tide.

Here's what's been happening. The bass have been sitting on the seam where the outflow meets the slower water along the beach, right where the sand drops off into that scoured channel. Guys throwing white bucktails, 1.5 to 2 ounces depending on how hard she's running, casting up-current and letting the jig swing through the seam before working it back — that's been the play. A couple of slot fish in the 30-inch range came off that rip Tuesday morning on that exact drift, and I heard of one over 36 inches taken on a chunk of mackerel fished on the bottom right at the base of the rip during the same window. Live eels after dark have been the better bet for the bigger fish — one 40-incher reported off the herring run side around 9 PM on a slow-swum eel, fished on the last of the ebb before the current went soft.

The mackerel run is done, though — that's a May-June story, and anybody still tying on tins expecting green backs is wasting casts. What's replaced it is squid. Small squid have been showing under the lights at night along both ends, and that's exactly what those bigger bass are keying on after dark. If you've got squid jigs or soft-bodied squid imitations in white or pink, work them slow near the bottom on the last two hours of the ebb, especially off the west end near Hog Island and the Mashnee flats where the current softens up before it reverses.

Speaking of the west end — that's been the more consistent water this week, if less explosive. The seams off Mashnee Island and Wings Neck have been holding schoolies and a mix of slot fish through the last two hours of the flood, when the incoming current pushes bait up against the rocks and the drop-off along the Cleveland Ledge side. Bucktails again, but lighter — 3/4 to 1 ounce — dragged slow along bottom, tipped with a strip of squid. Fluke have been mixed in on that same structure, a few keepers to 20 inches coming off the sandy patches between Mashnee and Phinneys Harbor on the last of the flood into the top of the tide, drifted with Gulp on light jig heads. Nothing to write home about size-wise, but steady enough that it's worth a stop if the east end rip has gone quiet on you.

Bluefish have finally shown some teeth in the mix — choppers in the 3 to 5 pound class crashing bait on both ends, mostly on the same tide stages as the bass, which means if you're marking bait busting on the surface during that ebb window at Scusset, don't be shy about throwing a wire leader and a Deadly Dick. They'll clear a school of peanut bunker in about ten casts and then vanish just as fast.

No albies, no bonito yet — too early for that, and I wouldn't waste time looking. That push usually doesn't show in the Canal until late August, once the water in the bay and Vineyard Sound climbs into the high 60s and pushes bait tight to structure. I'll start watching for false albacore signs closer to the third week of August, same as every year.

Honest assessment: this is not a wire-to-wire bite right now. The current is still strong enough off the spring tide to create real windows, but they're short — twenty, thirty, forty minutes around max ebb or max flood, and dead in between. I fished the midday slack Tuesday just to confirm what I already knew and got nothing but sunburn. If you're driving distance to fish this water, don't show up at noon and expect fish. Show up with the tide chart open and be standing on the rocks fifteen minutes before max current, because that rip line doesn't wait for anybody.

Looking ahead — we're sliding into neap tides by Sunday and Monday, which means less current, softer rips, and a quieter bite on both ends. That's when I shift tactics: smaller profile lures, slower presentations, and more patience working the seams instead of relying on raw current speed to concentrate the bait. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take Thursday's 5:40 AM ebb at the east end — still enough push off the tail of this spring cycle to turn the rip on, cool early light, and bait already staged from the last few days of squid activity. After that, I'd bank on the west end seams carrying the week through the weak tides, because that structure produces even when the current gets lazy. Watch the clock, watch the color of the water going through the pilings, and don't waste your best casts on slack.
