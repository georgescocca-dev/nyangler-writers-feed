# Bay bait's balled up and nervous — first albies can't be far
*Rain bait showering off Cuttyhunk and silversides thick in Quick's Hole have every rip in the corridor primed for the season's first false albacore and bonito.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — July 24** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

The bait's telling me something's close. I ran the west end of Quick's Hole Tuesday morning on the last of the ebb and the silversides were balled so tight against the rocks they looked like a school of anchovies from a Gulf Stream trip, not a Buzzards Bay rip in late July. Terns were working them from above, and every few minutes you'd see that nervous flash-and-scatter that means something's under them pushing up. I never got a hookup, but I didn't fish it long — I was there to look, and what I saw was enough to keep me up at night thinking about Friday.

We flagged this corridor last week as one of the better bets in southern New England for the season's first albies and bonito, and standing on that rip Tuesday, nothing about that call feels like a stretch. Water's sitting in that upper-60s-to-low-70s window that's historically the trigger down here, and the bait behavior — tight, nervous, showery — is exactly what precedes the first scout schools at Robinson's Hole and off the backside of Cuttyhunk. No confirmed albie or bonito yet in my zone as of this week. I want to be straight about that. But every year it starts with bait acting exactly like this one is acting right now.

While we wait on the main event, the bluefish have picked up the slack and then some. Menemsha Bight has had bunker schools getting worked over hard on the last two hours of the incoming, blues in the 3-to-6-pound class blowing up on the surface close enough to shore that guys are casting from the jetty rocks. Poppers are the move — a white Super Spook or a Yo-Zuri Sashimi popper worked with an aggressive walk-the-dog retrieve draws vicious strikes when the blues are actively feeding, and when they get finicky, switching down to a 1-ounce diamond jig with a tube teaser above it will still connect. This bluefish push matches what's happening the length of the coast right now — bunker schools pinned by warm surface water, blues working them without letup — and there's no reason to expect it to slow here as long as those bunker pods keep holding tight against the Vineyard shoreline.

Striped bass have gone quiet in the way they always do this time of year. Daytime effort in Buzzards Bay proper has been mostly a formality — a few fish picked off deep structure around the Weepecket Islands and the drop-offs south of Naushon on bucktails bounced slow along bottom, but nothing consistent. The better program has been working the rips at Robinson's Hole and Woods Hole itself right at first light and again after dark, when stripers slide up onto the current edges to feed before the sun pushes them back down. A 1-ounce bucktail tipped with a strip of squid, drifted through the seam where the fast water meets the slower eddy, has been the most dependable presentation — slow, natural, right on the bottom. Live eels after dark around Tarpaulin Cove have also produced, though you need to commit to the late shift; nothing's happening on that program before 9 or 10 PM.

Fluke have been steady but unspectacular — the kind of bite where you catch a lot of shorts and have to grind for keepers. The best water has been the deeper edges off Cuttyhunk, 25 to 35 feet, and the channel drop inside Quick's Hole on the tail end of the ebb when the current is still moving enough to keep bait pinned but not so hard you can't hold bottom. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a squid strip, dragged just fast enough to feel the tail kick — that combo has out-produced bare bucktails two-to-one in my boat this week. Scup fishing remains the reliable fallback around the Woods Hole pilings and the rocks off Nonamesset — small hooks, squid strips, bottom rigs, and you'll fill a cooler in an hour if you want dinner guaranteed.

Tide-wise, we're coming off a moon that's kept the current honest — good push through the holes, not the dead slack water that makes albie scouting a waste of gas. That current strength matters enormously for the next week: albies and bonito, when they show, want moving water and bait getting funneled through the rips, not sitting in a calm bay. Robinson's Hole and the west end of Quick's Hole on a hard-running tide are where I'll be spending my mornings through the end of the month, small metal in hand — a Deadly Dick or a half-ounce Albie Snax — ready the moment that first tail slice shows up.

If I had one day this week, I'd fish the last two hours of the ebb at Robinson's Hole, right where the current squeezes between the islands and the bait gets pinched into a narrow lane. That's the classic setup for the first arrivals, and with the bait already acting spooked and balled up the way it is, I don't think we're more than a few days out. Bring the light spinning gear, bring patience, and don't be surprised if the first fish you see busting bait this season isn't a bluefish at all.
