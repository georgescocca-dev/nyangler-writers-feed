# Halfway Rock holds its rhythm: pollock and bass steady while the mackerel start to thin
*Groundfish are still schooling the ledges on the slack, but cooling nights are nudging the bay toward its fall pattern.*

**PORTLAND, ME — August 31** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

Stand on the rail off Halfway Rock on a clear morning now and you can see the season turning without a thermometer. The water's still that deep green-blue of late summer, but there's a crispness to the light, and the terns that spent July working bait balls tight against the ledge are ranging wider, chasing mackerel schools that are starting to thin and scatter toward open water. That's the tell. Casco Bay in the last week of August is holding its pattern — but it's a pattern with an expiration date, and the smart money is already planning for what comes after Labor Day.

The groundfish bite around Halfway Rock and the outer ledges off Peaks and Bailey Island has been the most dependable thing going. Pollock in the 3-to-8-pound class are stacked over the rockpiles in 50 to 70 feet, and they're eating jigs — diamond jigs in the 2-to-4-ounce range, worked with a lift-and-flutter through the water column on the last of the ebb and into the early flood, when the current slows enough to hold bottom contact without a pound of lead. Cod have been mixed in with them, mostly shorts and a scattering of keeper-class fish in the 24-to-28-inch range, holding tighter to bottom structure than the pollock. Haddock have shown in modest numbers deeper off the Rock, out past 80 feet, but they're not thick — a fish or two on a drift rather than a program you can count on.

Striped bass have settled into the holding pattern we flagged last week — steady light-tackle action rather than blitz conditions, and that's exactly what's playing out. Fish are working the ledges off Bailey Island and around Cape Small on the moving tide, particularly the two hours either side of high, when current pushes bait against structure and bass pin it there. Live mackerel on a fish-finder rig has been the go-to for the better fish, but soft plastics — 6-inch white or bone Slug-Go style baits on a light jighead — are drawing plenty of strikes for anglers working the rockier stretches around Harpswell's outer islands. Two Lights continues to produce from the rocks for surf guys willing to work the tide right; the bite there is best on the incoming, when bait gets pushed up against the ledge rock and stripers move in tight to ambush it.

Bluefish have been present but inconsistent in the bay itself — more of a factor closer to the mouth, around the outer islands where warmer water pushes in on the tide, than back toward Portland. When they show, they're chopping mackerel and small pogies on the surface, and a straight retrieve with a Kastmaster or a tin will draw fast strikes, but the window tends to be short and tied closely to bait presence rather than time of day.

The mackerel run itself, which the inner bay through May and June and carried through much of the summer, is doing what it does this time of year — thinning out and pushing toward deeper, cooler water outside the islands. Sabiki rigs are still producing around Peaks Island and in the channels near Portland Harbor, but the schools are looser and moving more, which means less standing on one spot and more covering water to find them. That mackerel movement matters beyond the bait bucket — it's mackerel density that keeps striped bass and bluefish committed to feeding in a given stretch of the bay, and as that bait pulls back toward the outer ledges and Halfway Rock itself, expect the predator activity to follow it out.

Looking at the next several days, conditions favor staying with what's working rather than chasing a shift that hasn't fully arrived. Nights are cooling, which will accelerate the bay's transition toward the fall groundfish and striper push, but that transition tends to happen in stages here, not all at once — a few degrees of surface cooling, then another push of bait, then another wave of fish moving toward structure. If there's only one day to fish this week, work Halfway Rock on the slack tide for pollock and cod, then swing over to the Bailey Island ledges on the incoming for stripers — that double-header has been the most consistent combination on the water this month. Watch the wind out of the northeast; a sustained blow will dirty up the shallower ledge water fast and push the bite deeper and further out toward the Rock. Absent that, the pattern holding steady through early fall looks like the safer bet, with the real question being how fast the bait keeps sliding toward the bay's mouth — because wherever the mackerel go this September, the bass and bluefish won't be far behind.
