# The Race at 3.8 Knots: Neap Squeeze Sends Bass to the Wire at Valiant Rock
*As the tide range collapses into Wednesday's neap, the daytime bite goes deep and slow — and the real action shifts to the dark hours at Plum Gut.*

**THE RACE, CT — August 3** — _by Capt. Sabrina Cole, Zone Writer_

3.8 knots. That's what Race Rock was running on the last big ebb I fished, down from the 5-plus knot walls we were bucking three weeks ago on the full moon. That number tells you everything about this week — we're sliding into the neap on August 5-6, and every rip from Valiant Rock to the Sluiceway is going to feel it. Softer current means the bass stop stacking tight on the up-current edge of structure and start spreading out, which means you have to work harder and think slower to find them.

Valiant Rock has been the honest spot all week. Slack at the Rock ran right around 10:20 in the morning and 4:45 in the afternoon on my last two trips out there, and that window — fifteen, twenty minutes either side of dead slack — is when I've been marking the biggest concentrations of bass sitting tight to the rockpile in 55 to 70 feet. Wire line with a 2 1/2-ounce bucktail dressed in white bucktail hair and a strip of pork rind, trolled at a slow walk right through that transition from ebb to slack, has been the difference-maker. Three fish over 30 pounds came off that program for me this week, all on the last twenty minutes of the ebb before the water went dead. Once the current builds back the other way past a knot and a half, the bite shuts off fast — the fish drop off the structure and scatter into the deeper holes on either side of the Rock, and you're better off picking up the sea bass rods.

We flagged this pattern last week — said as the tides softened, bass would compress into the strongest current hours and the deepest, coolest structure, and that night eels at the Gut and the bridges would become the play. That's exactly what's happening. Plum Gut after dark has been quietly better than the daytime bite at the Race this week. Live eels, 10 to 14 inches, fished on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in 35 to 45 feet on the last two hours of the incoming — that's producing the better class of fish, 34 to 40 inches, with almost no daylight shorts mixed in. Water temp at New London is sitting at 69.6, which is on the cool side for early August, and that's keeping bass comfortable enough to feed a little later into the morning than you'd expect this time of year. Kings Point to the west is running 72.9, so there's a real gradient building across the Sound, and that cooler water on our end of things is a gift right now.

Fluke fishing has been the most consistent thing going, and it's not close. Doormat-class fish are showing on the edges of the Sluiceway and along the channel drop on the west side of Plum Gut, 40 to 60 feet, drifting 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails with a spearing teaser six inches up the leader. I had a stretch Tuesday where four drifts produced four fish over 5 pounds, along with a steady pick of shorts that kept the rods bent even when the big ones weren't showing. This bite doesn't care much about the neap — fluke are still locked onto the moving water regardless of speed, and with the season at its peak right now, I'd put this as the most reliable meat-in-the-cooler play on the whole beat.

Bluefish have been around but patchy — a few schools of choppers busting bait off Great Gull Island in the early morning, gone by 8 o'clock. This fishery is entirely bait-driven right now; wherever the bunker pods hold, the blues show up within hours and then move on when the bait shifts. I haven't found a concentration worth building a whole trip around this week, but if you're out there anyway working the rips, keep a metal jig rigged and ready.

Black sea bass remain the fallback everybody can count on. Little Gull Island's rockpile and the structure around Valiant Rock are both holding fish in the 15 to 20 inch range on squid strips and Gulp on a high-low rig, 50 to 65 feet, any stage of the tide. It's not glamorous but it fills a cooler when the rip goes quiet on you between slacks.

No albies or bonito yet — I want to be straight about that, because I know guys are already asking. The ingredients are close. Temperature breaks are building at Montauk and Block Island right now, and that's exactly the setup that usually tips the first scattered fish into the Gut and around the Sluiceway. I'd put good odds on the first confirmed reports coming out of this stretch of the Sound within the next week to ten days, especially if this softer current pattern holds and lets warmer water pool up around the islands. I'll have epoxy jigs rigged and ready every trip starting now — no sense being unprepared when they show.

Looking ahead: expect the neap to keep squeezing current speeds through midweek, which means lighter jigs, slower trolling speeds, and tighter attention to the actual slack window rather than the tide chart's general guess. If you've only got one trip in you, I'd fish the evening slack at Valiant Rock into the start of the new ebb, then run the eels at Plum Gut after dark. That's where the fish are, and that's where I'll be.
