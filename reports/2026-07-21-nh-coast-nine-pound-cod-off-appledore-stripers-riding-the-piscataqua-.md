# Nine-Pound Cod off Appledore, Stripers Riding the Piscataqua Neap
*Thirteen miles of coastline doesn't sound like much until you've spent a July morning working the slack at the Shoals and still made it back before the tourists find parking.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — July 21** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People hear thirteen miles of coastline and figure there's nothing to talk about up here. I've fished New Jersey with guys who cover more water in one tide than I do in a season, and I still put a better box of fish on the dock most weeks. You don't need more coastline. You need to know what's under the water you've got. My Lund's twenty feet, open console, no frills — but I fish it like it's rigged for the canyons, because six miles offshore of Rye the bottom does things most guys never see because they never bother running out there.

This week that meant the Isles of Shoals, and the Shoals have been good to the guys who respect the slack. We're coming off the first-quarter neap, which knocked the current down around White Island and the ledges off Appledore, and that's exactly when you want to be sitting over structure with a jig in your hand instead of fighting to hold bottom. I had a morning on the Appledore ledges in about 75 feet where the haddock showed first — schoolies mixed with a few keepers on diamond jigs tipped with a strip of mackerel belly — and then the cod came through on the change, biggest one going nine pounds on a plain white/red jig worked slow off bottom. Nothing dramatic, but steady enough that four rods kept bending through the last hour of the ebb. Halibut Rocks and the Sisters have been holding similar fish in that 60-90 foot range. If you're marking bait balls on the sounder before you drop, that's your green light — cooler Gulf of Maine water is keeping mackerel schools thick and tight to structure out there, and where the mackerel sit, the groundfish sit under them.

Speaking of mackerel, they've been stacked from the Shoals in tight to Odiorne Point, and jigging a string of six for an hour will fill a bait bucket and half your dinner plate in the same trip. That cooler water column is the whole story right now — while the water south of Boston pushes stripers into a strictly dawn-and-dark pattern, we're still getting daytime bites up here because the Gulf of Maine hasn't let this stretch get soupy-warm the way the Sound has. We called this exact pattern last week — said summer striper fishing would carry through August up here — and that's holding up so far, no surprise given how stable this water column has stayed.

Inshore, the Piscataqua has been the more consistent bass producer than the open beaches. Current seams below the Memorial Bridge and down along Pierce Island have been holding fish on the last two hours of the incoming, with schoolies to mid-20-inch class eating swimming plugs and soft-plastic paddletails worked with the current instead of against it. A few better fish, high-20s to low-30s, have come off the deeper water near the Route 1 bridge on live mackerel drifted on a fish-finder rig — makes sense given how much bait we're seeing stacked in that river right now.

Hampton Harbor has been the tide-dependent play it always is in July. The inlet mouth on the last of the outgoing, when the water's pouring out past the jetty and pushing bait with it, has been giving up decent numbers of schoolie bass to guys throwing white bucktails and Sluggos in the 5-inch range. Wait for that stage of the tide specifically — fish it too early on the ebb and you're just casting into slack water with nothing moving. Rye Harbor's channel has had similar action on the same tide stage, plus a few bluefish mixed in chasing the same bait pods, snapping off soft plastics if you're not running wire or at least a heavier leader.

Bluefish overall have been more of a presence than a headline up here — nothing like the blitzes you're hearing about further south where bunker schools are stacking blues right on the beach. Ours have been scattered fish working the harbor mouths and Great Boars Head on incoming water, chopping bait on the surface for twenty-minute windows before disappearing. If you catch that window, small metal — quarter-ounce Deadly Dicks or similar — worked fast will get bit. If you miss it, you're just casting at empty water.

Flounder have been a quieter story, but Hampton Harbor's channel edges and the flats inside Rye Harbor have produced decent numbers on standard flounder rigs with sandworms, best on the slower stages of the tide when the current isn't ripping bait rigs sideways.

Looking ahead, I don't see a reason for this to change much through the coming week. The neap tide should keep softening current at the Shoals through the weekend, which means more slack-water windows to work the ledges without fighting the drift, and the cooler water column isn't going anywhere fast enough to push our stripers into a strictly nocturnal pattern like they're dealing with south of us. If I had one morning to spend, I'd run to Appledore early, work the slack for cod and haddock, then swing back into the Piscataqua on the tail of the incoming for bass before the wind picks up in the afternoon. That's the trip that's been working. No reason to overthink it while it still does.
