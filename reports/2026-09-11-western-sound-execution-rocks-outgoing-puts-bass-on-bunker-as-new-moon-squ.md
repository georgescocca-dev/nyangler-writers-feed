# Execution Rocks Outgoing Puts Bass on Bunker as New Moon Squeezes the Bait Tight
*The fall bite everyone's been waiting on finally showed a pulse this week — striped bass committing hard to bunker schools from Throgs Neck to Matinecock on the stronger tides.*

**CITY ISLAND, NY — September 11** — _by Frank "The Sound Pro" Marinaro, Zone Writer_

Fella came into the shop Tuesday wanting to know if the bunker pods off Matinecock were as thick as his brother-in-law claimed. Told him they were thicker — and that the bigger question wasn't the bait, it was whether the bass had finally decided to eat it instead of just swimming through it. By Wednesday afternoon, with the tide ripping off Execution Rocks, that question got answered.

The September 10 new moon did exactly what a new moon in early September is supposed to do on this end of the Sound — it cranked up the tidal exchange and started squeezing bunker schools tight against structure from the Throgs Neck Bridge out through Stepping Stones and down toward Matinecock Point. We flagged this coming last week, said watch the bunker pods as the moon builds because the blitz-style feeding was going to show up on the stronger exchanges, and that's precisely what's happening now. This isn't the scattered, pick-at-stragglers pattern that had bass guys frustrated through August. This is fish committing.

Water's still holding up warm for the calendar — Kings Point is reading right around 71 degrees, with the gradient dropping as you move east toward New London in the high 60s. That west-to-east spread matters because it means the western Sound, our water, is still the warmest stretch and the bait hasn't been forced to move yet. It's stacking instead of scattering, which is exactly why Execution Rocks on the back half of the outgoing has been the most consistent call of the week. Current rips hard around those rocks on the ebb, bunker gets funneled and disoriented in the wash, and bass — some genuine mid-20-pound fish mixed with a lot of schoolies — have been sitting right on that edge waiting for the current to do the work.

The presentation that's been producing isn't complicated. Guys working live bunker on a fish-finder rig, 20 to 30 feet down off the rock pile, hooked through the nose or the gills depending on how lively the bait's staying in the well, have been getting bit consistently starting an hour into the outgoing and running through the last two hours of the tide. If you don't have livies, a bunker spoon trolled slow along that same current seam, or a big white bucktail with a swim shad trailer bounced through the rip, has been drawing strikes too. Don't be afraid to fish it heavy — 3 to 4 ounces to hold bottom contact in that current.

Stepping Stones is playing the same tune, especially on the last two hours of the flood as bait pushes up out of the Throgs Neck channel and gets pinned against the light structure. That's traditionally been a spring highway for stripers moving through, but the fall version works almost identically in reverse — bait moving with the tide, bass stacking on the downcurrent side waiting to ambush. Diamond jigs in the 2 to 3-ounce range, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right off bottom, have taken some quality fish there this week, along with a scattering of bluefish mixed in that are starting to show more weight than the snappers we've had all summer.

Speaking of snappers — they're still thick in Little Neck Bay and up in Manhasset Bay, and that's not just a kids'-rod story. Heavy snapper numbers in the back bays are historically the tell that bigger bluefish are coming behind them as the water eases down through the 60s, and with New London already reading nearly two degrees cooler than our end, that cooling trend is on its way west. Give it two to three weeks and expect those snapper counts to translate into real adult blues showing up on the same structure the bass are using now.

Fluke has been the disappointing half of the report, and there's no sense dressing it up. The keeper-grade fish have largely bailed for deeper, cooler water offshore, and what's left in the Sound proper — off Hart Island, along the Throgs Neck flats, in the deeper holes of Hempstead Harbor — is mostly short fish. If you're set on fluke, fish the deepest water you can find inside the zone, work white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails slow along the bottom, and expect to cull a lot of shorts for every keeper. This bite is going to keep thinning through the month as the last of the mature fish slide out toward the deeper exits.

Porgies and blackfish are the honest, steady part of the report right now. As the water starts its seasonal cool-down, both species are locking onto hard structure — the rock piles off Execution Rocks, the mussel beds in Hempstead Harbor, the rubble edges around Hart Island. Green crab and clam on a standard porgy rig or a simple blackfish jig has been putting fish in the box consistently, no drama, no complicated presentation needed. This is the kind of bite that just keeps giving through fall as the water temperature drops into the fish's comfort zone.

Looking ahead, the next few days should keep building on what started this week. The moon's still close enough to new that the tidal push stays strong through the weekend, and if the wind stays out of the north-to-northwest and holds the water clean, expect the Execution Rocks and Stepping Stones pattern to hold or even sharpen as more bunker schools funnel down from the east. If you've only got one tide to fish, make it the last two hours of the outgoing at Execution Rocks — that's where the current, the bait, and the bass are lining up most reliably right now. Keep an eye on Matinecock too; if those bunker schools that have been holding there start getting pushed by predator pressure, that's usually the next spot to light up after Execution Rocks starts working.
