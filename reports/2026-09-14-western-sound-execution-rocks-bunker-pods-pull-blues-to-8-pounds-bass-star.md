# Execution Rocks Bunker Pods Pull Blues to 8 Pounds, Bass Starting to Lean In
*The bait's stacked from Matinecock to the Rocks and the fall predators are finally showing up to eat it.*

**CITY ISLAND, NY — September 14** — _by Frank "The Sound Pro" Marinaro, Zone Writer_

Guy came into the shop Thursday asking if the bunker schools off Execution Rocks were "the real deal or just bait sitting there." Fair question. There's a difference between bunker parked and bunker getting worked, and this week we crossed that line. The pods that have been stacked from Matinecock Point down through the Rocks all summer are finally drawing blood, and the bluefish showed up to prove it.

Water's still carrying a decent gradient across the Western and Central Sound — Kings Point reading close to 71 degrees, New London down around 69 — and that half-degree-per-mile drop from west to east is exactly why the bite is loading up on our end first. Warmer water holds bait longer, bait holds predators longer, and right now the bunker schools have nowhere to go but tighten up against the reefs and rockpiles as the nights cool. We flagged this two weeks back — said the bunker pods would tighten under the building moon and the blitz-style feeding would follow. That's what's happening now off Execution Rocks and the flats north of Matinecock.

Bluefish are the headline right now, and they're not the snapper-sized nuisance fish from July. Boats working bunker schools off the Rocks on the last two hours of the ebb are into blues from 4 to 8 pounds, sometimes bigger, busting bait right on the surface in the rip that forms as the tide pulls hard past the light. Snag-and-drop with a treble hook ahead of a bunker spoon is still the most efficient way to connect — snag one out of the pod, let it flutter down on a fluttering drop, and hang on. Guys without a snag rig are doing fine chunking fresh bunker on a fish-finder rig, 20 to 30 feet down, anchored just off the northwest corner of the Rocks where the current stacks bait against the rock.

Stripers are the story to watch, not the story that's fully arrived yet. We called this shift coming — said the fall migration would start showing real teeth as the new moon tightened up the bait dynamics, and there's early confirmation of that now. Reports out of Stepping Stones on the last two hours of the outgoing have bass to 28 inches hitting bucktails worked slow along bottom, 1 to 1.5 ounces, white or chartreuse, bounced right through the rip line where the light marks the edge of the flood channel. It's not a blitz yet — it's a handful of quality fish per tide, mostly at first light or the last light of the day — but the pattern is textbook early fall staging. As more peanut bunker and mullet get pushed out of Manhasset Bay and Little Neck Bay over the next two weeks, expect those numbers to build steadily rather than explode overnight.

Hart Island and the flats off Throgs Neck Bridge are still holding fluke, though that bite is thinning out the way it always does mid-September. Boats drifting the deeper edges off the bridge in 25 to 35 feet are still picking fluke to 4 pounds on white Gulp swimming mullets paired with 3/4-ounce bucktails, best on the last of the incoming when the current's still got some push but hasn't gone full send. Worth a stop if you're already out there for bass or blues, but don't plan a whole trip around it — that window's closing fast as water temps ease and fluke start sliding toward deeper water outside the Sound.

Porgies remain the dependable player on the reefs off Matinecock Point and around the rockpiles near Hart Island. Small hooks, sandworms or clam, bottom rigs — that bite hasn't changed all season and it's not going to until the water really drops. Good option for a Sunday with kids or when the wind kills the bass bite up top. Weakfish have been quiet — a few scattered reports from deeper holes near the Throgs Neck pilings, nothing worth planning around yet.

Looking at the next few days, the setup favors more of the same with a chance to build. Tidal range is increasing as we push toward the next moon phase, and that's exactly the mechanism that turns a slow bunker push into a real blitz — more current means more bait getting pinned against structure, which means more predators committing instead of just picking at stragglers. If the wind stays out of the north and northwest and doesn't churn the surface, the outgoing tide at Execution Rocks in the two hours around sunrise is the highest-percentage window on the board right now for blues, with a real shot at a striper mixed in if you're working the deeper edge of the rip rather than the white water on top.

For bass specifically, Stepping Stones on the dropping tide remains the pick until something better shows itself closer to the bridge. Keep the bucktail heavy enough to hold bottom in that current — anything lighter than an ounce gets swept off the structure before it has a chance to get bit. And keep an eye on Matinecock; if the bunker schools there start getting pushed the way the Rocks pods have been, that stretch could be next in line for the same treatment.
