# Fort Trumbull Ebb: Stripers Down Deep, Bluefish Own the Top
*The Thames has stratified hard this week — cool water underneath, warm slick on top, and the bite has split right along that line.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 3** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

You can see the seam now without even dropping a thermometer. Stand on the Fort Trumbull point around the top of the ebb and watch the surface — it's gone glassy, almost oily, that flat warm sheen you get in early August once the sun's had all July to work on it. But drop a bucktail down eight, ten feet along that rip line off the point and it's a different river down there. Cooler, tighter, more current push. That's where the stripers have gone, and they're staying there through most of the daylight hours.

New London's reading around 69 degrees at the surface, which sounds modest until you remember how that number sits on top of a harbor that doesn't flush fast. The Thames holds heat in its upper few feet and lets the current do the work underneath, and right now that stratification is dictating everything. Bass are down in the green water hugging the channel edges — off the sub base pier faces, along the Gold Star Bridge pilings, and in that deep hole below Fort Trumbull where the river narrows and the current really cranks on the ebb. They're feeding, but they've gone mostly nocturnal and low-light. Best window I've had this week was forty minutes either side of last light, drifting live eels and chunked bunker on fish-finder rigs right along the base of the bridge pilings, letting the current sweep the bait into the seam instead of anchoring dead on it. Twenty-six to thirty-two inch fish, a handful of shorts mixed in, nothing over the slot but steady if you're patient and you're fishing the tide change correctly — start that drift on the last hour of the ebb when the current's still got some shoulder to it.

Daytime, if you want stripers, you have to go get them in the shade — under the bridge structure itself, tight to the pilings where the sun never quite reaches bottom, jigging bucktails and soft plastic paddletails straight down on 3/4 to 1-ounce heads. Slow lift-and-drop right against the concrete. That's producing better than anything worked out in open water in the middle of the day.

Bluefish, on the other hand, want the warm top layer and they're not shy about it. This is the pattern we flagged last week — said the porgy and bluefish bite around the sub base and river mouth would hold steady while bass activity built with the tide, and that's exactly how it's playing out. Blues are running the surface from the harbor mouth out past Pleasure Beach, blowing up on peanut bunker and small mullet in a foot of white water early in the morning. Diamond jigs, half-ounce to an ounce, worked with a fast erratic retrieve, or metal — Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — cast right into the slicks. Nothing subtle needed. These aren't the biggest blues of the year but there's real numbers of them, two to four pounds, and they'll keep you busy for an hour at first light before they slide off with the bait.

Porgies remain the honest bread-and-butter right now, and if you want a bend in the rod without overthinking tide charts, this is it. Anchor up on the hard bottom off the sub base, or drift the flats outside Fort Trumbull in 15 to 25 feet, sandworms and clam on a hi-lo rig, small hooks, light sinkers. They're stacked and cooperative regardless of tide stage, which makes them the fallback when the stripers are being difficult about their schedule. Ocean Beach side has had decent porgy scratching too, a little shallower, 12 to 18 feet, same rig.

What's driving all of this is pretty simple current mechanics layered on top of a warm surface mass that doesn't want to mix. The Thames is a working river — current through the Gold Star Bridge and around Fort Trumbull is strong enough on a good ebb to keep bottom water turning over even while the top three or four feet just sits there and cooks in the sun. That's your two-tier fishery right now: blues and porgies up top and in the mid-column, bass down deep and mostly after dark.

Looking ahead, we're building toward the new moon, and that means the tides are going to get some real muscle back into them over the next several days. Stronger current through the Fort Trumbull rips and around the bridge pilings should push more bait through those seams and could be enough to pull some stripers up onto a more traditional dawn and dusk pattern instead of strictly after dark — I've seen that shift happen before when the tidal push builds enough to break up the surface layer a little. It's not a guarantee the bass go shallow again, but if I had one evening to fish this week, I'd take the building tide on the ebb at Fort Trumbull, eels drifted deep, and stay out through last light into full dark. That's where the better fish have been, and stronger current only helps that pattern hold.
