# Ran the West Wall at Wilmington — Six Yellowfin and a Mahi for the Trouble
*A hard call to skip the chunk grounds and troll the temp break paid off, but only after the sun came up and the squid showed themselves.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 17** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday morning to run straight to Wilmington instead of splitting the difference at the Sausages, and it cost us an extra forty minutes of fuel each way. Here's the thing about running a canyon boat out of Cape May in mid-July — you can't afford to guess. The eddy that's been sitting off the Wilmington elbow has held its position for going on ten days now, which in this business is as close to a sure thing as you get. When an eddy holds that long, the temperature break sharpens up, bait gets pinned against it, and the tuna stop wandering. So we ran the numbers, decided the break was worth the extra diesel, and pointed the bow at the 100-fathom curve on the west wall, working down toward 38°28' N, 72°38' W where the color change was the cleanest I've seen all season — hard blue water butting up against a greener push from the shelf.

We started on the troll at first light with a standard spread — naked ballyhoo and small Green Machines off the short riggers, a spreader bar down the center, and a bigger skirted ballyhoo out on the shotgun for anything wandering wide. For the first two hours, nothing. Marks on the sounder, bait balls stacked at sixty to ninety feet, but the tuna weren't committing. That's typical for this time of year when the sun's high and the water's calm — yellowfin will sit under the bait and sulk rather than come up and eat a trolled bait in flat, bright conditions. Around 9:30 the wind put a little ripple on the surface, and that's when it turned. We picked up four yellowfin in about ninety minutes, all in the 25 to 40-pound range, plus one better fish north of 50 that ate the long bar. Every one of them came off the edge itself, right where the color changed color-and-clarity, not out in the blue and not back in the green.

The squid are the story right now, and anybody working this canyon needs to pay attention to that. We're seeing good numbers of shortfin squid on the meter overnight, and that's dragging the tuna up shallower after dark and keeping them close to the break at dawn. If you've got the stomach for it, a night chunk drift over that same temp line — butterflied bunker chunks with a few whole squid mixed in, fished on 30 to 50-pound fluoro leader — has been out-producing the troll after the sun climbs. We tried it Wednesday night in about 850 feet of water, set up a slow drift with the current pushing us along the break, and picked away at three more yellowfin between midnight and 3 a.m. Slower fishing than the morning bite, but more consistent bite-to-hookup ratio. If I had to pick one program for a full day right now, I'd troll dawn through mid-morning, then either head in or switch to a chunk drift if you're staying over.

Mahi have been a bonus, not a target, which is about right for mid-July. We picked one nice bull off a mat of sargassum weed sitting right on the edge — 38 pounds, came up under a hookless teaser and got a pitch bait for his trouble. If you see any floating grass or a lobster highflyer out there, it's worth a five-minute detour. Wahoo have been quieter than I'd like this week — one fish boated on a wire-line rigged Ilander trolled deep off Poor Man's, but that's the only report I trust. White marlin have shown some interest — a handful of shots on dredge teasers and dink ballyhoo skirted small, but hookup rates were poor. We went 1-for-4 on marlin bites Tuesday, which is honest work, not a hot bite. Bluefin have been mostly a midshore story rather than canyon business — schoolies showing in the 20 to 25-fathom range closer to Ocean City and Indian River, which is a shorter, cheaper run if tuna are your only goal and you don't need the bigger class fish.

Water temps on the break have been sitting in that 74 to 76-degree range where it's crisp, not blown out — exactly what you want for a clean bite window rather than a soupy, disorganized edge. The moon's swinging up toward full over the next several days, which historically pushes more feeding activity into darkness and can make the daytime troll bite spottier while the night chunk stays steady. That's the pattern I'd plan around this week.

If the eddy holds its position — and there's no reason yet to think it won't, given how stable it's been — I'd keep running Wilmington's west wall and work the same troll-then-chunk combination. If it starts sliding east like these things eventually do, I'd shift attention south toward Baltimore Canyon, where the structure sets up similarly and you're not chasing a break that's moved out from under you. Either way, I wouldn't burn the fuel unless you've got a read on where that temperature line is sitting that morning. Guessing on an eddy costs you money and daylight both.
