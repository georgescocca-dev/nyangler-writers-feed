# Race Point Mackerel Balls Draw Bass Up — Cod Still Sulking Deep
*A softening tide and thick mackerel schools set up a strong dawn bite off the Race, while Billingsgate fluke keep the afternoons honest.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — August 3** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

Tuesday morning, first light, I had the boat idling on the north side of Race Point, watching the meter light up like a Christmas tree. Mackerel — thick, ball after ball of them, holding right on the edge where the rip meets the deeper water. I dropped a Sabiki down through the middle of one of those balls, came up with three at once, and before I even had them in the well I saw the reason those macks were bunched so tight. A big swirl, then another, right under the bait. Bass. We hooked a live mackerel through the collar on a 5/0 circle, freelined it back into the melee, and it didn't get twenty feet before the rod bent in half. That fish went thirty-eight inches. We had two more in the low thirties before the tide slacked off and the whole show shut down like someone flipped a switch.

That's been the pattern all week off Race Point and Peaked Hill Bars — mackerel stacking on the rip lines at first light and again toward dusk, and the bass riding up underneath them for a short, violent window before they drop back down. Surface water in the bay is sitting in the mid-to-upper 70s now, which is warm enough that the bigger fish don't want to sit up in it all day. They're doing what they always do this time of year — holding deep on the structure through the heat of the day, then coming up to hammer bait in the low-light hours. If you're out there at noon casting into empty water, that's not bad luck, that's just where the fish are supposed to be. Best move during the day is dropping bucktails or jigs down onto the humps along the Bars, working them slow off bottom, rather than beating the surface.

Sandy Neck has been a different game — quieter water, less current, but the stripers there have been sliding up onto the flats after dark chasing sand eels and small bait pushed in by the tide. Guys drifting live eels along the edges of the deeper guts off Sandy Neck after sundown have done well, nothing giant but a steady pick of schoolies and a few keepers mixed in. Bluefish have been in and out — when the bait schools hold together you'll get a short window of chopper blues busting bait on top, but it's not the sustained blitz you sometimes get here in August. It's bait-driven entirely. Wherever the mackerel or peanut bunker bunch up tight, the blues show within the hour. When the bait scatters, so do they.

Billingsgate has been the more dependable afternoon play. The fluke bite over that shoal has been solid — not a doormat bonanza, but a steady pick of fish in the 18 to 22 inch range, with a few pushing toward five and six pounds mixed in. We've been drifting the sand edges on the moving tide with white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter ounce bucktails, working them slow along bottom, and switching over to squid strips when the current really starts moving. The fluke are feeding hardest on the last two hours of the incoming, when the water's pushing bait up off the flats and onto the drop.

Cod, I'll be honest with you, have been scarce. This is typical for early August — the deeper structure off Provincetown, the humps out past eighty, ninety feet, just aren't holding fish the way they will once the water starts cooling in September and October. A couple of boats have picked at a few fish jigging diamond jigs tipped with squid strips over the deeper structure, but it's been slow, inconsistent work, more of a bonus than a target species right now. If cod are what you're after, I'd wait another month before putting serious time into it. Right now your time is better spent on the bass and fluke.

We're heading into a neap tide over the next few days, and that's going to soften the current across the whole bay — the rips off Race Point and Peaked Hill won't run as hard, which usually means the bait doesn't get pushed and concentrated the same way. When that happens, the bass bite tends to compress into shorter windows right around the strongest push of current, and the presentations need to slow down to match it. I'd expect the early morning bite off the Race to still produce, but I wouldn't be surprised if it's a tighter window than what we saw this week — maybe forty-five minutes instead of two hours. Lighter jigs, slower retrieves, and patience on the drift will matter more than usual the next few days.

Looking ahead, keep an eye on those mackerel schools. As long as they're stacking on the rips at Race Point and Peaked Hill, the bass will keep showing up to feed on them in the low light. If the macks scatter or move off the structure, the bass pattern will shift with them — probably deeper, probably tighter to Billingsgate and the deeper edges off Barnstable. That's where I'd start looking if the Race goes quiet. For now, if you can get out for that first hour of daylight, it's worth the trip. Just don't expect it to last all morning.
