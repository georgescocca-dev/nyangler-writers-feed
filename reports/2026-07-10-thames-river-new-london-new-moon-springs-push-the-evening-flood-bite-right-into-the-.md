# New moon springs push the evening flood bite right into the sunset at Fort Trumbull
*Big tides and warm harbor water are shoving bait — and stripers — toward the river mouth just as the light goes gold.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — July 10** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

Last week the Thames did what it does every July — it got soft and warm on top, sluggish on the flood, and the fish backed off the pilings during the heat of the day. Harbor water up around Fort Trumbull and the state pier has been sitting in the high 70s on the surface, warm enough that the stripers that were holding tight to the Gold Star Bridge structure in June have slid deeper into the scour holes on the outgoing, or pushed out toward the harbor mouth where the water still has some backbone to it. We had a stretch of light, variable wind that let the upper river go glassy at dawn, which is nice for casting but it also let that top layer bake. Fish fed hard in short windows and shut off fast — a pattern, not a slump, but you had to be there for the window or you got nothing.

This week the moon changes the math. We're building into new moon springs, peaking July 11-13, and that's the first real PM setup we've had since the June full moon. The tides are getting bigger, the current's got more push in it, and — this is the part that matters — high stand at the harbor mouth is lining up almost right on sunset through the weekend. That's the window I'm circling. Bigger tides mean more water moving through the Fort Trumbull rip and around the Gold Star pilings on the last two hours of the flood, which stacks bait against the current seams right as the light goes low and the bass get bold. I've fished this alignment before — it doesn't happen every month, and when it does you want to be positioned before the tide turns, not chasing it after the fact.

Striper fishing itself has been honest work, not a gimme. The evening bite off Fort Trumbull on the last two hours of the flood has been the most consistent thing going — schoolies to 26 inches, with a scattering of fish pushing 30, hitting swimming plugs and soft plastic paddletails worked slow along the seam where the harbor channel current meets the eddy off the point. Olive and white 5-inch paddletails on a 3/8-ounce jighead have been the ticket, fished with a slow swing so it stalls right in the current break. Live eels, when you can get them, are still the best card to play after dark around the Gold Star Bridge pilings — drift them tight to the concrete on the outgoing, especially the pilings on the New London side where the scour is deepest. That bite has been a bit stop-and-start; some nights it's non-stop short strikes, other nights you fish two hours for one good take. That's the nature of a harbor bite riding a warm surface layer — the fish are there, they're just not committed the whole tide.

Bluefish have been mixed in but scattered, not schooled up tight yet. A few choppers in the 3-4 pound class have shown up on topwater poppers worked at first light off Ocean Beach and along the Pleasure Beach flats, but I wouldn't plan a whole trip around them right now — more of a bonus fish if you're already out there for stripers.

The bigger story for bait boats and light-tackle guys chasing fluke is the shift out of the upper harbor. With the river and inner harbor running warm, the fluke that were scattered through the mid-harbor channel in June have pushed toward the mouth — Ocean Beach, Pleasure Beach, and the deeper water off the New London Ledge light. That cooler, cleaner water at the harbor entrance is holding fish better than anything upriver right now. Drift the channel edge off Pleasure Beach on the outgoing with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, tipped with a strip of squid, and keep the boat moving slow enough to feel bottom but not so slow you're stalled in the current. Depths of 20 to 30 feet along that edge have been the sweet spot.

Porgies are doing what porgies do in July — thick around the rock piles and the breakwalls off Ocean Beach and the outer harbor structure, biting steady on sandworms and clam on light tackle almost any stage of tide, though the last hour of the incoming has been a touch better for size. Not glamorous, but it's a sure thing when everything else is being stubborn, and it's a good way to fill an afternoon with the kids while you wait for the evening striper window to set up.

Hickory shad are done for the season up in the Thames — that run wrapped up back in May, so if someone tells you they're still on them, they're either misremembering or fishing memory instead of water. Blackfish are locked up in their summer closure and the smart money's already thinking ahead to the fall reopener on the rock piles off Groton.

Looking ahead: if the new moon springs hold through the weekend and that high-stand-at-sunset alignment stays put, I'd be at Fort Trumbull or under the Gold Star Bridge with an hour of daylight left, working the last of the flood into dusk. That's the best structural setup we've had all month for a real evening bite. If the wind kicks up out of the southwest and muddies the harbor mouth, I've got a backup plan — pull back into the river proper and fish the current seams on live bait after dark, since dirty water at the mouth usually means the fish slide back upriver looking for clean current instead.
