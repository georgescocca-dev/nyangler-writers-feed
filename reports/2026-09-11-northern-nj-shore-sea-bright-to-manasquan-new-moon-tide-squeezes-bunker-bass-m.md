# Sea Bright to Manasquan: New Moon Tide Squeezes Bunker, Bass Move In
*The first legitimate bunker blitzes of the fall run showed up on the rocks this week, and the guys throwing pencils at first light aren't complaining.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — September 11** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

First light on the Manasquan north jetty this week looked like it should this time of year — six, seven guys spread down the rocks, headlamps still on, throwing bunker-pattern swimmers and pencil poppers into current that was actually moving something. That's the tell. When the bunker schools get squeezed tight against the beach and the current has real push behind it, the stripers stop picking at stragglers and start hunting. That's exactly what the new moon on the 10th delivered — the strongest tidal pull of the young fall season, and the bass responded the way they're supposed to. We flagged this last week, said the building moon into September 10th would trigger the first real blitz activity on bunker from the Jersey beaches on north, and that's what's showing up on the rocks from Sea Bright down through Manasquan Inlet right now.

The pattern isn't a full-blown blitz every tide, but it's real. Bunker pods are getting pinned against the beachfront at Sea Bright and Monmouth Beach on the last two hours of the outgoing, and bass are working them from underneath — showing as boils and nervous water rather than the classic surface explosion, but showing consistently enough that the rock guys are staying put through two tide changes instead of moving spot to spot. Fish are running mixed sizes, low 20s up through a few bumping the 30-inch mark, and they're eating bunker-imitation swimmers — Gibbs Danny surface swimmers, bunker-spook style plugs, and white/bunker bucktails worked slow along the current seam off the north jetty rocks. The bite window at Manasquan has been tightest on the last two hours of the ebb into dead low, when the bait gets funneled hard against the rocks and has nowhere left to go.

Same story down at Sea Bright, where the outer rocks hold fish on a west or northwest wind — that wind lays the water down and keeps the bunker pinned tight to the beach instead of scattering them offshore. A south or southeast blow does the opposite: it pushes swell into the beachfront, dirties the water, and pushes the bait — and the bass with it — off the immediate structure. Buoy readings off the Bight this week showed seas running close to four feet at a short five-second period, which tells you the ocean's had some size to it. That kind of chop keeps the outer bars and exposed jetty tips sloppy through midday, so the honest advice is the same it's been for twenty-five years on this stretch: first hour of light, before the wind builds, is when the rocks fish clean.

Fluke has been the tougher conversation at the docks. The keeper-grade fish have largely cleared out of the inshore water — Manasquan and Belmar boats are still marking fluke, but the ratio is running heavy toward shorts, a classic late-summer tell that the bigger fish already staged toward deeper, cooler water ahead of their fall exit. We called that fade a week ago and it's playing out exactly as expected. If you're still chasing a limit of keepers, the better play right now is deeper structure outside the inlet mouths rather than the flats and back-bay edges that produced all summer. Inside the inlets themselves, there are still fish to be had on Gulp swimming mullets in white or pink shad, dragged slow on 3/4-ounce bucktails through the deeper channel bends on the last of the incoming — just don't expect a fast box.

Bluefish are the other bright spot. Snapper blues are thick in Shark River and up in the Manasquan River docks, snapping at small spearing under the lights at night — a reliable sign that bigger blues aren't far behind as the water keeps cooling. There's already evidence of that along the beachfront, where adult blues have been mixing in with the bass on the same bunker schools, occasionally stealing plugs meant for stripers and leaving hooks bent for the trouble. If you're throwing metal — Kastmasters or Deadly Dicks off the Spring Lake jetties — expect to connect on blues as often as bass right now, and that's not a bad problem to have.

Blackfish and porgy chatter is starting to pick up around the rock piles as water temps ease off their summer high, though it's early yet for a full tautog push. Porgies are biting steady around the Manasquan Inlet rocks and bridge structure on bloodworms and clam, a good bet for anyone who wants a guaranteed bend in the rod while waiting on the tide to turn for bass.

Looking ahead, the moon is still close enough to new that the tidal push should stay strong through the weekend, and that means the bunker-and-bass pattern along the beachfront has room to build before it eases. If the wind holds out of the west or northwest, Sea Bright and the Manasquan jetties should keep producing on the outgoing, particularly that last two hours into dead low. If it swings southeast and the surf builds, shift into the inlets themselves — Shark River and Manasquan Inlet both hold current and bait regardless of what the ocean's doing outside. Either way, first light remains the highest-percentage window before the wind and the boat traffic pick up. The bunker are here, the bass know it, and for the first time this fall the timing actually lines up.
