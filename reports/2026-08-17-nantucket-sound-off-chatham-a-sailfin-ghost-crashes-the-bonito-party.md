# Off Chatham, a Sailfin Ghost Crashes the Bonito Party
*A sailfish showed up in a bonito school 200 yards off the beach — a sign of how warm and strange this water has gotten, and a preview of the albie run still building behind it.*

**NANTUCKET, MA — August 17** — _by Charlie St. Pierre, Zone Writer_

Picture this: flat calm morning off Chatham, bonito busting bait on the surface the way they do when the water's right, and then a long, sickle-shaped dorsal knifes up through the middle of it that has no business being anywhere near 40 feet of water off Cape Cod. An Atlantic sailfish. , feeding, right in the thick of the bonito. I've fished this water my whole life and I can count on one hand the times anybody's logged a sailfish this close to shore up here — this might only be the second one on record. Doesn't mean much for your Tuesday afternoon trip, but it tells you everything about what's going on with the water temperature right now. It's warm, it's holding warm, and it's pulling fish up here that flat out shouldn't be here.

We flagged this zone for early bonito activity last week, and here it is — bonito are actively showing and getting caught from the Monomoy rips up through the mouth of the Sound. That warm push is exactly what does it. Regional readings off the Connecticut side of the Sound are sitting in the high 60s to low 70s, and inshore around the Cape and the islands the water's running that same range or better, which is squarely in bonito and soon-albie territory. The bonito bite has been the story so far this month — fish showing on bait pods over sand and gravel bottom around Monomoy, working the rip lines on the last two hours of the ebb when the current stacks bait against the shoal edge. Half-ounce Deadly Dicks and small epoxy jigs in olive/white have been the ticket, cast into the nervous water and worked with a fast, erratic retrieve. Some days it's stupid easy — three or four fish in twenty minutes if you find the right push of bait. Other days the wind kicks up out of the southwest, the surface goes choppy, and you can't find a single sign for an hour. That's been the pattern all month: good, not constant. Worth the run out, but bring patience and a full tank.

False albacore haven't shown in real numbers yet, and I'll be honest, I've been checking every day for two weeks now like a kid waiting for Christmas. Great Point on the ebb is where I'd expect the first real showing — that current line off the point concentrates bait hard when the tide's dropping, and albies love working that seam where clean water meets the roiled stuff coming off the rip. We're not there yet this week. I had one report of a scattered pod working briefly off Sankaty on a calm morning, gone in twenty minutes, no repeat. If the warm water signal holds — and that sailfish sighting says it's holding plenty warm — I'd expect albies to start showing in real numbers over the next two to three weeks. When they do, small metal — half-ounce to one-ounce Deadly Dicks, Albie Snax in bone or pink — worked fast on 20-pound fluoro leader will get it done. Until then, keep your eyes on the birds and don't waste gas chasing ghosts.

Striped bass are mixing in but not headlining right now. With the Sound running this warm, the bass have pulled back into that classic low-light pattern — strong at first light and again at dusk, quieter through the heat of the day. I've had decent luck working the edges of Horseshoe Shoal early, before the sun gets up, drifting live eels or bouncing a bucktail with a swimming mullet trailer along the sandy drop-offs where the current sweeps bait off the flat. Bluefish are showing consistently mixed into those same bait schools — nothing giant, mostly 3 to 5 pounds, but they'll save a slow morning and they hit hard enough to keep things interesting on light tackle. Snapper blues are also thick in the back creeks and harbors around the island right now, which tells you the adult class fish should keep building behind them as we push toward September.

Fluke have been a mixed bag. Some boats working the edges of Nantucket Shoals with squid strips and Gulp on bucktails are finding keepers in 25 to 35 feet, but it's not a slam dunk — plenty of shorts mixed in, and you've got to move around to find the pockets holding size. I wouldn't call it the story of the week the way I might have hoped. It's there if you put the time in, but it's not carrying the report right now the way the bonito are.

Tide-wise, we're sliding off the recent push toward a weaker neap cycle heading into the first quarter moon, which should ease the current a bit over the next several days — good news if you've been getting pushed around out at the rips. Then we build back toward the full moon on the 28th, and that's when I'd expect current strength to really load back up around Great Point and Monomoy, which almost always kicks bait and predators into higher gear together. If the warm water pattern holds through that build — and there's no sign yet it's breaking — I'd bet on albies in numbers right around that window. That's where I'd put my money and my gas money this coming week: working the ebb at Great Point, checking the Monomoy rips for bonito each morning, and keeping half an eye out for anything with a fin that doesn't belong.
