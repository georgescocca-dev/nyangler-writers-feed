# Outgoing After Midnight: Bass Hold the Deep Hole, Weakfish Still Working the Bridge
*With the surface water running warm, the real bite at Shinnecock happens after the sun's been down for hours — not right at dusk.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — August 3** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

I skipped the 9 p.m. slack this week and made myself wait. That's the mistake most guys make out here in August — they show up right at dark, fish the tail end of the incoming, and wonder why the inlet feels dead. It's not dead. It's just not ready yet. With the water running warm the way it has been, the bass at Shinnecock have gone almost fully nocturnal, and they don't really turn on until the tide's been ripping out for an hour or two and the deep hole below the Coast Guard station has had time to load up with bait getting flushed out of the bay.

So that's where I put my time this week — 1 a.m. to about 4 a.m., fishing the last two-thirds of the outgoing on the ocean side of the jetty. Eels drifted along the rock edge on a fish-finder rig, and bunker chunks fished dead-still on the bottom in that 18 to 22 foot hole, produced the best fish — a few in the mid-30s, mixed with a lot of schoolies that wouldn't leave the bait alone. Nothing epic, but consistent enough that I stuck with it three nights running. If you're a plug guy, a black Danny or a bone-colored Gibbs pencil worked across the current seam where the outgoing water meets the ocean swell will draw strikes, but you need to be quiet getting into position — no white deck lights, no slamming hatches. These fish in the hole are educated and they spook off a hull shadow same as they spook off a bright flashlight.

Ponquogue Bridge is still doing what it does in early August, which is weakfish after dark on the outgoing, though I'll be honest — it's a thinner bite than it was three weeks ago. The fish that are there are quality, mostly 18 to 22 inches, holding tight to the pilings on the bay side where the current breaks around the concrete. Bucktails in the 3/4-ounce range tipped with a strip of squid, bounced right along bottom in that 12 to 15 foot depth, are still the most reliable presentation. A few guys are doing well on small ribbonfish-imitation soft plastics too — something with flash and a subtle wobble seems to matter more than color right now. Bring a headlamp with a red lens if you're going to be handling fish or retying — full white light over that bridge rail and you'll watch the whole school slide off into the dark.

During the day, the fluke bite has stayed the most dependable thing going on in the bay, and honestly we called this one — figured the drift out here would stay steady rather than blitz-style, and that's exactly what's playing out. Boats working the sod banks off Tiana Beach and the channel edges near the Canal mouth are picking up a mix of keepers and shorts on white Gulp Swimming Mullets and chartreuse bucktails, 12 to 18 feet of water, moving bait just fast enough to keep it ticking bottom on the last of the incoming into the start of the outgoing. Nothing that fills a cooler in an hour, but steady enough that a patient drift puts two or three keepers in the box most trips. Porgies are mixed in on the same structure if you switch over to sandworms on a hi-lo rig — good action for kids or anyone who just wants bent rods without overthinking it.

Bluefish have shown up stronger than they have in a few weeks, which tracks with the warm surface water pushing down the coast right now. I've seen decent-sized blues — some pushing 5 and 6 pounds — chopping bait on the surface just outside the inlet in the early morning gray light, right before the sun gets high enough to shut them down. Diamond jigs or wire leader with a Hopkins if you want to avoid getting bit off. They're not sticking around long once the light comes up, so that's a dawn-only window, not an all-day thing.

As for the Canal and the run through to the Peconic — bait's been moving through there steadily on the tide changes, and that matters more than people think. When you see peanut bunker and spearing pushing hard through the Canal on an outgoing, it's usually a signal that the same forage is about to show up on the Montauk side within a few days. Worth keeping an eye on if you're planning a trip east later this week.

Looking ahead, the tides are building toward the new moon, and that stronger current should sharpen up both the inlet bass bite and the bridge weakfish, at least for a few nights. If a front comes through and knocks the surface temp down even a couple degrees, I'd expect the bass to loosen up and feed a little earlier in the evening instead of making us wait until 1 a.m. Until that happens, though, I'm staying on the night shift.
