# A 3-Degree Break at 39°34'N Puts Yellowfin on the Chunk Line
*Warm-core ring water is still parked on the Hudson wall, and the numbers say it's not going anywhere fast.*

**MANASQUAN INLET, NJ — July 21, 2026** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Seventy-one point two. That's what the Bight buoy was reading before we even cleared the beach this week, and it told me everything I needed to know about the ride out. Warm surface water sitting that shallow inshore means the filament we've been tracking off the Hudson wall hasn't slid off — it's still stacked up against the canyon edge, and it's dragging bait with it. I ran the plotter out past the 100-fathom line to about 39°34'N, 72°45'W — inside edge of the Claw — and picked up a break of just over three degrees in less than a mile of water. That's the kind of gradient that piles bait against a wall and makes tuna stupid.

We flagged this exact pattern last week — said the warm filament would keep building the bite if it held position, and it held. The canyon is fishing better right now than it has all month, and the reason is written right there on the chart: warm-core ring water pushing east off the Stream, meeting cooler shelf water along the 100-fathom break, and stacking bait right where the bottom drops off hard between the Dip and the Claw.

Daytime troll has been the more consistent play this week. Spreader bars with naked ballyhoo, run at six and a half to seven knots along that temp seam, are drawing yellowfin in the 35 to 55 pound class, with a scattered bigeye mixed in on boats willing to push the spread out past the color change. Green and yellow machines in the shotgun, small cedar plugs and Ilanders in close — nothing exotic, just clean presentations tight to where the water goes from that milky green shelf color to the deep cobalt blue. When you see the color break, you're close. I've had my best strikes within a quarter mile of where the SST chart shows the line, not right on top of it — the fish are sitting just inside the cooler water, ambushing bait that gets pushed across.

Night chunking has been the bigger story for quality fish. Butterfish chunks fished on a slow drift over 900 to 1,200 feet of water near Texas Tower and out toward the Dip have been putting bigeye in the 60 to 90 pound range in the boat between midnight and 3 a.m. The first-quarter neap that set up around the 21st has actually helped this bite — current at the edge has softened enough that a chunk bag drifts naturally instead of getting swept off the structure, and the slower drift means baits stay in the strike zone longer. If you're running a chunk spread, keep at least one bait unweighted and let it fall naturally with the chum slick — that's where the bigger fish have been eating.

The bluefin slot fishery is holding steady, which is worth mentioning since a lot of boats gave up on it after the trophy-class closure hit July 3rd. Fish in the 27 to under-47-inch window are still showing on the troll around the 60-fathom curve on the way out to the canyon, mixed in with the yellowfin. Small green machines and Zucker rigs in the daisy chain positions have been drawing strikes from fish clearly feeding on sand eels — nothing subtle about it, just a clean two-fish-a-boat limit if you're willing to work the spread through that transition zone before you commit to deep water.

Mahi have been a bonus rather than a target — scattered fish under any floating debris or weed line inside the break, usually school-size, 3 to 8 pounds, eager for a small skirted jig dropped right off the transom. Wahoo have shown sporadically on the high-speed gear when boats run planers or a deep-diving lure through the color change at 12 to 15 knots — not a dedicated bite yet, more of a byproduct when guys are hunting bigger game. White marlin have been quiet this week, a couple of shots reported on dink ballyhoo but nothing consistent enough to call it a pattern.

Sea state has been workable — 3.9 feet at a five-second period out of the south when I checked, short and a little sloppy but nothing that keeps a well-run 32 to 40 footer off the grounds. It's the kind of chop that makes the ride out uncomfortable for an hour and then lays down once you're sitting on the edge working a chunk slick. Worth timing your run around the wind forecast rather than fighting it the whole way out.

Honest assessment: this isn't an every-drop bite. I've had trips where the troll produced steady doubles through the morning and trips where we worked the same temp break for three hours and only picked at fish. The night chunk has been the more reliable producer for quality over quantity. If the warm filament holds its position through the weekend — and right now the gradient looks stable, not eroding — I'd expect the yellowfin and bigeye numbers to keep building rather than fade. If it slides east the way these things sometimes do, I'd shift effort toward Wilmington and the Washington Canyon before I'd give up on the Hudson entirely. For now, though, the data and the fish are telling the same story, and that's rare enough to trust.
