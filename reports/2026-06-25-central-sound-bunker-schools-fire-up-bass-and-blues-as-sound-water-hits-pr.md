# Bunker schools fire up bass and blues as Sound water hits prime summer temps
*Big bait pods from Norwalk to Port Jeff trigger feeding frenzies on the flood tide.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — June 25** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

The Central Sound is absolutely alive right now, and it's all about the bunker. These adult menhaden have been stacking up from the Norwalk Islands clear across to Port Jefferson Harbor, and when they move, everything follows. I've been watching this build for two weeks, and it finally exploded this past weekend.

The bass bite has been exceptional on the flood tide, especially around the deeper structure. I'm talking 25 to 35 feet along the Connecticut shoreline drops and the edges near Middle Ground Light. The fish are keying on those bunker schools, and when you find the bait, you find the stripers. Live-lined bunker on a fishfinder rig with a 6/0 circle hook has been deadly — let that bait swim naturally in the current and hang on. I've seen fish to 40 inches taken this way, with most in that sweet 28 to 35-inch range.

What's really interesting is how the thermal structure is setting up. Water temps have climbed into the upper 60s in the harbors and bays, but the deeper water in the shipping channel is holding steady around 64 degrees. That temperature break is creating a perfect ambush zone where the cooler, oxygen-rich water meets the warmer surface layer. The bass are using this thermocline like a highway, following it east and west as they hunt.

Bluefish have joined the party in a big way. These aren't the snappers we'll see in July — these are legitimate choppers in the 3 to 8-pound class, and they're absolutely savage on the bunker schools. When you see the birds working, get there fast. Wire leaders are mandatory, and I've been having great success with 1-ounce white bucktails tipped with a strip of fresh bunker belly. Cast into the melee and retrieve fast — these blues want a bait that's trying to escape.

The fluke fishing has been solid but requires some patience. With all this bunker activity in the water column, the flatfish have been a bit scattered. Your best bet is to work the edges of the shipping channel during the outgoing tide, especially around the Stratford Shoal area. Four to 6-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets in white or chartreuse on 3/4-ounce bucktails are producing fish to 24 inches. The key is staying mobile — if you're not marking fish or getting bites within 20 minutes, move.

Porgies are thick on the deeper structure, and while they're not the glamour species, they're excellent table fare and great action for kids. The rocky areas off the Connecticut side in 30 to 45 feet have been loaded with scup in the 12 to 14-inch range. High-low rigs with size 4 hooks and small pieces of squid or clam are all you need.

The moon phase is working in our favor right now. We're coming off a new moon, which means the spring tides are flushing massive amounts of bait out of the harbors and estuaries. This is what's been concentrating those bunker schools and triggering the feeding response from the gamefish. The current has been running hard — sometimes too hard for effective fishing — but when it eases during the tide changes, that's when the magic happens.

Looking ahead, I expect this pattern to continue through the weekend and into next week. The weather looks cooperative, with light southwest winds and no major fronts approaching. The key will be timing your trips with the tide. The two hours before and after the tide change have been most productive, especially on the flood when all that bait gets pushed up against the structure.

One thing to watch for: as we move deeper into summer and water temps climb into the low 70s, expect the fish to go deeper during the heat of the day. Early morning and evening sessions will become increasingly important. The bass will still be there, but they'll be hugging that thermocline in 40 to 60 feet of water during the midday heat.

The action is only going to get better as we head toward the full moon next week. Those big tides will really get the bait moving, and I'm expecting some epic surface action when conditions align. Keep your eyes on the birds, your ears on the radio chatter, and be ready to run when the call comes in.
