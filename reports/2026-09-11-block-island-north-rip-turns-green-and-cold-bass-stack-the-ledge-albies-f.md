# North Rip Turns Green and Cold — Bass Stack the Ledge, Albies Finally Show
*The new moon push tightened the bunker and pulled the first real albie run of the fall into island water.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — September 11** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

There's a smell that comes off the North Rip this time of year, right before the albies show — a cold, mineral edge under the bait smell, like the water itself changed temperature overnight. The mates at the dock in New Harbor noticed it first this week, and by Wednesday the birds confirmed what everybody already suspected: the fall run has finally punched a hole through and it's here.

The September 10 new moon did exactly what a strong new moon does around this island — it cranked the tidal push hard enough to squeeze the bunker schools tight against the beaches and rips instead of letting them spread out lazy across the open Sound. That's the difference between stripers picking at stragglers and stripers committing. This week they committed. Bunker pods that had been loafing off the West Side and around Sandy Point got pinned against structure by the extra current, and the bass that have been shadowing them all August finally treated it like a meal instead of a snack. We flagged this pattern last week — said the staging bass from Raritan Bay up through Narragansett Bay were about to turn the corner into real feeding behavior — and that's exactly what showed up on the Ledge and off Southwest Point this week.

The albie news is the bigger story for a lot of the center console guys running out of New Harbor and Payne's Dock. We said the albie bite would build as more fish pushed into the zone, and it built fast — false albacore mixing with schoolie bonito on bait balls working the rip lines off the Southeast Light and out past Southwest Ledge in 20 to 40 feet. These fish are following the same warm filament that's been pushing up the coast all month, and with water still holding comfortable through the low-to-mid 70s around the island, there's no reason for that edge to back off soon. The bite windows have been tight — usually the first hour of light and again the last two hours before dark, right around the top of the tide when the bait gets pushed up and pinned near the surface. Small profile epoxy jigs and 3-inch Deceivers in bay anchovy patterns have been the difference-makers; these fish want speed and a small silhouette, not a big splashy presentation.

Striper fishing has been the most consistent story of the week. The Southwest Ledge has been holding fish on the last two hours of the ebb, when the current sweeps bait off the high spots and funnels it into the deeper troughs — 30 to 45 feet has been the sweet spot, bass sitting right on the drop where the sand gives way to rock. Live-lined bunker on a fish-finder rig, or fresh chunks fished on the bottom with just enough weight to hold in the current, have been outproducing artificials there, though guys throwing big soft plastic swim shads on 2-ounce heads have connected too, especially working the up-current side of the boulders. The North Rip has been more of a moving target — some mornings stacked with bass under diving gulls, other mornings dead quiet an hour after the tide turns. When it's on, it's usually right at the top of the flood, current running hard enough to hold bait against the rip but not so hard you can't hold bottom. That's when the 40-pound-class fish show up. Nobody's landing those every trip, but the fleet is seeing enough of them in the 20 to 35-pound range mixed with slot fish to call it a legitimate pattern, not a fluke.

Fluke has been the disappointing piece of the week, and there's no point dressing it up. The keeper-grade fish that were around Block Island and Point Judith through July and August have largely cleared out for deeper, cooler structure — the same pattern playing out from the mid-Atlantic all the way up here. What's left inshore around the island and off Narragansett is mostly short fish, quick to bite and quick to go back over the side. We called this fade coming last week, and it's playing out on schedule. If fluke is the priority, working the deeper edges off Block Island in 60 feet-plus with a heavier bucktail and Gulp combo is a better bet than the shallower structure that produced all summer.

Bluefish have been a bright spot for anyone frustrated by finicky bass or albies that won't stay put. Snapper-sized blues are thick in Great Salt Pond and around the Newport breachways, and adult blues in the 3 to 6-pound class have been mixing into the same bait schools drawing bass to the Ledge, hitting poppers and diamond jigs worked fast on top.

Looking ahead, the moon is easing off its new-phase peak, which usually means the tidal push softens a bit over the next several days — less brute-force current, but the bait should stay concentrated enough to keep the bite going, just maybe not as explosive as this week's peak. If the wind stays out of the southwest and holds under 15 knots, the Southwest Ledge and North Rip should keep producing on the tide changes through the weekend. If a northeast blow pushes in, expect the albies to scatter first and the bass bite to hold up better, since they're less dependent on flat, glassy water to feed. Either way, this is the window — the bunker are here, the bass are committed, and the albie run is finally what it's supposed to be in September.
