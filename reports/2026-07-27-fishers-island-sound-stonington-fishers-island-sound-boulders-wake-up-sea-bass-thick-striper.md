# Fishers Island Sound Boulders Wake Up: Sea Bass Thick, Stripers Gone to the Dark
*Current's building back out of the neap, sand eels are pinned against the rocks, and the albie clock is ticking louder every tide.*

**STONINGTON, CT — July 27** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

Poled the skiff over the boulder field off Ram Island Tuesday morning, low light, and the sand eels were doing what they do this time of year — balled up tight against the rock faces, nervous, getting pushed around by something underneath them I couldn't see yet. That's the tell. When bait sits that tight to structure and won't leave it, there's usually a reason, and out here in late July the reason is almost always sea bass working the crevices or stripers staging deeper in the current seam waiting on dark.

We said last week the current had gone soft on this neap cycle and the bite in current-dependent water would taper until it rebuilt — and that's exactly what's happened. The tide's coming back now as the moon works toward full in early August, and you can feel it in the rip off Wamphassuc Point and through the gut between Ram Island and the mainland. Water's moving with purpose again on the last two hours of the ebb, which is when I want to be there. Slack water in this sound is a dead zone for anything that eats — the fish here live and die by current, and for three or four days they didn't have much to work with.

Black sea bass have been the most honest fish out there. Structure from the Fishers Island Sound boulder fields down through the rock piles off Esker Point is holding them thick — 14 to 18 inches, some pushing better than that, stacked tight to vertical rock faces in 15 to 25 feet. I'm dropping small green crabs and Gulp Peach Swimming Mullets on light jigheads, quarter to three-eighths ounce depending on how hard the current's running, and letting it bounce right down into the cracks. You feel the take different on these rocks than you do on sand — it's a thump right at the bottom, and if you don't set fast they're back in the crevice with your jig before you know it. This has been the most consistent thing in the Sound for three weeks running and it hasn't slowed a bit.

Stripers are a different animal right now. Surface temps at the western end near New London are sitting in the high 60s, and once you get up into Little Narragansett Bay and the Mystic River mouth on a sunny afternoon it's pushing low 70s. That's warm enough to shut the daytime bite down almost completely. I haven't had a keeper bass on a rod in daylight in over a week — not for lack of trying. What's working is fishing the last two hours of light into full dark, eels drifted through the current seams off the Stonington breakwater, and bunker chunks fished on the bottom in the deeper holes around Fishers Island Sound where the water stays cooler. Slot fish have come from both spots, along with a couple of bigger models pushing past 34 inches off the breakwater rocks on the outgoing tide around 9 and 10 pm. If you want stripers here right now, you're a night owl or you're not catching much.

Fluke have softened up in the shallow spots the way they always do once it gets hot — the open sand flats inside the bay aren't holding much. But the edges are still good. I've had decent drifts along the channel bend off Watch Hill and through the deeper cuts between the Fishers Island boulder fields, working 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow right on bottom where the moving water stays cool even when the flats around it don't. Nothing huge — mostly 16 to 19 inches with the occasional keeper mixed in — but steady enough on a good tide stage to be worth the trip if fluke's what you're after.

Now the one everybody's asking me about at the dock — albies and bonito. Nothing confirmed yet in this stretch of water, and I'm not going to tell you different just because it's the last week of July and everybody wants to hear it. But the pieces are lining up. Water temps at the mouth of the Sound have crossed the line where these fish start showing in a normal year, and the current's rebuilding fast now that we're out of the neap. Bonito tend to show first in the rougher water off Watch Hill and the outer edge of Fishers Island before they push into the calmer Sound water, so that's where I'd be looking first — small epoxy jigs and Deceiver flies worked fast through any nervous water or diving terns. If the pattern holds the way it usually does, I'd expect the first real signs within the next week to ten days, and once they show in this Sound they tend to stay through September.

Looking ahead — I'd fish the sea bass hard while the current's building, it's the surest thing going right now. For stripers, commit to the dark hours around the breakwater and the Mystic River current lines rather than burning daylight for nothing. And keep a rod rigged light with epoxy jigs in the boat from here on out, because when the albies do show in Fishers Island Sound, they don't give you much warning — and I don't want to be the guy still tying on a bucktail when the water starts boiling.
