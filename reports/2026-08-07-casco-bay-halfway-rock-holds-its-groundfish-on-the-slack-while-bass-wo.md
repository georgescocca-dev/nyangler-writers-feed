# Halfway Rock holds its groundfish on the slack while bass work the river mouths
*Cold Gulf of Maine water keeps the bite predictable this week — steady if you fish the structure right, quiet everywhere else.*

**PORTLAND, ME — August 8** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

I anchored off Halfway Rock Tuesday morning, right at the top of the tide, and watched the current go from a whisper to nothing at all — that dead slack window when the whole bay seems to hold its breath. A gull working bait fifty yards off the ledge told me everything I needed to know before I ever dropped a line. Mackerel, tight to the surface, getting pushed by something below. That's the tell this time of year: find the birds, find the bait, and the groundfish are usually stacked underneath waiting for the current to give them an excuse to feed.

That's been the story of Casco Bay this week — patient, structured, a little slow if you're not fishing the right piece of bottom. The water's held cold and clean, no warm-water push showing up anywhere in the bay, which keeps this whole system leaning northern. Pollock, cod, and the occasional haddock are exactly where they should be in early August: down on the hard bottom around Halfway Rock, Jewell Island's eastern ledge, and the rockpile off Eagle Island, sitting in 60 to 90 feet. Diamond jigs — the 4- and 6-ounce chrome ones — worked on a slow lift-drop through the last hour of the ebb and the first hour of the flood have been the most consistent producer. I took two keeper haddock and a pollock over 8 pounds off Halfway Rock doing exactly that on Wednesday, right as the tide started to build again after the slack. Cod have been scattered but present — nothing to write home about in numbers, but a few nice fish in the 6- to 10-pound range coming up on cut mackerel bounced right on bottom.

Striped bass have settled into their August pattern, which means they've mostly abandoned open water for the river mouths and the structure that holds bait against current. The Presumpscot mouth, the Royal River off Yarmouth, and the Harraseeket up in Freeport have all been giving up fish on the last two hours of the incoming, when the tide pushes bait up against the pilings and the grass edges. Live mackerel under a slip bobber, or a chunk of the same fished on a fish-finder rig, has out-produced everything else I've tried. Down toward Cape Small and around the ledges off Bailey Island, the bass bite has been more about structure than tide stage — soft plastics, an 8-inch white paddle tail on a 3/4-ounce jighead, worked slow along the rock faces at half-tide, has taken some respectable fish in the 28- to 34-inch range. Nothing enormous, but a solid, honest bite if you're willing to cover water.

Mackerel themselves are still around, just not in the tightly packed schools you'd have seen back in May. Bailey Island's Mackerel Cove and the backside of Peaks Island have both had pockets of fish moving through on the tide changes — small jigs, the classic mackerel tree with no bait needed, will still load a bucket in twenty minutes if you find the right push of current. I wouldn't plan a whole trip around them right now, but they're a fine way to fill a cooler with fresh bait for the bass and groundfish while you're out there anyway.

Bluefish have been a non-event this week, and I want to be straight about that rather than pretend otherwise. With the bay running cold and no warm intrusion showing up on the thermal side, there's just no reason for them to push this far north in numbers yet. If that changes — and it can change quickly if a warm eddy slides up the coast — I'll be the first to say so. For now, don't plan a trip around bluefish in Casco Bay.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the holding pattern would persist until the new moon tides built enough current to sharpen things up at structure, and that's exactly what's happening as we run into the August 12 new moon. The tidal range is noticeably increasing day over day, and I felt it firsthand at Halfway Rock — the drift picked up speed through the week, and the groundfish bite got more decisive with it. That's not a coincidence. Bigger tides mean harder current through the ledges and river mouths, which means bait gets pinned against structure more aggressively, which means the fish feed with more conviction instead of picking at things.

Looking ahead, I expect that trend to keep building right through the new moon. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take the last two hours of the ebb at Halfway Rock for groundfish, then run into the Harraseeket or Royal River on the building flood for bass — that tide sequence lines up nicely on most days right now. Two Lights is worth a look too if you're fishing from shore; the current runs hard enough there on a big tide to hold bait, and a few schoolie bass have been taken casting bucktails off the rocks at the base of the light on the incoming. The pattern's stable, the water's cold and clean, and the fishing rewards anyone willing to read the tide chart before they read the water.
