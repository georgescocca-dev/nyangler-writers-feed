# Fort Trumbull Rips Go Quiet at Noon, Loud at Midnight — Stripers Sliding Deep, Blues Still Chasing Bunker
*New London's holding a cooler read than the rest of the Sound, but the fish still know it's late July — daylight belongs to the porgies, dark belongs to the eels.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — July 27** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

Stood on the seawall below Fort Trumbull Tuesday evening watching the ebb build against the fort's old stone footing, and you could see the seam sharpen right on schedule — that clean line where the river current peels off the rock and meets the harbor swing. Twenty years watching that seam and it still tells you everything. Right now it's telling me the stripers have gone underground during the day and only show themselves once the light's off the water.

New London's reading 69.4 degrees at the surface, which is actually the cool corner of the Sound this week — Kings Point to the west is pushing near 71. That gradient matters more than people think. It means the Thames and the harbor mouth are holding onto a little more oxygen and a little more comfort for bass than the water to our west, but make no mistake, we're still deep into that mid-summer stretch where daytime stripers want nothing to do with the surface. I've been finding them stacked on the down-current side of the Gold Star Bridge pilings, 18 to 25 feet, sitting tight to the concrete where the tide breaks and slows just enough for them to hold without burning fuel. During the day that's a bottom bite or nothing — eels on a fish-finder rig, bunker chunks anchored with 3 ounces of lead to hold in that current, fished dead slow on the last two hours of the ebb. After dark, though, those same fish slide up onto the flats off Fort Trumbull and the river mouth proper, and that's when eels swum slow on a plain 3/8-ounce jighead start producing better fish — nothing giant, mostly schoolies and low-20s, but a couple of guys have put mid-30-inch bass on the beach fishing the Fort Trumbull rip an hour either side of the tide change, working the current seam where the fresh river water folds into the salt.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the warm, stable weather would keep stripers locked into this nocturnal script, and that's exactly what's playing out. Nothing dramatic, nothing that's going to fill a cooler by 10 a.m., but if you're willing to fish the dark hours and work structure instead of open water, there are fish to be had.

Bluefish are the ones putting on the show right now, and they don't care what time it is. Bunker pods have been running the harbor from Pleasure Beach up toward Ocean Beach, and when the tide's moving — doesn't matter ebb or flood, just moving — you'll see the water erupt where the blues pin a school against the beach or against the riprap. Poppers and metal — the classic 1-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — thrown right into the froth is about as simple as fishing gets right now, and it's been paying off with blues in the 3 to 6-pound class, plenty of them, enough to keep rods bent even on days when the striper bite is a grind. If you see gulls working tight and diving hard off Ocean Beach, don't wait — that bait won't hold in one spot long, and the blues push it fast.

Porgies have been the steady, unglamorous producer all month, and they're still there. Fort Trumbull's rock pile and the rip lines around the sub base pilings are holding good numbers on bloodworms and skimmer clam, fished on a simple high-low rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in the current — 2 to 3 ounces depending on the stage of tide. Best window is that hour before and after the tide change when the current eases and the porgies stack up to feed instead of fighting the flow. Nothing trophy-sized, mostly hand-sized fish in the 10 to 12-inch range, but it's a reliable way to bend a rod with kids or when the bigger stuff isn't cooperating. Blackfish are still shut down for the summer closure and won't be back in play until the fall opener, so if you're itching for tog, that wait continues.

Hickory shad have thinned out from where they were earlier in the season — a few stragglers still show on light jigs and small Kastmasters up in the river above the bridge on the last of the incoming, but that window's closing fast as the water holds in the high 60s to low 70s. Don't plan a trip around them at this point; treat any shad as a bonus while you're working the same stretch for stripers or blues.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full in early August, and that means current through the Race and up into Fishers Island Sound is going to strengthen over the next week, which should sharpen every seam in the Thames system — Fort Trumbull, the bridge pilings, the river mouth flats. If that holds, I'd expect the nighttime striper bite to get a little more consistent and the bass to push a bit shallower on the stronger tides as bait gets squeezed harder against structure. My plan for the next few outings is simple: eels after dark on the Fort Trumbull rip on the building current, bunker chunks off the bridge pilings on the slower stretches of the ebb, and metal in hand any time I see birds working off Ocean Beach. It's not a blowup bite on any one species, but between the blues on top and the bass willing to show after sundown, there's enough going on to make the trip worth planning around the tide chart rather than the clock.
