# New moon springs load The Race as fluke push out of the bays and bonito knock on Fishers Island's door
*Bigger current, colder edges, and a widening bonito corridor mean this week's fishing is about picking the right five-minute window, not the whole tide.*

**ORIENT POINT, NY — July 8** — _by Mike Rizzo, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound was in a holding pattern — post-solstice current still building toward these new moon tides, and a run of NE wind put a chill on the surface layer that pushed the thermocline around more than I like to see in July. That upwelling is exactly why the fluke bailed out of the back bays. Water sitting near 78 degrees in the shallow flats got uncomfortable fast, and the fish slid to the channels and inlet mouths where that cooler 71-degree water was stacking up underneath. I saw it happen at Plum Gut first — a Tuesday outgoing that felt more like October than July, current ripping and bottom temp noticeably cooler than the drift I ran two days earlier. That's your fluke water now. Forget the flats until this settles.

This week the moon is the story. New moon spring tides build through Friday into the weekend, peak alignment landing July 11-13, and that means the current through The Race and Plum Gut is about to get meaner than it's been in three weeks. More exchange means harder push, which means the eddies behind Valiant Rock and off the Orient Point rip reload faster and hold bait longer on the slack. If you've been getting bored fishing that structure on neap tides, this is your reset. I'm also watching the cold wall that collapsed between Block Island and Rhode Island Sound — water jumped over ten degrees on that boundary, which is opening a real bonito and Spanish mackerel corridor stretching from Newport down toward Point Judith. Fishers Island sits right on the doorstep of that. The LI bonito window that opened July 4th is closing out over the next few days, but with that cold wall gone, I wouldn't be surprised if the first fish show up on the east end of the Sound before it's officially shut.

Striped bass fishing has been real, but it's not automatic. I've had two solid nights out of four working the Race on foot from the Orient rocks — big paddletails and 2-ounce Gibbs pencils worked through the seam where the rip meets the eddy, best from an hour before high through the first hour of the drop. Fish have run mixed, mostly schoolies and mid-20s, but there's chatter of real cows showing up in pockets up toward Block Island and Cape Cod this past week, and eastern Sound structure like this holds the same class of fish when the current's right. Plum Gut has been more of a boat game — drifting bucktails, half-ounce to one-ounce depending on the stage, tipped with a strip of fresh bunker, worked along the 30 to 40 foot edge on the west side of the Gut on the outgoing. That's produced better than the slower, weaker tides did two weeks back. The takeaway: bigger tides equal a shorter, harder bite window, but a better one. Don't fish the whole six hours — fish the ninety minutes around the turn.

Fluke have been the more consistent player, just not where guys expect. Inlet and channel drifts are outfishing the open bay right now. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow through the deeper cuts off Plum Gut and the mouth of Orient Harbor, have been putting keepers in the box — nothing giant, mostly 18 to 21 inches, but steady on the outgoing when that upwelled ocean water is flushing through. If you're marking bait stacked tight to bottom in 30-plus feet, slow the drift down and go heavier on the head. That cooler water is concentrated fish instead of spreading them out.

Porgies are exactly where you'd expect — Fishers Island structure, the rockpiles off the Race, bottom rigs with squid and sandworm working 20 to 35 feet on the last two hours of the incoming. That bite has been reliable all season and hasn't changed with anything above. Bluefish have been more show than substance so far — a few chopper-sized fish busting bait on the surface out toward the Gut at first light, gone as fast as they showed. If the bonito corridor pushes this way like I expect, the blues and albies will start overlapping, and that's when things get fun fast — small metal, Deadly Dicks, half-ounce Deceivers, keep them in the bag.

Weakfish — I'll be straight with you, nothing. Water's been warm enough for two-plus weeks now and there's still not a single report worth mentioning. That old rule about hitting 70 degrees triggering a bite clearly isn't the whole story anymore. I'm not chasing that one this month.

Here's what I'd do with the week ahead. If the wind stays out of the northeast through the weekend, I'm fishing the outgoing at Plum Gut Friday and Saturday for bass and fluke both, working the same drift twice — bucktails deep on the first pass, then switching to plugs along the surface eddy as the tide slackens. Sunday and Monday, with the moon at its peak, I want to be on the rocks at Orient at first light watching for surface activity — if bonito or albies have pushed into the corridor, that's when they'll show. Best case, the cold wall keeps collapsing east to west and Fishers Island gets its own pelagic run before the month's out. If it stalls at Block Island, I'll keep grinding bass and fluke on the bigger tides, which on their own are worth the trip.
