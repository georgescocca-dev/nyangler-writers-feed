# Outgoing After Midnight: Bass Own the Deep Hole, Weakfish Still Working the Bridge
*Rough ocean has pushed the real action inside the inlet, and the night tide is where the numbers are.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — August 31** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one window this week, make it the last two hours of the outgoing tide after dark at Shinnecock Inlet. That's the call, and it's not close. The ocean side has been ugly — better than three-foot swell stacking up on a short period out front — which means the stripers and blues that would normally be working the bar and the jetty tips in daylight are tucking into the inlet itself, holding on the current seams and in the deep hole below the Coast Guard station. Daytime boat traffic and sun-bright water push them down and back. Slack water in the middle of the night with a hard outgoing push pulling bait past the rocks is when they come up and feed. That's been the pattern for two weeks now and there's no reason to think it flips before Labor Day.

The bigger picture: warm water sitting the length of the south shore has bait balled up and predator fish staged, waiting on the next trigger. Offshore, the moon's building toward full, and that means more water moving through the inlet and canal on every tide — which concentrates bait tighter against structure and usually kicks the bite up a notch as we get into the weekend. We flagged the inlet rips last week as the place to watch for the first bonito or albie of the season, with warm nearshore water and building bait setting the table, and that read still holds — the ingredients are all there, bunker and bay anchovies pushing through the inlet mouth, but nobody's iced one yet on this end. Worth rigging a spinning rod with small Deadly Dicks or epoxy jigs and keeping it within reach if you're working the rips, because when that push finally slides west from the Rhode Island side, it can happen fast.

Striped bass inside the inlet have been running mostly schoolie to mid-20-inch fish, with the occasional slot mixed in working the seam where the outgoing current collides with the incoming ocean swell just outside the rock pile. Bucktails in the half-ounce to one-ounce range, white or chartreuse, worked slow along bottom on the drift through the deep hole, have been the most consistent producer after dark. Eels fished on a fish-finder rig, drifted naturally with the current rather than retrieved, are still taking the bigger fish that are holding tighter to the rock structure on the ocean side of the jetty. The window is short — really the two hours around max outgoing — but it's been repeatable most nights this week when the wind's been manageable.

Bluefish are the one species nobody's complaining about right now. They're pushing into the inlet on both tides, chopping up peanut bunker along the bay side flats near Tiana Beach and around the mouth, and they'll hit almost anything with flash — Hopkins spoons, bucktails, or bunker chunks fished on wire leader if you don't want your tackle shredded. This has been the most reliable bite of the week for anyone fishing daylight hours, and with bunker schools still building along the south shore, expect that to hold through the holiday weekend regardless of what the bass and fluke do.

Fluke fishing has softened the way it always does this time of year, but it hasn't collapsed — we said last week it would hold steady through the weekend while everyone starts checking the rips, and that's about right. Fair action, not fast, on channel edges throughout Shinnecock Bay, particularly the drop-offs near the canal entrance and the deeper cuts off Tiana. Warm, oxygen-thin bay water is pushing the better fish toward the inlet and ocean-front structure where there's more flow. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the drift in eighteen to twenty-five feet, have been outproducing bait-only rigs. Expect this pattern — fair fishing, concentrated near structure and current — to hold through most of September before the real fall exodus offshore begins.

Weakfish at the Ponquogue Bridge have been a night bite almost exclusively, which suits this column just fine. The pilings on the bay side, particularly the ones closest to the deeper channel water, have been holding fish that show up as the tide starts to move after dark — not necessarily max flow, but that first hour or two of current building either way. Bucktails tipped with strips of squid, or small soft plastics like three-inch Fin-S in white or bone, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right down in the current break next to the piling, have taken keeper weakfish along with the usual porgy bycatch. It's not a blitz-style bite — more a patient, one-or-two-a-drift kind of night — but it's been consistent enough to make the trip worthwhile if you're willing to fish the dark hours.

Porgies remain a solid backup throughout the bay, particularly around the canal and the deeper holes off Hampton Bays, taking sandworms and clam on standard bottom rigs pretty much any tide. Blackfish season is still off in the distance, but with cooler nights starting to show up, it's not too early to start thinking about the structure spots inside the inlet that'll turn on once that opens.

Looking ahead: the moon builds toward full over the next several days, which should tighten up the tidal push and, combined with the bait already staged along the coast, sets up the kind of conditions that trigger the first real blitz activity of the fall transition. Watch the inlet rips hard for bonito sign — breaking fish, diving birds, bait getting nervous on the surface — because if that push arrives from the east like it has in past seasons, Shinnecock is usually one of the first Long Island inlets to see it. Rough ocean conditions look likely to persist into the weekend, so keep leaning on the inside water and the night tide until that changes.
