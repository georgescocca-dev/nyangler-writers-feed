# The West Rip Cut Deeper This Week — Bunker Stacked In It, Bass Still Won't Commit
*Moriches Inlet's sandbar has reshaped the drift lines again, and the bait is thick, but the stripers are staged, not feeding hard yet.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — September 4** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The west rip off Cupsogue has scoured out noticeably since the last blow — the sandbar that sits off the jetty has pushed the main channel current tighter against the rocks, and the hole there is running a couple feet deeper than it was in July. That matters because it changes where the bait stacks on the ebb. Bunker schools pouring through the inlet on the outgoing tide are now holding in that deepened pocket instead of spreading across the flat like they did earlier in the summer, and that concentration is exactly what's been drawing bluefish into tight, repeatable ambush zones rather than scattered pods.

The bigger story this week is bait density building everywhere from Moriches west, and that's the trigger everyone on the bay is watching. We flagged this last week — said the bunker schools stacking in the back bays and along the south shore corridor would need to tighten up before the stripers committed in numbers, and that's precisely the pattern setting up now. Water in the low-to-mid 70s is still warm enough to keep bass staged rather than blitzing. They're sitting under and around the bunker schools in the inlet and in the deeper guts off Tuthill Point, but they're not yet pushing bait to the surface with any consistency. That should change as the moon builds toward full on the 6th-7th — stronger tidal flow tightens loose bunker schools into the kind of dense, panicked balls that trigger real blitz behavior. Right now we're in the staging phase, not the eruption.

Bluefish are the one thing anyone can count on this week, and that's held true basin-wide. Choppers in the 3-to-6-pound class are running the inlet mouth and the rip off Cupsogue on both the last two hours of the incoming and the first two of the outgoing, hammering bunker spoons and 2-ounce diamond jigs worked with a fast, erratic retrieve through the current seam. Some better fish, pushing 8 pounds, have come from the deeper cut on the west side of the inlet on cut bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig with 40-pound leader — the bluefish don't seem to care that the water's still warm, and that lines up with what we expected heading into Labor Day week. Anyone working peanut bunker imitations — 3-inch white or bunker-colored soft plastics on a 3/4-ounce jighead — through the same current edge has been getting cut off regularly, which tells you the blues are thick even when they're not visibly blitzing on top.

Striped bass action inside the bay has been a mixed bag, and it's worth being straight about that. The better fish — mostly mid-20s to low-30-inch, with a scattering of overslot models pushing 34 — are coming out of the deeper water off Tuthill Point and along the channel edge running past Smith Point on the last two hours of the ebb, when the outgoing current pulls bait out of the marsh creeks and funnels it past the deeper structure. Bucktails in the 1-to-1.5-ounce range, white or chartreuse, fished with a slow bottom bounce through that current seam have been the most consistent producer. Live bunker on a fish-finder rig, drifted right along the drop-off where the channel meets the flat, has also worked for the bigger fish holding deeper and less willing to chase. But this isn't wide-open blitz fishing yet — most reports describe scattered fish that need to be worked methodically rather than schools you can run-and-gun on.

Fluke are winding down, as expected for early September, but they haven't disappeared. The better doormats have moved off the shallow back-bay flats and out toward the deeper structure near the inlet mouth and the ocean-side edges off Westhampton, exactly the seasonal shift we called out last week — normal transition, not a collapse. Boats working the deeper holes in 15 to 25 feet with 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the drift, are still picking off keeper-class fish in the 18-to-20-inch range, with an occasional fish pushing 4 pounds. The window for that pattern is narrowing fast as water temperatures ease down through the month, so anyone still chasing fluke here should treat the next two to three weeks as the tail end, not the middle, of the season.

Weakfish have been quiet, which tracks with typical early-September behavior in this system — most of what's being caught is incidental, mixed in with the bass bite along the deeper channel edges on bucktails and soft plastics. Porgies and blackfish are still mostly a structure game around the inlet rocks and the bulkheads at Tuthill Point, best worked on the last of the ebb with green crab or clam on a hi-lo rig, and that bite should firm up as water cools into fall.

Looking ahead, the next several days hinge on that building moon and whatever wind shows up behind it. If the tidal flow strengthens as expected and the first real cool nights start knocking water temps down even a degree or two, the bunker schools stacking in the inlet and along the ocean-side edges should tighten enough to trigger the first legitimate blitz activity of the fall — most likely first light or last light on the stronger ebb tides, right where that deepened west rip funnels bait past the jetty. Until that happens, the smart play is fishing the current seams methodically rather than waiting for surface chaos — the bass are there, they're just not desperate yet.
