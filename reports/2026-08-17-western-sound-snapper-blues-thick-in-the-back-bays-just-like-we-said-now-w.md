# Snapper Blues Thick in the Back Bays, Just Like We Said — Now Watch the Adults
*Porgies are paying the bills on the Western Sound while fluke stay undersized and stripers keep banker's hours before the afternoon thunder rolls in.*

**CITY ISLAND, NY — August 17** — _by Frank "The Sound Pro" Marinaro, Zone Writer_

Guy at the bait shop in City Island Monday morning was griping that he'd measured forty fluke off the Throgs Neck rocks Sunday and kept exactly none of them. I told him that's not bad luck, that's mid-August on the Western Sound — everything's running twelve to eighteen inches right now and you've got to kiss a lot of frogs before you find a prince. He wasn't thrilled, but that's the honest read on the flatfish this week and I'm not going to sell him a different story.

What I will tell him — and you — is that porgies have quietly become the best game going. Anywhere there's structure and current, from the rockpile off Hart Island down to the mussel beds inside Manhasset Bay, scup are stacked and biting like they know summer's got an expiration date. Small hooks, a strip of squid or a piece of clam, half-ounce sinker to hold bottom on the drift — that's the whole program. I've had guys limit out in ninety minutes working the drop-off east of Hart Island on the last two hours of the incoming, water still carrying that mid-70s warmth that's got everything with fins in a feeding mood.

Stripers are a different animal right now, and if you're not on the water by 6 AM you're chasing shadows. Kings Point is reading right around 71 degrees, New London's a good degree and a half cooler, and that west-to-east gradient is exactly why the Western Sound still holds fish tight to structure instead of scattering them thin. Execution Rocks on the tail of the outgoing has been good for schoolies and the occasional mid-20s fish on bunker chunks fished on the bottom, or a white bucktail worked slow along the edge where the rock drops into deeper water. Stepping Stones has been quieter than it was in the spring — that light isn't the highway it is in May — but I've still marked fish holding on the down-current side early, before the sun gets up and the boat traffic pushes them down.

By nine, nine-thirty, the thermometer and the humidity both start climbing and those afternoon thunderstorm cells build right on schedule most days this week. The bass go quiet, the bite shuts like someone flipped a switch, and you're better off running for porgies or blackfish than beating a dead horse on stripers past mid-morning. That's been the pattern for two weeks now and I don't see anything in the sky or the water changing it before we get some real relief in temperature.

Bluefish are scattered but present, mostly around Oyster Bay and off Matinecock Point where there's still enough bait moving to hold their interest. We flagged this a few days back — said the snapper blues thick in the back bays and estuaries were a sign the adult class would keep building, and that's holding up. I'm seeing more three- and four-pound blues mixed in with the schoolies off Matinecock than I was two weeks ago, and if that trend keeps climbing through late August into September like it usually does, we'll have a real fall run setting up early. Small metal — quarter-ounce Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — worked fast on the surface has been the ticket when you find them working bait.

Weakfish have shown up in dribs and drabs around the mouth of Hempstead Harbor on the last of the outgoing, mostly smaller fish, nothing to write home about, but worth a cast or two with a bucktail tipped with a strip of squid if you're already out there for porgies.

One thing worth saying plainly: the upper harbors — Little Neck Bay, the inner reaches of Manhasset — have taken a hit from runoff after the recent rain, and the water in there is carrying more color and less oxygen than it should this time of year. If you fished a spot in there two weeks ago and it's been dead since, that's likely why. The fish know it too — they're pushing out toward cleaner, better-oxygenated water in the open Sound, which is part of why Execution Rocks and the Matinecock drop-off have stayed more consistent than the back bays.

Looking ahead, we're building toward the August 28 full moon, and that means tidal flow is going to sharpen up through the week. More current means better current edges, and better current edges mean the bass and blues both get pushed into tighter, more predictable feeding lanes — Execution Rocks, the Matinecock rip, the down-current side of Stepping Stones. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd want an outgoing tide starting an hour before first light off Execution Rocks, bunker chunk on the bottom to start, bucktail in hand for when the current gets moving. Get there early. Once those thunderheads start stacking up over the city, you've had your window.
