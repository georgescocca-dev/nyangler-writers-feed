# Block Island sits in a data fog as June bass push onto the Southwest Ledge
*With offshore buoys offline and the SST package three days stale, structure and tide stage become the only reliable read on the island bite.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — June 10** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

The honest opening today is that the ocean is talking quieter than usual. The offshore buoy network that normally frames a Block Island report — 44017 south of Montauk, 44097 on the shelf, 44025, 44039 and 44040 — is either offline or not reporting wind, and the SST composite package out of the analyst desk has not refreshed since June 8. That leaves us reasoning from a two-day-old satellite snapshot, the seasonal clock, and what little inshore signal is coming off the bay. For a beat where the entire premise is cool, oxygenated, current-swept water pulling bass off the mid-Atlantic and stacking them on rips and ledges, that data gap matters, and it has to be named up front rather than papered over with invented numbers.

What we can say with confidence is calendar-driven. By June 10 in a normal year the surface around the island is running in the low-to-mid 60s, the thermocline is still shallow and disorganized, and the bass that staged through Watch Hill and Point Judith in late May are now committed to the island's structure. The Southwest Ledge, the North Rip, and the Southeast corner off Black Rock are the three engines, and the controlling variable on each of them right now is tide stage, not water column temperature. With the bait — sand eels primary, juvenile scup and small bunker secondary — pinned to structure, the fish are eating on moving water and sulking on slack. That argues for a slot-and-overslot mixed class on the ledges during the strong middle hours of the ebb, and a shot at the bigger resident fish at the top of the flood after dark on the southwest corner.

On the ground, the signal coming back from the inshore fleet is thin but consistent with the season. Sea bass season is open and producing on the wind farm towers and the rockpiles south and southeast of the island in 70 to 110 feet, with the better grade of fish holding on the deeper structure as the shallower pieces get worked over. Fluke reports out of the island sand have been streaky — a function of drift speed more than fish density — and when the tide rips hard enough to bury a four-ounce bucktail, the better play has been tucking inside toward the southwest bell or working the lee depending on wind. Porgy numbers around Newport and the lower bay are building toward their summer peak, and the early tog window before the July 1 closure is winding down on the deeper boulder fields.

The bass story itself is the one worth paying attention to. The North Rip on a hard ebb with a live eel drifted on a three-way is the classic June program and there is no reason to think it has changed; what changes year to year is whether the fish are predominantly slot-class schoolies or whether the 30-to-40-pound class has moved in yet. By the second week of June the larger fish are typically here, holding deep on the ledge edges in 40 to 60 feet and feeding on the turn. Wire line with a parachute and pork rind during the day, live eels at the tide change after dark — that is the assignment.

Looking out three to five days, the questions I want answered are whether the SST pipeline catches up and confirms that the 60-degree isotherm has consolidated south and east of the island, and whether the offshore buoys come back online in time to read the next southwest blow. A sustained southwest wind against an ebb on the Southwest Ledge is the single most productive setup of the early summer on this beat, and we are in the moon window for it. If the wind cooperates and the bait stays pinned, the next good tide on the ledge should produce the first credible 40-pound report of the season. If the wind goes hard south and piles warm surface water against the island, expect the bigger fish to slide off into deeper water on the southeast side and the bite to get more technical.

For now the read is patient and structural. Fish the rips on moving water, respect the slack, keep eels in the bait well, and treat any number you see quoted from a buoy this week with appropriate skepticism until the network is fully back up.
