# Canyon water fires as shelf hits 67 degrees — yellowfin and mahi crash the Spencer
*Warm water surge pushes pelagics into feeding mode at the southern canyons.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — June 19** — _by Capt. Ray Lugo, Zone Writer_

The southern canyons are lighting up like a Christmas tree this week, and it's all about that magic 67-degree mark we've been waiting for. Spencer Canyon is the star of the show right now, with yellowfin tuna in the 40-60 pound class crushing spreads at first light and mahi mahi moving in behind them as the sun climbs.

I've been running Spencer hard the past three trips, working the 100-fathom curve where the canyon wall drops into the blue. Water temp is holding steady at 67-68 degrees on the shelf — that's the threshold that flips the switch for these fish. The yellowfin are coming up on the troll between 5:30 and 7:30 AM, hitting cedar plugs and small feathers pulled at 7-8 knots. Pink and white Ilanders with ballyhoo have been money, especially the 9-inch size rigged with a stinger hook.

What's really got me excited is the mahi action behind the tuna bite. Once the sun gets up and the yellowfin go deep, the dolphins move in to clean up. They're not giants — most running 10-20 pounds — but they're aggressive and plentiful. Small spoons and Hogy lures worked around any floating debris are producing steady action through mid-morning.

Lindenkohl Canyon is running a close second, particularly on the eastern wall where the 20-fathom curve bends north. The water's a degree cooler there, but the bait concentration is incredible. I'm seeing massive schools of sand eels and small mackerel getting worked by birds, and that's drawing everything from wahoo to white marlin. The wahoo bite has been sporadic but violent — wire leaders and high-speed trolling with Rapala X-Raps in the blue/silver pattern.

Poor Man's Canyon is producing the most consistent action for smaller boats. The run from Ocean City puts you on fish in 90 minutes, and the protection from the southwest wind we've been getting makes it fishable when Spencer gets lumpy. Bluefin tuna in the 30-50 pound class are showing on the northwest corner, feeding on the surface at dawn. They're spooky — long leaders and small offerings are key. I've had success with 4-inch Hogy eels on 40-pound fluorocarbon, cast ahead of the school and worked back slowly.

The key to this whole bite is understanding what's happening with the water. That 67-degree shelf temperature is creating a feeding corridor that's pulling pelagics in from the deep water. The thermal break is setting up right at the canyon edges, concentrating bait and creating the perfect ambush zones for tuna and mahi. When you combine that with the steady southwest flow we've had, it's pushing warm Gulf Stream water right up against the continental shelf.

Norfolk Canyon has been the sleeper this week. It's the longest run from my home ports, but the payoff is there for crews willing to make the trip. White marlin are showing in good numbers on the southern wall, particularly around the 50-fathom curve. Circle hooks with ballyhoo on the long rigger, pulled at 6 knots, have been the ticket. Most fish are coming up between 10 AM and 2 PM when the sun angle is right.

Bait has been the story everywhere. The sand eel schools are thick from the 30-fathom line out to the canyons, and that's what's driving this whole food chain. When you see the birds working, get your spread out fast — the tuna and mahi aren't far behind.

Looking ahead, this pattern should hold through the weekend and into next week. The water temperature is stable, the bait is thick, and the moon phase is right for continued feeding activity. Southwest winds are forecast to continue, which keeps the warm water pushed up against the shelf. If you're planning a canyon trip, Spencer and Lindenkohl are your best bets for variety, while Poor Man's offers the most reliable action for a shorter run. Just remember — early morning is prime time, so plan to be on the grounds before sunrise.
