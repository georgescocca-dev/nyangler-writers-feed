# Bunker Round the Hook: Blues Blow Up on Top, Bass Slide Under at First Light
*The bunker we said were coming from Belmar showed up right on schedule — now it's about picking your window before the neap softens everything.*

**HIGHLANDS, NJ — August 3** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

I made the call Sunday morning before I even had coffee in me: run to Flynn's Knoll first, not Romer Shoal, because the bunker pods I'd been watching stack off Keyport all week were finally thick enough to hold fish through a full tide change instead of scattering on the turn. That's the difference this time of year — August bunker schools are nervous, they get pushed around by every boat and every blitz, and if you're not sitting on the ones that are settled, you're just burning gas watching birds work water you already left.

We flagged this two weeks back — said the bunker pushing north out of the Belmar blitz zone would round the Hook and pull blues and bass in behind them within a day or two — and that's exactly what happened. Raritan Bay has bunker stacked from the Chapel Hill Channel all the way up into the Reach, and the bluefish found them fast. This is as good a topwater bluefish stretch as I've seen in this bay in a few years. Poppers and bunker spoons worked over breaking pods at Flynn's Knoll and along the edge of Romer Shoal are getting hit on nearly every cast when the blitz is up — 3 to 6 pound blues mostly, with some better ones mixed in that'll strip drag if you're using anything lighter than 20-pound braid. Best window has been first light through about 8 AM, before the boat traffic scatters the pods and pushes them down. By midday the bunker sound deep and the surface action shuts off — that's when guys switch to jigging bucktails or diamond jigs down 15 to 25 feet where the pods are holding tight to bottom structure off the Knoll.

Striped bass are in the mix, but they're playing it smart the way they always do once the water sits in the mid-to-upper 70s like it is now. You're not going to find keeper bass chasing bunker on the surface at noon — they're down deep on structure, holding along the Ambrose Channel edges and the deeper humps off Romer Shoal through the day, and coming up to feed under the bunker schools right at dawn and again as the light fails in the evening. I had my best bass action last week live-lining bunker under a balloon rig, drifting the edge where the Knoll drops into the channel, right on the tail of the incoming tide — three fish between 28 and 34 inches in about forty minutes before the sun got too high and they dropped back down. After that window closes, if you want bass, you go to bucktails and eels worked slow along the bottom near the bridge pilings in the Highlands or right in the Navesink River mouth after dark. That's been the more reliable bass program lately — not exciting, but it produces.

Fluke are having their moment too, and honestly this might be the best all-around week of the summer to fish the bay because you can bounce between blitz action and a solid doormat bite without changing zip codes. Keyport Flats has been holding fish in 12 to 18 feet, but the better-grade fluke — I had one go just over 5 pounds Thursday — are coming off the channel edges where the flat drops into deeper water, working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on ¾-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow on the last two hours of the outgoing. The Chapel Hill Channel edge and the drop just off Ideal Beach have both been productive on that same pattern. Porgies are still around the reef structure off the Hook for anyone who wants a change of pace and dinner that doesn't require a fight.

Here's the thing to watch this week: we're sliding into a neap tide around August 5-6, and that means less water movement, softer current everywhere in the bay. That's usually bad news for current-dependent bites, but it can actually concentrate bunker pods that would otherwise get pushed around by a stronger tide — so don't be surprised if the blitz stays tight and predictable rather than spreading out. The tradeoff is the fluke bite may go a little quieter on those slack tide stretches, since less current means less bait movement off the channel edges. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take the calmer morning, hit Flynn's Knoll right at first light for blues and any bass riding under the pods, then slide over to the Chapel Hill Channel edge for the last of the outgoing to hunt fluke before the tide goes dead. Keep an eye on the bunker — as long as they stay thick in the bay, this pattern holds. If a blow scatters them out toward the ocean side of the Hook, the whole program resets and we're back to hunting.
