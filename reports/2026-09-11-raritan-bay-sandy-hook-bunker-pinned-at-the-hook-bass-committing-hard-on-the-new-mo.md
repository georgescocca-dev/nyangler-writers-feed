# Bunker Pinned at the Hook: Bass Committing Hard on the New-Moon Push
*The first real blitz signs of the fall run are showing from the Highlands to Romer Shoal — here's where to be and what's actually biting.*

**HIGHLANDS, NJ — September 11** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one window this week, spend it working the stretch from the Navesink River mouth out past the Highlands bridge and down to the Rip at the Hook. That's where the bait is getting squeezed, and squeezed bait is the whole story right now. The new moon on the 10th put the strongest tidal push of the early fall season into the bay, and it's doing exactly what a big new-moon tide is supposed to do — pinning bunker schools tight against the beach, the river mouths, and the Hook's inside edge where the current funnels everything south. We flagged this last week — said the bunker balls were tightening under the building moon and that this tide would be the trigger for the season's first real blitz behavior. It's here.

The bunker aren't just sitting anymore, they're nervous, balled up tight and skipping on top, and the bass under them aren't picking at stragglers — they're committing. That's the difference between August and September in this bay. Reports out of the Highlands fleet this week have boats marking big bunker schools stacked from the base of the bridge down through the Rip, with fish blowing up under them on the last two hours of the ebb as that bait gets pushed out past the Hook into open water. The better bite has been working the edges of those schools rather than the middle — snag a bunker on a weighted treble, drop it back on a fish-finder rig into 15 to 25 feet right where the school thins out, and let it sink on a semi-slack line. Bass in the mid-20s to low 30-inch class have been the read, with a scattering of bigger fish mixed in on the deeper drops off Romer Shoal where the channel drops to 30-plus feet. Chunking fresh bunker on the bottom, fished on the last of the ebb into the start of the flood, has been the more consistent producer than snag-and-drop when the schools get spread out and skittish, which happens fast once bird activity gives away the position.

Fluke are a different conversation right now, and the honest read is that the good window has mostly closed. The keeper-class fish that were working the channel edges off Keyport Flats and down through Flynn's Knoll through July and August have largely slid off toward deeper, cooler water the way they do every September — that's the standard late-season pattern, bigger fish peeling out ahead of the real cold while the juveniles hang back in the shallows. What's left inside the bay right now is mostly short fish, plenty of them, but not much worth keeping. If you still want to run a drift, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails along the channel edge from Flynn's Knoll toward the Chapel Hill Channel will still put bites in the boat, just don't expect much size. Better use of tide time this week is on the bass and blues.

Speaking of blues — snapper blues are thick right now in the Navesink and Shrewsbury, exactly the kind of sign that usually shows up a few weeks ahead of the bigger bluefish pushing into the bay proper. Dock talk from the Highlands side has kids and families picking snappers on small silver spoons and bits of bunker off the docks and bulkheads on any stage of tide, which is a good early tell. Once those snappers grow up and adult blues start following the bunker migration down from the north, expect that bite to show up hard around Romer Shoal and the outer edge of the Knoll, likely within the next couple weeks as water temps keep sliding.

Looking at the next 72 hours, the tide is still carrying residual push from the new moon even as it eases off peak strength, so the window for blitz-style surface activity should hold, maybe even sharpen, particularly on strong ebb tides pulling bait past the Hook toward the ocean side. The best two windows to target are the last two hours of the ebb and the first hour of the incoming, when bait gets compressed against structure and predators pin it rather than chase it into open water. By the weekend, as the moon moves further off new phase, expect the tidal energy to soften a touch — that usually means the blitzes get shorter and more localized rather than disappearing outright, so keep moving if the birds go quiet in one spot. The bunker migration itself isn't slowing down, and as long as that bait keeps stacking against the beach and the river mouths, the bass and the early blues are going to keep showing up to eat it. Worth clearing the schedule for a morning tide this week before the pattern shifts again.
