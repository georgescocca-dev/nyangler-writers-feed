# North jetty cut stacking peanuts on the ebb — bass finally showing behind the blues
*Harvest Moon tides are jamming bait against the rocks at Barnegat and Beach Haven, and the first real striper push of the fall is riding in behind it.*

**SHIP BOTTOM, NJ — September 21** — _by Tommy Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Walk the north end at low light this week and the story is written right in the sand. The cut that formed off the Barnegat Light rock pile after the August blow has held its shape and deepened a touch — there's a defined trough running maybe 60 feet off the rocks now, and it's holding peanut bunker in tight, nervous clouds that scatter and re-form every time something pushes through underneath them. That's the tell. Ten days ago this same stretch was just white water and shorts on bloodworm rigs. Now there's bait stacked against structure, and bait against structure this time of year means one thing is coming.

The Harvest Moon is doing exactly what it's supposed to do. Bigger swings on the tide chart mean more water moving harder through the inlets — Barnegat and Little Egg both — and that current is what's pinning the peanut schools against the jetty rocks and the bulkheads instead of letting them spread out over open sand. We flagged this a few days back, that once the mullet schools tightened up against the beach the blues and the early bass would show hard behind them, and that's precisely the pattern setting up now from the north jetty down through Ship Bottom and into Beach Haven.

Bluefish are the headline right now, not stripers — let's be straight about that. The blitzes breaking at Barnegat Inlet on the last two hours of the ebb have been mostly cocktail blues in the 2- to 4-pound range, with some better fish mixed in tearing through the peanut pods hard enough to leave slicks on the surface. Metal is the call — half-ounce to one-ounce Deadly Dicks and Kastmasters worked fast through the froth, or bucktails in white and chartreuse bounced just under the melee if the blues are sounding. Poppers will draw strikes too when the fish are up and slashing, which has mostly been first light and again in that last hour of outgoing water before the bottom of the tide.

Stripers are mixing in, but thin still — a fish here and there in the wash at the north end jetties and around the rock piles at Holgate, mostly at night or in that gray window right before sunrise. Nobody's calling it a blitz yet. The pattern to watch is peanut bunker imitations worked slow and low — 3-inch SP minnows in bunker or chartreuse, or a black Bomber swimming plug fished just off the drop-off where the trough meets the sandbar. As more bunker funnel down the beachfront over the next tide cycles, expect the bass numbers to build behind them, especially once the water starts losing a few more degrees at night.

Fluke are still cooperating, which is a little unusual for this deep into September, but the warmer-than-normal water sitting nearshore has kept them shallow and feeding later than a typical fall. Beach Haven flats and the deeper guts off Holgate have been giving up keeper-class fish on cut mullet strips and bloodworms fished on a simple hi-lo rig, bounced slow along the bottom on the last of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing when the current is still moving but not ripping. This bite is on borrowed time — any real cold push through the region will tighten it up fast, so if fluke is the target, this week and next are the window, not November.

Kingfish are still in the wash the way they've been all month, working the same troughs as the fluke on bloodworm and sand flea rigs fished tight to the beach in two to four feet of water. Small hooks, light sinkers, patience — the usual fall surf grind, but steady enough to be worth the sand fleas if that's your speed.

Back in the estuary, Little Egg Inlet and the flats around Graveling Point are carrying the fall weakfish run at a slow simmer. Nothing explosive, but a few fish in the 16- to 20-inch range have come off small soft plastics and bucktails worked along the channel edges on the dropping tide, particularly where the current sweeps bait off the flats and funnels it toward the deeper cuts. The Mullica is starting to show the same mullet activity that's driving the ocean-side bite, so keep an eye on that river mouth and the mouth of Great Bay over the next week — that's usually where the back-bay bass show up first once the main run gets rolling.

Looking ahead, the next few days should keep building on this pattern as long as the Harvest Moon tides stay strong and the wind doesn't turn hard onshore and blow the water dirty. If the forecast holds clean with modest wind, the smart play is working the inlets and jetty points on the last two hours of the ebb, when the current is draining bait past structure and the blues and early bass are stacking to intercept it. The bigger question is the first real cold front of the season — once temperatures start dropping at night in a serious way, expect the fluke bite to shut down quickly while the striper push accelerates and gets more serious. For now, the smart bet is fishing the tide changes at Barnegat Inlet, the Ship Bottom jetties, and Holgate's rock piles, with a rod rigged for blues on top and a second rigged slow and subsurface for the bass that are starting to slide in behind them.
