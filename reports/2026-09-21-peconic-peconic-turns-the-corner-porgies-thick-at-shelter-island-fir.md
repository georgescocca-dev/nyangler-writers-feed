# Peconic Turns the Corner — Porgies Thick at Shelter Island, First Bass Filter Into Plum Gut
*The bay is cooling ahead of the ocean side, and that head start is exactly what's pulling bass, weakfish, and porgies into position for the next two weeks.*

**GREENPORT, NY — September 21** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

The equinox is the real marker out here, more than any calendar date. The Peconic starts shedding heat before the ocean side even notices, and that's the story of this week — the bay's clock is running ahead of the Sound and the open Atlantic, and the fish know it. Bay scallop season opened right on top of this transition, which always stirs up bottom activity and pulls bait into the creeks and coves as boats work the grass flats. That disturbed, bait-rich water is doing exactly what it always does this time of year: drawing porgies tighter to structure and pulling the first real numbers of striped bass off the ocean side and into the bay mouths.

We flagged this shift last week, saying the false albacore run would keep improving as it pushed west — and that's held. Albies have been showing consistently around Orient Point and into the Gut on the last two hours of the ebb, chasing bay anchovies and rain bait in the rips. Nothing has changed in that pattern except the numbers are better than they were ten days ago.

The bigger story right now is the bass. Montauk's bunker pods have been pushing west along the ocean beaches for a couple weeks, and that forage is finally spilling into the bay mouths at Orient and through Plum Gut. It's early — this isn't the full fall run yet — but the first wave of migratory bass is showing up shadowing those bunker schools tight to structure, exactly the pattern we said to expect. Boats working the Gut on the last of the ebb into the start of the flood have been picking up bass to the low 30-inch class on bunker spoons and umbrella rigs trolled along the rock pile edges, with some fish coming on live bunker drifted on a fish-finder rig in 20 to 35 feet of water where the current squeezes hardest. Orient Point itself, off the lighthouse rip, has produced a few better fish on bucktails worked slow along the bottom as the tide turns — that's the Skinner-style presentation, cast across the seam, let it sink clean off the edge, and work it back with a slow rhythmic bounce. It's working here the same way it's working on the ocean beaches.

Porgies are the sure thing right now, and Shelter Island is the address. The stretch between Big Ram and Cedar Island, along with the drop-off outside Coecles Harbor, has been holding thick schools of porgies in 15 to 25 feet over mussel and sand-mud bottom. High-low rigs with size 6 or 8 hooks, sandworm or clam strips, fished on the last two hours of the incoming tide have been the standard producer — porgies stack up on structure as the current builds and feed hardest right before the top of the tide. Smith Cove and the water off North Ferry have also been steady, though the fish there run a little smaller on average. This bite doesn't look like it's slowing down anytime soon; if anything, the cooling water should concentrate them tighter to the deeper edges as we move into October.

Fluke are tapering, which is normal for this point in the season, but they haven't shut off. The channels have been the play — Noyack Bay's deeper cut, the drop along Jessups Neck, and the stretch through the Route 114 bridge area have all given up keeper-class fish on white Gulp Swimming Mullets tipped on 3/4-ounce bucktails, worked slow on the drift with the tide. Best window has been the last of the outgoing into dead low, when bait gets funneled through the narrower cuts and fluke stack on the down-current side waiting on it. Don't expect the numbers of August — this is a fish-or-two-per-drift bite now, not a limit bite — but the quality has actually held up better than the quantity, with a few fish over four pounds mixed in around Jessups.

Weakfish are still showing in small pockets, mostly around Three Mile Harbor and the deeper holes off Noyack, working the same tide windows as the fluke. Nothing to build a trip around, but worth a few casts with a bucktail-and-worm combo if you're already fishing that water. Blackfish haven't turned on yet — water's not cold enough to push them tight to the rock piles and mussel beds around Shelter Island and Plum Gut, but that switch usually flips in the first two weeks of October, and it's worth keeping half an eye on as the nights keep cooling.

Looking at the next few days, the pattern should keep building rather than breaking. The bigger tides around this moon are moving more water through the Gut and the bay mouths, which is exactly what pulls bait — and the bass tracking it — deeper into the system. If the wind stays out of the north and northwest and doesn't stir the bay into a mess, the porgy bite at Shelter Island should stay dependable through the week, and the bass filtering through Plum Gut and Orient should keep building numbers as more bunker gets pushed west. The one thing that could slow all of it down is a real cold front — if temperatures drop hard in the next week, expect the fluke bite to fade fast and the bass push to accelerate even quicker as fish start staging for the run south. For now, porgies at Shelter Island are the safest bet on the calendar, and the bass at the Gut are the one worth checking daily, because this is the point in the season where that bite can go from a trickle to a real push almost overnight.
