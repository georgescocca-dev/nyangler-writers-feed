# Why are the blues showing up in the bass blitzes? Absecon and Great Egg on the Harvest Moon tides
*Bigger bunker schools and bigger tides are stacking bait at the inlets, and the fluke and kingfish bite hasn't read the calendar yet.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — September 21** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

Every fall I get some version of the same question from readers standing at the rail at Absecon Inlet: why does the bite suddenly organize itself around the jetties and inlet mouths this time of year, when for most of September it's been scattered up and down the beachfront? The short answer is the moon and the bait. The Harvest Moon tides that peaked this week are bigger than anything we've seen since the new moon runs of August, and bigger tides move more water, which means more current, which means bait gets funneled and concentrated instead of drifting around loose. Peanut and adult menhaden schools that have been working their way down the beachfront from Brigantine to Stone Harbor are getting squeezed against the inlet mouths on the last of the ebb and the first of the flood, and everything with a mouth in this zone knows it.

That's the mechanism behind what the fleet is seeing right now — bluefish riding shotgun on those bunker schools, and stripers starting to mix in underneath them. We flagged this pattern last week, saying bluefish activity would intensify as bigger bait waves pushed south, and that's exactly what's playing out along the Absecon and Great Egg fronts this week. The blitzes aren't constant — they're tide-windowed, mostly the last hour of the ebb into the first hour of the flood at first and last light — but when the bait balls up tight against the rocks at Absecon Inlet or along the Great Egg jetty, the surface erupts fast and it's worth having a rod rigged and ready rather than digging through the box.

Water temperatures holding warmer than typical for late September are the other half of this story, and they're the reason the fluke and kingfish programs haven't shut down the way they usually would by now. The sod banks in the back bay behind Absecon and up into the Brigantine marsh are still stacking short-to-keeper fluke on the outgoing tide, working 3-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the mud-to-sand transitions in 8 to 15 feet. Great Egg Inlet's channel edges have been doing the same thing on the last two hours of the ebb — that's when the bait gets pulled off the flats and the fluke stack up on the drop-offs waiting for it. Keeper-class fish to 20 inches have been coming out of both spots, though the ratio of shorts to keepers is still running heavy, so expect to cull through some fish to find dinner.

The surf has stayed productive longer than it has any right to this late in September, and that's squarely a warm-water story. Kingfish are still working the troughs at Ocean City, Sea Isle City and Stone Harbor on bloodworms and sand fleas fished on high-low rigs, best on the incoming tide when the wash gets some push to it without turning rough. Cut mullet strips in the same troughs have been picking off a mix of kingfish and the occasional early-run weakfish at dusk, though the weakfish bite specifically has stayed thin and inconsistent this month — worth a few casts if you're already rigged for kingfish, not worth a dedicated trip yet. Triggerfish are still holding around the rock piles at Absecon Inlet and the Townsends Inlet jetty rocks, a sign the water hasn't turned the corner into true fall temperatures — small pieces of clam or squid fished tight to the rocks on light tackle will still find them.

For the next 48 to 72 hours, the bigger Harvest Moon tides should keep sharpening this pattern rather than breaking it. Bait density at the inlet mouths should keep building, and that means the bluefish-into-bass blitzes at first and last light around Absecon and Great Egg should keep getting more predictable, not less. If you only have one morning, start at the Absecon Inlet jetty on the last hour of the ebb — that's where the bait gets pinched hardest and where the bass have been showing under the blues most consistently. Bring both a bucktail for working the bottom and a metal — a Deadly Dick or an Ava jig — for when the fish are up in the wash chasing bait on top.

The thing to watch for over the back half of this week is any real cold front. Everything productive right now — the fluke holding shallow, the kingfish still in the wash, the triggerfish sitting on the rocks — is a function of water that's warmer than it should be for late September. The first legitimate cold front that drops nearshore temps will tighten the fluke bite fast and likely push some of these stripers further along on their run south rather than holding them at the inlets. Until that happens, the back bay sod banks and the inlet mouths at tide change remain the two best classroom lessons this zone has to offer right now — pay attention to which one the calendar takes away first.
