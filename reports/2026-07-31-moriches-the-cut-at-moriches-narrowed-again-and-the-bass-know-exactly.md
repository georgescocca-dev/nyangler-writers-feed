# The Cut at Moriches Narrowed Again — And the Bass Know Exactly Where It Went
*A summer shoal is choking the inlet down to one hard lane, and everything from bunker to bass is funneling through it on the last two hours of the ebb.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — July 31** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

I ran the depth finder through the inlet Tuesday on a dropping tide and the number that jumped out wasn't the depth — it was how fast it changed. Eighteen feet off the Cupsogue side, then eleven, then six inside of eighty yards. That's the shoal off the east jetty building out again, the same one that reformed after the spring nor'easters, and it's doing what it always does in high summer: squeezing the whole outgoing flow into one narrow, hard-running lane along the Westhampton side of the channel. If you fished this inlet last month and the drift felt different, that's why. The bottom moved.

That narrowed lane is exactly where the bait is getting stacked and exactly where the bluefish are working it over. We flagged this pattern last week — said the bunker-driven blitzes at the inlet and beachfront would hold with the tide building, and that's precisely what's happening. Peanut bunker are getting pinched through that cut on the last two hours of the ebb, and the blues are waiting on the seam where the fast water meets the eddy behind the jetty rocks. Cast a 1-ounce Deadly Dick or a chrome Kastmaster into the boil, let it swing, and you're tight in two or three cranks. These aren't cocktail blues either — plenty of three and four pounders mixed with the smaller stuff, enough to make you re-tie leaders every third fish.

Striped bass are in that same current but they're not playing the same hours. Surface water out front is sitting in the high 70s, well above where bass want to be, so the good fish are holding low — right on the bottom of that new cut, in eight to twelve feet, and they're only really committing during the last hour of dark and the first gray light before the sun gets on the water. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, weight just heavy enough to hold bottom in that current, is the simple and effective program. I had a client stick a 34-incher off the Cupsogue side at 5:15 a.m. Thursday, right as the tide was going still — that dead-slack window before the flood is when the bigger fish seem to commit, probably because the current stops shoving bait through and lets them feed instead of just holding position.

Inside the bay, the fluke bite has been the most dependable thing going, and it's holding to the pattern we called — solid fish on the channel edges, not the flats. Work the drop-off along the edge of the channel off Tuthill Point, and the deeper cuts running past Smith Point where the current sweeps bay water out toward the inlet. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, dragged slow with the drift, not ripped — that channel edge bottom is soft enough that an aggressive hop just buries the hook in mud. Keeper fish in the 18 to 20-inch range are mixing with plenty of shorts, so it's not a five-minute limit, but a couple hours of patient drifting will put two or three keepers in the cooler most days right now. Bigger fish seem to want the deeper edge — 14 to 18 feet — where the bottom stays cooler under that warm surface layer.

Porgies are stacked tight to structure right at the inlet rocks and around the Cupsogue jetty base, and that bite hasn't slowed a bit. Small hooks, half a sandworm or a piece of clam, right on bottom in that rocky scour. It's not glamorous but it's about as sure a thing as this bay offers in late July, and it's a good way to fill a bucket while you're waiting for the light to drop enough to switch over to bass gear.

Weakfish are around but thin — a few keeper-class fish showing up as bycatch on the bass chunk baits after dark, nothing you'd plan a whole trip around. If that inshore squid push that's been building up around Rhode Island keeps sliding this way over the next couple weeks, I'd expect the weakfish bite to firm up some, but that's a September conversation more than an August one.

Wind's been out of the southwest and stable, which is part of why this pattern has held together as long as it has — no chop pushing sediment through the inlet, no dirty water breaking up that current seam. The tides are easing off the full-moon push toward neap over the next several days, which should slacken the inlet flow just enough that the bluefish blitzes may spread out a bit rather than staying pinned to that one narrow lane. I wouldn't call that a bad thing — it just means covering more water at Cupsogue and along the Westhampton beachfront rather than parking on one spot.

Looking ahead, I expect the bass to stay locked into that dawn-and-dark program as long as this surface warmth holds, and the fluke to keep producing off those same channel edges regardless of what the tide cycle does — that bite's more about structure than moon phase. The one thing worth watching is any afternoon thunderstorm rolling through, which happens fast and often in early August; a hard downpour can dirty the bay side quickly and shut the fluke bite down for a tide or two. If I had one trip to make this week, I'd take the last two hours of the outgoing at the inlet, right at that new cut, working bunker on bottom into first light. That's where the current, the bait, and the bass are all lining up right now.
