# The 6:04 Ebb at the East End: Bass on the Bunker, Blues Riding Shotgun
*Two days off the new moon, the Canal's current is running hard and the bass are stacked tight to the bait — but you've got to hit the window.*

**SANDWICH, MA — August 14** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

6:04 this morning, east end, and the ebb was already ripping at better than four knots off the Railroad Bridge rip. That's the number I've been chasing all week. Two days past the new moon on the 12th, and the Canal is doing exactly what it does on a big spring tide — current stacking hard against the rip line, peanut bunker getting squeezed into that seam where the fast water meets the slower inside edge, and bass sitting right on the transition line waiting for the buffet to come to them. We flagged this a week ago — said the tidal flow building into the new moon would sharpen the bite on both ends, and that's exactly what's playing out right now.

The east end has been the story. From the Bourne Bridge down to the Railroad Bridge, bass are working peanut bunker schools that are getting funneled through on the back half of the ebb — best window's been about two hours before dead low through the first twenty minutes of the turn, when the current's still got some push but the bait starts bunching up instead of getting blown straight through. I've had good luck throwing 3-ounce Deadly Dicks and white/chartreuse Hogy epoxy jigs into the boils where you can see bunker getting nervous, letting it sink on a controlled drift with the current instead of fighting it, then working it back with the flow. Bluefish are mixed right into the same schools — some of them snappers, but plenty of adult class three and four pounders that'll about take the rod out of your hand on a Ballistic Missile popper worked across the top of the rip at first light.

West end's been a different animal, and that's normal for this time of year. Current seams off Mashnee Island and around Hog Island have been holding bass, but you have to work the stage of the tide more precisely — the bite there wants the last hour of the flood pushing into the top of the tide, when the water stacks up against Wings Neck and pushes bait into that flat calm pocket inside Phinneys Harbor. I had a solid forty minutes there Tuesday evening with schoolies to 28 inches on a soft plastic paddletail, white Al Gag Whip-It Fish rigged on a 3/4-ounce jighead, worked slow along the drop where the sand gives way to the deeper channel edge. Nothing huge, but steady, and enough to keep the rod bent.

Scusset Beach has been more hit or miss than the rip lines proper — the surf guys are getting into fish on the outgoing when bait gets pushed out past the jetty, but you're fishing into open water there and it's less current-structured than working the rip itself. If I only had one spot to send a reader with a rod and no boat, I'd still say the east end rocks below the Railroad Bridge on the last two hours of ebb. That's where thirty years of watching this ditch has taught me the bait gets concentrated hardest.

Honest talk on size: this isn't a slot-limit bonanza. Most of what's coming over the rail is schoolie to mid-20s, with the occasional slot fish mixed in on the harder tides. I haven't heard of anything over 34 inches out of the Canal proper this week, though the blitz activity building from Narragansett up through Buzzards Bay and into Cape Cod Bay tells me there are bigger fish staging somewhere close, probably following those same bunker schools that are pushing bass activity everywhere from Long Island Sound on up. Worth remembering this is peanut bunker season — small bait, and the bass chasing it right now are mostly built for speed, not size, until the bigger fish decide to show up behind them.

Slack water is still killing the bite dead, same as every August. When that current drops off — and on these bigger spring tides it can go from ripping to nearly flat in fifteen minutes — the bass scatter off the seams and go quiet. I've watched guys keep fishing through slack out of stubbornness and it just doesn't produce. Better to walk the bridge, grab a coffee at the bait shop, and be back rigged up when the current starts building again on the other stage.

No albies or bonito confirmed in the Canal yet, and I wouldn't expect them here specifically — this isn't their water even when they show up elsewhere on the Cape. But the water's warm enough and the bait's thick enough that if they do arrive in Buzzards Bay proper over the next few days, like the pattern building from Montauk on up suggests, don't be shocked if some push up toward the west end mouth chasing bait out of Phinneys Harbor.

Looking ahead: the moon's still working for us, tidal flow should stay strong through this week before easing back toward the quarter. If the pattern holds, I'd keep hitting that east end ebb window and working the west end top-of-flood push at Mashnee. Watch the wind — anything hard out of the southwest chops up the rip line and makes it tougher to read the boils, so pick your mornings.
