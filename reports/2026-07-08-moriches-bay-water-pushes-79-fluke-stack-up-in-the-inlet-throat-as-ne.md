# Bay water pushes 79°, fluke stack up in the inlet throat as new moon springs build
*Moriches Bay got too warm for its own good this week — the fluke bailed for the cut, and a new moon flood-sunset window is lining up for bass by the weekend.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — July 8** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay did what it does every July when we get a stretch of light southwest wind and no rain to flush it — it cooked. Great South Bay pushed 78.8°F, and Moriches wasn't far behind on the flats off Tuthill Point and the mid-bay guts. That's warm enough to shut fluke down on the feed, and warm enough that they made the only smart move available to them: they left. Every year I watch this happen and every year guys keep soaking bucktails on the flats wondering where the fish went. They went to the inlet. They went to the channel edges where the current still moves cold water twice a day, and some of them pushed clean out into the ocean where an upwelling event has stacked 71°F water against the beach — eight degrees colder than the bay they just left. That's not a small gradient. That's a wall, and fluke respect it.

This week the moon is doing the heavy lifting. We're running into a new moon with spring tides building toward peak alignment Friday through Monday (7/10-13), and that means bigger swings, stronger current through the inlet throat, and — this is the part I care about — the PM flood stacking right on top of sunset. That western Sound pattern people talk about has its twin here: when the flood tide peaks as the light goes down, bass move up out of the deep cut and feed the drop-off edges hard for that last hour. First real PM window like this since the June full moon. I'd circle Saturday and Sunday evening on the calendar and build the trip around it, not around convenience.

On the fluke: the bite in the bay proper has been mediocre to poor, and I'm not going to sugarcoat it — guys drifting the humps off Pattersquash and the flats inside Cupsogue are working hard for two or three keepers on a good outgoing tide. But move to the inlet itself, work the throat on the last two hours of the ebb when that cold water is ripping out past the jetty rocks, and it's a different story. Ounce-and-a-quarter white bucktails tipped with 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet, dragged slow along the north side of the cut in 12 to 18 feet, have been putting quality fish in the boat — nothing giant, mostly 18 to 22 inches, but steady. If you're fishing the ocean side off Cupsogue or the Westhampton groins, that 71-degree upwelled water is holding fluke tight to the second bar. Work a high-low rig with squid strips or Gulp on the incoming, right as the water starts pushing back over the bar — that's the window, twenty minutes either side of the tide change, before the surf gets confused.

Stripers have been the more honest fish this week — not blowing up, but present and predictable if you fish the structure right. The inlet's storm-cut geometry hasn't changed much since the last reconfiguration; the main channel still hugs the west side coming out, and that's where the current funnels bait past the rock jetty on the ebb. Guys drifting live eels after dark through that funnel, letting them swing with the current instead of retrieving, have been picking off fish in the 24 to 34-inch range. During the day, bucktails and swim shads worked the eastern bar edge on the last of the outgoing have produced schoolies mixed with a few keepers. Surfcasters at Cupsogue are doing better at first light on the outside bar with topwater — Danny plugs and Gibbs poppers — before the sun gets high and pushes the fish off the structure.

Bluefish have shown in spurts, mostly small to mid-size, chopping bait on top around the inlet mouth and along the Smith Point stretch during the last hour of daylight. Nothing sustained enough to call it a blitz, but if you see the birds working, get a metal lure — a Deadly Dick or a Hopkins — in the water fast, because those windows close in ten minutes.

Here's the one I'm watching closest: the pelagic window for bonito and Spanish mackerel closes out around the 11th, and every signal that needed to line up for a first Moriches/Shinnecock report has lined up — the ocean-side clean water, the incoming tide timing, the bait pushed tight to the beach by that upwelling. If it's going to happen on our stretch this year, it happens in the next three days on a clean incoming tide at the inlet mouth or off the eastern jetty at Cupsogue. I wouldn't build a whole trip around it, but if you're already out there working the fluke bite on the incoming, keep a small metal jig or Deadly Dick rigged and tied off, because that's exactly the kind of fish that shows up unannounced and gone in the same trip.

Look-ahead: if the wind stays out of the northeast through the weekend the way it's been sharpening the offshore breaks, expect it to keep pushing that cooler water against the beach here too — good news for the ocean-side fluke and whatever pelagics show up. Inside the bay, I'd write off the deep flats until we get a real blow to flush the heat out, and I'd spend my bay time working inlet-adjacent structure on the tide changes. Best case for this weekend: the new moon flood stacks on sunset Saturday and Sunday, the bass feed hard in that last hour of light, and somebody connects on the first bonito of the year working the incoming at Cupsogue. Worst case, the bonito window shuts without a report and we're back to grinding bucktails through the inlet throat — which, honestly, has been productive enough on its own to justify the trip.
