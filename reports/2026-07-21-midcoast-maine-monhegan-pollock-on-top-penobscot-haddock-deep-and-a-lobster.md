# Monhegan pollock on top, Penobscot haddock deep, and a lobsterman's bycatch tells the real story
*Cool Gulf of Maine water is keeping stripers feeding through the day from Boothbay to Eastern Egg Rock while groundfish hold tight to Penobscot Bay's channels.*

**BOOTHBAY HARBOR, ME — July 21** — _by Rusty Pelletier, Zone Writer_

Had a call Tuesday morning from a fellow I've hauled traps near off Two Bush Island for going on twenty years. Said he pulled a trawl up through a school of stripers thick enough he had to shoo them off his buoy line, right there in forty feet of water at ten in the morning. Now, that's not something you'd have seen much of ten years back — bass working open water in broad daylight, midweek, no birds working, no bait busting the surface. But that's exactly what this Gulf of Maine water is doing to the pattern this July. While the water south of the Cape has pushed the bass into feeding at night and around the edges of light, our water up here hasn't warmed enough to shove them off that daytime bite. It's one of the few advantages we get for running colder than everybody south of us, and right now it's paying off.

The Damariscotta River mouth and the ledges off Ocean Point have both been holding stripers this week, schoolies mostly, up to maybe 28 inches, with a scattering of better fish mixed in on the bigger tides. Soft plastics — 5-inch white or bone Slug-Go rigged weightless, worked slow over the eelgrass edges on the last two hours of the incoming — have outfished bucktails most mornings. If you want to put a bigger fish in the boat, live mackerel under a slip float off Fisherman Island has been the ticket at first light, though you'll need to catch your own bait, which this week hasn't been hard.

Eastern Egg Rock is fishing the way it does every July — puffins overhead, bass underneath. The bass there have been holding tight to the ledge on the northeast corner, feeding on the pollock fry and sand eels that pile up when the tide runs hard. Best window is the last hour of the ebb into the first hour of slack, working eels or a 3/4-ounce bucktail with a white curl-tail trailer down along the rockweed line. It's not a five-fish-a-drift bite, but it's steady, and the fish have run a decent average size — mid-20s to low-30-inch class.

Monhegan is where I'd send anybody looking for a mixed bag worth the run. The pollock have been thick on top around the Washington Ledges and off the back side toward White Head, chasing bait right at the surface on calm mornings — you can see them roll before you even rig up. Diamond jigs, the 4-ounce chrome ones, worked with a fast retrieat and a pause, will connect on pollock to eight or nine pounds. Underneath them, in 90 to 120 feet on the drop-offs south of the island, cod and haddock have been coming up on herring-tipped jigs, though I'll be honest — the haddock bite has been spotty this week, better on the last two hours of the ebb than anywhere else in the tide. You've got to be willing to work the drift and not just anchor and hope.

Penobscot Bay itself has settled into its summer groundfish pattern — cod and a few haddock holding in the deeper channels off Vinalhaven and North Haven, 130 to 170 feet, where the water stays cool enough to keep them comfortable through the warm stretch. Jigging bait-imitation soft plastics or a straight diamond jig tipped with clam has worked better than trolling down there this week. The current in that part of the bay runs hard enough that you want the tide working with you, not against — plan your drift for the last of the ebb or the first hour of the flood and you'll cover the fish without dragging bottom the whole way.

We called this one last week when we said the bunker-driven bluefish pattern would keep spreading up the coast as long as the weather held calm — and sure enough, we're finally seeing a few scattered blues mixing into the mackerel schools off Stonington and around Isle au Haut, though nothing close to a real run yet. Worth keeping a metal jig tied on if you're mackerel fishing off the Deer Isle bridge or the Stonington breakwater, because when those blues show, they show fast and clear out just as quick.

The moon goes first quarter this week, which means we're sliding into a neap tide — softer current, less water moving, and that tends to widen the bite windows rather than shrink them, especially through tighter spots like Eggemoggin Reach and the Deer Isle Thorofare. If you've had trouble timing the rip at those spots on the big tides, this is your week to fish them without fighting three knots of current the whole time. I'd expect the striper pattern to hold steady through the weekend as long as we don't get a stretch of southerly wind pushing warm water up the bay — that's the one thing that could push these fish back into a low-light pattern like everywhere south of us. Barring that, fish the tide, watch for the pollock rolling on top at Monhegan, and don't be surprised if a lobsterman tells you where the bass are before any chart does.
