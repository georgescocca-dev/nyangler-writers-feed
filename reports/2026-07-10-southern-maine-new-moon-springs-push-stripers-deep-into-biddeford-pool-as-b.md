# New moon springs push stripers deep into Biddeford Pool as bluefish crash Higgins Beach
*Big tides and a warming shoreline have the bass feeding on the push while blues chop bait right in the wash — but Prouts Neck cod fishing stays a grind.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — July 10** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Last week the wind sat out of the southwest more days than not, which is exactly what you want along this stretch this time of year — it lays the surf down, keeps the green water tight to the beach, and lets the sand eels and pogies stack up instead of getting scattered by a northeast chop. We had a few gray, drizzly mornings mixed in, but nothing that rolled the water or dirtied it up bad. The shoreline temps have been climbing steady through the low-to-mid 60s, warm enough that the bait is thick and the fish are hunting instead of just passing through.

Now we're swinging into a new moon, and that means spring tides — bigger swings, stronger push, more water moving through the guts and creeks than we've seen since before the last full moon. That's the whole story this week. Biddeford Pool runs on tide more than almost any spot on this coast, and a new moon flood shoves a wall of water and bait through the mouth by the Yacht Club and up into the back channels. Friday through Monday, the high stand is lining up close to sunset, which is the exact scenario that gets stripers feeding hard right at last light instead of sulking off in deeper water. I haven't seen a window like this stack up since the June full moon, and if you can only fish one tide this week, make it the evening flood.

Stripers have been showing well but not everywhere — this isn't a blanket bite, you still have to find them. The Pool has been the most consistent read: fish stacking on the last two hours of the incoming, working the current seam off the rocks below the Bridge, and pushing bait up against the flats on the inside once the tide tops out. Live mackerel and eels fished on a fish-finder rig have been getting bit right at the color change where the clean ocean water meets the darker back-bay water, but the better move for most guys has been swimming a 3-ounce Savage Gear sand eel or a Game On soft-plastic paddletail right through that seam on the drift. Slow and steady beats a fast retrieve — these fish are eating sand eels, not chasing anything frantic.

Higgins Beach has had the more visible show. Bluefish, mostly cocktail-size in the 2-to-4-pound range with a few better ones mixed in, have been blitzing right in the wash on the last of the outgoing and the first hour of the incoming, chopping bait practically at your feet some mornings. Pencil poppers — the old Gibbs wood ones or a Super Strike — worked with a walk-the-dog cadence right through the whitewash have been the ticket, and if the blues are up top busting you don't need to overthink the retrieve, just keep it moving and expect to get bit. Bring wire leader or you'll lose gear all morning. The bass have been mixed in at Higgins too, but they're holding a little deeper than the blues, so if you're getting cut off on wire but not connecting, downsize to a 40-pound fluorocarbon leader and work the outer sandbar where the stripers are sitting.

Prouts Neck has fished the way it usually fishes on a southwest wind — tough but not dead. The ledges off the point have given up a scattered bass or two on bucktails tipped with a strip of pork rind or a curly-tail trailer, worked slow along the boulder fields on the last two hours of the outgoing when the current's still got some push but isn't ripping. Cod fishing out deeper, on the rockier bottom off Cape Elizabeth, has been a grind — you're working for every fish, jigging diamond jigs or clam bellies on a high-low rig in 60 to 90 feet, and most trips are producing a few keepers rather than a pile. The water out there stays cooler longer than the beach water, which the cod like, but it also means the bite windows are shorter and tighter to the tide change. Mackerel have been reliable filler almost everywhere along the rocks — Wells Harbor breakwater, the Cape Elizabeth shoreline, even inside the Pool on the last of the flood — small Norwegian jigs or a mackerel tree worked with a quick snap have been good for fast action and a bait bucket full of fresh strip bait for the bigger stuff.

Flounder have been steady but slow-going in Wells Harbor and the calmer stretches behind Pine Point, working bloodworms or clam strips on a simple two-hook flounder rig, bounced along the mud on the bottom of the tide when the current slacks off. Nobody's filling a cooler with flounder right now, but if you want a change of pace and a fish that'll actually eat dinner, that's the play on a slow-tide morning.

Looking ahead, the spring tide push through the weekend is the thing to build around. If the wind stays out of the southwest like it's supposed to, I'd be at Biddeford Pool for the evening flood Friday into Monday, working that current seam below the bridge as the sun drops — that's where I'd put my money this week. Higgins Beach should keep producing blues on top through most tide stages as long as the bait stays pinned to the beach, so it's a good backup if the Pool's slow or crowded. If a northeast wind sneaks in behind this system, expect the outer ledges at Prouts Neck to cool off fast and the cod bite offshore to tighten up even further — that's when I'd shift effort back inshore and let the rocks do the work instead of chasing open water.
