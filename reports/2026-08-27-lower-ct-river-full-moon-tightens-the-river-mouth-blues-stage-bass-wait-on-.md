# Full Moon Tightens the River Mouth — Blues Stage, Bass Wait on the Dark
*Late-August current at Saybrook Point is doing exactly what fifty years of Augusts have taught this river to do, and the bluefish are the first sign fall is loading up.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — August 27** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The marsh grass on Great Island has that late-August bronze creeping into the green, and the ospreys are already thinning out along Old Lyme — a couple weeks early by the calendar most of us keep in our heads, but right on schedule for the water. That's the tell this time of year. Not a thermometer, not a tide chart. It's the birds leaving before the fish arrive. The river mouth is deep into its summer holding pattern, and the moon building toward full tomorrow night is about to put some teeth back into the current for the first time in weeks.

That's not a small thing on this river. When the moon swells, the tide swings harder through the gut between Saybrook Point and the outer bar, and that current is the whole engine of this fishery in August. It's the thing that stacks bait against the breakwaters, that turns a slack, soupy afternoon into a moving conveyor of silversides and peanut bunker getting squeezed through the funnel at the mouth. We flagged this pattern building last week — said to watch the current tighten as the moon came up on full — and that's exactly what the fleet is seeing set up now heading into the weekend.

Striped bass are still sitting in their predictable late-summer posture: deep, shaded, or nocturnal. Daytime water in the lower river and the flats off Great Island is warm enough that the bigger fish just don't want to work in the sun. The producing program right now is the same one that's worked every August I can remember — fish the last two hours of light into full dark, on the last of the ebb, with the current pulling bait out past the Saybrook Point breakwaters and the old lighthouse rocks. Live eels on a fish-finder rig, or chunked bunker fished on the bottom in eight to fifteen feet along the edge of the channel, are taking slot fish and the occasional bigger one. A few boats working the deeper holes off Essex after dark with white or bone bucktails, swimming them slow along bottom, have picked at fish that won't touch anything in daylight. It's not a banner bite. It's a night-shift bite, and if you're only fishing daylight hours right now you're going to work harder than you need to.

Bluefish are the more honest story this week, and they're the fish worth paying attention to if you're trying to read where the season is headed. Blues have been thick and reliable at the river mouth, working the rips where the outflow meets the Sound current, and that's exactly the kind of staging we called for two weeks back — adult bluefish showing up near the mouth ahead of the real fall push. That's happening now. Cocktail-sized blues in the two-to-four-pound range are blitzing bait on the surface around the outer bar and off Great Island on a strong tide change, and they'll hit almost anything moving fast — Kastmasters, Hopkins spoons, or a simple white bucktail retrieved with speed through the froth. Bring wire or a long fluorocarbon leader; these fish have been cutting mono clean.

White perch are still there for anyone working the upper stretches around Essex and the DEP Marina flats, particularly on the last two hours of the incoming tide when the water's pushed up warm and slightly stained from the tributary creeks. Grass shrimp under a small float, or a sliver of bloodworm on a light jig head, worked slow along the drop-offs near the marina moorings, is still producing perch in the eight-to-ten-inch class, with the occasional better one mixed in. It's a quiet fishery this time of year, but a dependable one, and it's a good option for anyone who wants action without waiting on the tide to swing hard.

Looking at the next several days, the building moon should sharpen everything at the river mouth through the weekend — stronger current, tighter bait concentrations, better tide-change windows for both the bass and the blues. Southwest winds in the eight-to-twelve range should keep the water clean and the mouth fishable without the chop that's made the outer bar sloppy on other weeks this month. The bass pattern isn't going to break wide open yet — this is still the summer holding game, not the real push south, and that shift won't show itself in earnest until nighttime air temperatures start dropping hard, likely not before mid-September. But the bluefish staging near the mouth is worth watching closely over the next two weeks. If that presence builds and adult fish start pushing further up past Saybrook Point toward Essex, that's usually the first real signal the fall run is loading behind it. For now, work the dark hours for bass, hit the rips on the tide change for blues, and keep an eye on those ospreys — when the last of them clear out of Old Lyme, that's usually when this river starts to turn.
