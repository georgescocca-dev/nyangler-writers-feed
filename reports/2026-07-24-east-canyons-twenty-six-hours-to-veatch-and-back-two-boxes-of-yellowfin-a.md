# Twenty-Six Hours to Veatch and Back — Two Boxes of Yellowfin and a Bigeye at 4 A.M.
*The run from Montauk out to the east wall cost us fuel and sleep, but the fish were exactly where the water break said they'd be.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 24** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the dock at Montauk at 9pm Tuesday, and by the time we cleared the Point the swell had already laid down to something reasonable — two to three feet, long period, the kind of sea you can run at eighteen knots without beating the boat or the crew to pieces. Six and a half hours to the east wall at Veatch, another twenty minutes to work the temp break we'd marked the trip before. My mate Danny fell asleep against the transom door somewhere around 2am. I didn't. You don't sleep on the run out, not when you're the one doing the fuel math — 26 hours round trip if everything goes right, closer to 30 if the wind swings west and you're punching into it on the way home. We had 800 gallons aboard and I wanted 150 in reserve when we tied up. That's not paranoia, that's just how you stay in business running this far from a mid-Atlantic port.

The bigeye came at 4:15am, before there was even color in the sky. Ballyhoo bar on a long rigger, planer down about sixty feet, and the rod just buried — no warning bite, no shaking, just weight. Took Danny twenty-five minutes standing up in the chair, and it was a solid 190-pound fish, the kind that makes the whole trip worth the diesel bill by itself. We iced it and kept trolling, and by first light we had the spread reset over the edge where the water color changed from that dirty green inshore water to the clean cobalt blue you want — the wall was sitting right where the satellite break suggested, about four miles east of where we found it two weeks back, which tells you the current's been sliding it northeast all month.

Yellowfin showed up on the troll starting around 6:30, right as the sun cleared the horizon. Green machines and cedar plugs did the work, mixed spread with a couple of naked ballyhoo on the shotgun. We boxed nine yellowfin in about three hours, mostly 40 to 65 pounds, working a stretch of water holding right around 76 to 78 degrees on the surface — that temperature range has been the sweet spot all month, and it held true again out here at Veatch same as it's been doing at the closer canyons. We marked bait balls on the sounder holding tight to the thermocline around 180 feet, and every time we ran back through that same piece of structure we picked up another fish or two. Mahi showed on a weed line we crossed mid-morning — five fish, 8 to 15 pounds, came off a mat of sargassum holding a few triggerfish and small baitfish underneath it. Nothing огромный, but a nice bonus for the fish box.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the yellowfin bite would keep building on stable high pressure and warm water pushing into the canyon edges, and that's exactly what played out. The high's held for going on ten days now, and it's given the whole system out here time to set up — clean water, steady breaks, bait staying put instead of getting scattered by a frontal passage. That kind of stability is worth more than any single tactic. You can have the right lures and still get skunked if the water's a washing machine.

Bluefin overlap has been real but modest — we picked up two on the troll before daylight, both right in that recreational slot size, nothing that made us think about the trophy-class fish that got shut down after July 3rd. That fishery's grinding along the way it should this time of year — steady, not spectacular, and the slot restriction means you're not gambling the trip on one big fish anymore. Fine by me. I'd rather bring home six boxed tuna than one fish that puts you over on size and costs you the trip.

No white marlin came tight for us this run, though we did have one show behind a teaser for about ten seconds before it lost interest — a reminder that they're out there but not exactly committed yet. No wahoo either, though a smoker-class fish did cut off a green machine clean at the leader around 8am, which is as close as we got to confirming they're moving through. That's the kind of loss that sticks with you longer than it should.

The run home was the long part. Wind came around out of the southwest in the afternoon, nothing dangerous, but enough to slow us to fifteen knots and add ninety minutes to the trip. We tied up at Montauk with about 140 gallons left in the tanks, which is cutting it about as close as I like to cut it. That's the honest math on this run — Veatch and Atlantis both take a full tank and a full night's commitment, and if you're not comfortable running six-plus hours each way in the dark, you're better off working the closer pieces off Block Island canyon or waiting for a flatter window.

Looking at the week ahead, the high pressure shows no sign of breaking, which means I'd expect the yellowfin bite to hold steady at Veatch and push similarly at Atlantis and Hydrographer where the same warm water's sitting on the edge. If you're planning a trip out of Montauk, Block, or one of the Rhode Island ports, I'd watch the wind forecast closer than anything else — a calm run out buys you fuel margin for working the grounds longer, and that margin is what turns a mediocre trip into a two-bigeye morning. Squid signs building around Rhode Island and Nantucket Sound suggest the bait situation inshore is improving too, which down the line should help pull more bluefin up onto the shallower structure. For now, my money's on another solid week at the east wall if the weather cooperates — but I'll believe it when the fuel gauge says I can afford to find out.
