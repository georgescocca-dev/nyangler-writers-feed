# New moon springs flood the Hook at sunset as bay fluke push to the channels
*Big tides and warm bay water are moving fish out of Raritan Bay's flats and into the inlets — here's where to find them this week.*

**HIGHLANDS, NJ — July 10** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

We're rolling into a new moon this week, which means spring tides — bigger swings, stronger current, more water moving in and out of the bay than we've seen since the June full moon. That matters more than people think. Last week the bay sat warm and mostly slack on the neaps, water pushing into the upper 70s over Keyport Flats and up into the Navesink, and the fish did what warm, sluggish water always makes them do — they left. Fluke that were happily working the flats in June are sliding out toward the channels and the ocean side where there's actual current and cooler water holding oxygen. The new moon change kicks that process into high gear. Bigger tides mean more exchange, more bait getting flushed out of the back bay, and — this is the part I'm watching closest — a PM flood tide that's now converging with sunset. That's the window. High stand meeting last light on a building spring tide is about as good a striper setup as this zone offers in July, and it hasn't lined up like this since before the last full moon.

So that's the why. Peak alignment on this new moon push is Friday through Monday, July 10-13, with the tide swings growing each day into the weekend. If you've got one evening to fish this week, that's the window — not because I'm promising fish, but because the mechanics are right: moving water, dropping light, bait getting squeezed through the Chapel Hill Channel and out past the Hook.

On the striper side, it's been a scratchy month but not a dead one. Guys working live bunker on fish-finder rigs off Romer Shoal and down along the edge of Flynn's Knoll have been picking at holdover bass in the 26-34 inch range, mostly on the last two hours of the incoming into dusk. Depths matter here — 18 to 25 feet along the Knoll's drop, tighter to bottom than you'd fish in spring. The Rip at the Hook has given up a few better fish to guys drifting live eels after dark, but it's been an every-other-trip proposition, not a sure thing. With this new moon flood-sunset window opening up, I'd be at Romer Shoal or working the edge of the Ambrose Channel with a bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig right as that tide turns to flood and the sun starts dropping. That's where I'm putting my evening this weekend.

Fluke is where the real story is right now, and it's a mixed bag depending on where you fish it. Inside the bay, Keyport Flats and the Highlands flats have gone quiet — too warm, too slow-moving water, and the fluke know it. But push out to the channels and it's a different world. Sandy Hook Channel and the mouth of the bay near Ambrose have been holding good numbers of keeper fluke, 18 to 22 inches, with the occasional bigger fish mixed in — there was a doormat pushing 12 pounds weighed out of the lower harbor a couple weeks back, and that tells you there are still genuinely big fish working through this system on their way to deeper water. Drift 4-inch white or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, 25 to 35 feet of water, timed to the last two hours of the outgoing when the channel current really starts ripping bait out of the bay. That outgoing push is the whole game for fluke right now — the fish are stacking on the current edges waiting for it to deliver food, and if you're not fishing that tide stage you're fishing the wrong two hours.

Bluefish have been a bright spot and an honest one — no exaggeration needed. Bunker schools have been thick around Romer Shoal and pushing into the mouth of the Raritan, and the blues have been all over them, especially on that same outgoing tide when bait gets pinned against the channel edges. Poppers and metal — Ava jigs, diamond jigs in the 2-3 ounce range — worked fast through the peanut bunker schools have been producing non-stop action, 3 to 6 pound blues mostly, with some bigger choppers mixed in early morning. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod this week, this is it.

Weakfish, I have to be honest with you — they're just not here. Water's warm enough on paper, but that threshold alone hasn't meant anything the last few seasons, and this year's no different. I haven't heard of a single weakfish report out of the Navesink or the Shrewsbury in weeks. I wouldn't burn a tide targeting them right now.

Porgies are steady and unspectacular off Romer Shoal and along the edges of Flynn's Knoll — bloodworms or squid strips on a high-low rig in 15-20 feet will keep a rod bent for kids and beginners, which is worth remembering in July when everybody wants an easy win.

Looking ahead: the new moon spring tides build through the weekend and peak Sunday-Monday, so that PM flood-sunset striper window is going to be at its best then — I'd fish it two nights if I could swing it, because these alignments don't come around often. On the fluke side, keep working the channels and inlets on the outgoing as long as the bay stays warm, because that pattern isn't reversing anytime soon. And keep an eye on the ocean side of the Hook — with warm water pushing up the coast and the first bonito starting to show further east, it's not out of the question we see an early Spanish mackerel or bonito flash through the Hook's rip before the month is out. Nothing confirmed yet, but that's exactly the kind of signal worth watching for.
