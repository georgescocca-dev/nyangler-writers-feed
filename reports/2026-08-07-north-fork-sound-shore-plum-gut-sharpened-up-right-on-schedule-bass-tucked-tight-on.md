# Plum Gut sharpened up right on schedule — bass tucked tight on the seam
*Porgies are steady, the first blackfish are peeking out, and the bass have gone back to feeding after dark.*

**ORIENT, NY — August 7** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

There was a kid down at the Mattituck Inlet dock Tuesday morning, maybe ten years old, working a bucktail rod half his size, hauling in snapper blues one after another off the bulkhead while his father stood there with a coffee doing nothing useful at all. I watched that for a good twenty minutes before I even rigged up. Told the father his kid was going to outfish him by noon. He didn't argue. That's the kind of morning it's been out here — the little stuff snapping fast and easy, the big stuff making you work for every bite.

Water's been sitting warm the length of the beat, mid-to-upper 70s depending where you dip a thermometer, warmer the further east you go toward the Gut than it is up around the inlet. That's backwards from how it usually lays out this time of year, but the wind's had a say in it. We had that southerly blow blow through a few days back, and I said at the time it would push cleaner, cooler water against the warm side and sharpen up the thermal seam out at Plum Gut. That's exactly what happened. Bass have been stacking tight against that line on the last two hours of the ebb, holding right where the cold water and the warm water shake hands. Trolled bunker spoons and wire-line umbrella rigs have been doing the damage for the boats that know how to hold a drift through that current — it's not forgiving water, the Gut, never has been, and if you don't respect what it does to a hull you'll learn the hard way.

Shore side, the bass have gone nocturnal on me, same as they do every August when the days get sticky. Daytime, they're down deep and sulking. After dark, they come up onto the rock structure — Horton Point, the boulder field off Rocky Point, the riprap out toward Orient — and they'll feed hard for a couple hours around the tide change. I've had decent nights on bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, sinker just heavy enough to hold bottom in the current, and on black or dark-green soft plastics swum slow along the rocks. Nothing enormous — most going 26 to 32 inches — but enough of them, and enough consistency, that I'd call it a real bite, not a rumor. Bring a headlamp and patience. The blitz boys chasing bunker schools out in open water during the day are catching bigger fish, but that's a different game than what I fish.

Bluefish are thick wherever the bunker are thick, which right now is most everywhere along the beat. Snapper blues have taken over every cove and marina basin from Mattituck to Orient — fun for the kids, useless for the freezer, but they'll keep a rod bent. The adult blues are out with the bass working the bunker schools, and when those schools get pinned against the shore near Rocky Point you'll see the whole surface come apart for a few minutes. Doesn't last. Get there or don't, that's the blitz game.

Porgy fishing's been the most honest thing going. Sandworms and clam on a high-low rig, fished on the rock piles and the reef structure off Rocky Point and out toward the Gut approach, and you can fill a bucket without much fuss. Scup don't care about the heat the way stripers do — they've been steady all summer and they're steady now. If you want a fish dinner without a lot of drama, that's your play.

First blackfish of the year started showing up on the same structure this week, which always gets my attention. Green crab on a jig head, worked slow around the boulders. Most of what's coming over the rail right now is undersized, and I've been putting them all back without much thought about it — early sign more than a real bite yet. But it tells you the water's doing what it's supposed to do heading into fall, and it's worth remembering where you found them for when the season and the size catch up to each other.

Fluke are out on the deeper structure now, off the flats and onto the channel edges and reef humps where the bottom stays cooler. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the drift, have taken a few decent fish, nothing spectacular but a fair fish or two most trips if you keep moving and keep your bait fresh. Weakfish have been scarce — a few show up mixed in with the porgy catches on the same structure, nothing you can plan a trip around.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward those springs around the 11th through the 13th, and that's going to re-arm the whole zone — stronger current through the Gut, sharper tide changes along the beach, fish pushed to feed harder on a shorter clock. Until then I'd fish precisely rather than long. Pick your structure, pick your tide window, and don't waste daylight hours on the bass — save that energy for after dark and let the porgy and fluke carry the boat during the day. If the wind stays out of the south a while longer, that seam out at the Gut is worth another look before the bigger tides scramble it back into something new.
