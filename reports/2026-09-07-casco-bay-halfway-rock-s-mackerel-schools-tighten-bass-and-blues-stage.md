# Halfway Rock's mackerel schools tighten, bass and blues stage for the new moon push
*Bait is bunching on the ledges from Bailey Island to Two Lights, and the tide arriving September 10 looks like the trigger the bay has been waiting for.*

**PORTLAND, ME — September 7** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

From the rail at Halfway Rock this week, the water told a simple story: mackerel balled tight against the granite on the last of the ebb, gulls working nervous circles overhead, and underneath them, bigger shapes cutting through the bait without committing. That's the pattern across Casco Bay right now — bunker and mackerel schools compressing under the building moonlight, stripers and bluefish shadowing them but not yet in the kind of full-throated blitz mode that marks the real start of the fall run.

We flagged this a few days back — said the full moon of September 6-7 would tighten these bait schools further and that the first legitimate blitz activity was likely within the week. That's exactly what's setting up. The mackerel have gone from scattered pods to dense, nervous schools off Halfway Rock and along the eastern face of Peaks Island, and the predators trailing them are getting more aggressive by the tide. What hasn't happened yet is the full commitment — the surface-erupting, water-boiling blitz that turns a good week into a great one. That trigger, by the read of tide and bait density, is the new moon arriving September 10. Give it another 72 hours before expecting the bay to really open up.

Water temps in the bay are sitting in the low 60s, which is right where you want them for this transition — cool enough to push bait tight to structure, warm enough to keep stripers actively feeding rather than just staging. The mackerel runs that the bay back in May have their fall counterpart happening now, just quieter, more methodical. Diamond jigs — the 1- to 2-ounce size, unadorned or with a bit of red tube — are still the most efficient way to load a bucket at Halfway Rock on the slack tide, when the mackerel bunch up tight against the rock face in 20 to 40 feet of water. Work the jig on a fast, erratic retrieve just off bottom; the mackerel are there in numbers, and where the mackerel go, the bass and blues aren't far behind.

Striper reports out of Harpswell and around Bailey Island have been steady rather than spectacular — fish in the 20- to 30-inch class working the ledges on soft plastics, 4- to 5-inch white or bone-colored swimbaits on 3/4-ounce jig heads, dragged slow along the drop-offs where the current pulls bait off the rocks. The better bite has come on the first two hours of the outgoing tide, when the ledges around Cape Small start shedding bait into the current seams. Slack water has been mostly quiet for bass, though it's produced some of the better mackerel and pollock action for anglers happy to fill a cooler rather than chase trophy fish.

Bluefish are mixed in throughout, and they're not shy — choppers in the 3- to 6-pound range have been busting up mackerel schools off Two Lights and along the outer edge of Peaks Island, especially on the last two hours of the flood when bait gets pinned against the rocks. Bucktails and poppers worked fast on the surface have drawn violent strikes; wire leader is worth the trouble right now given how thick the blues are running through the bait.

Groundfish have been the quieter story of the week, but a dependable one. Cod and a scattering of haddock are still coming off the deeper structure around Halfway Rock and the ledges further out toward the mouth of the bay, fish in the 3- to 8-pound range on diamond jigs tipped with a strip of squid or mackerel belly, worked on the bottom in 60 to 90 feet as the tide turns. This is patient fishing — methodical drifts, marking bait on the sounder, working the same piece of structure two or three times before moving — but it's produced enough fish to be worth the diesel for boats willing to put in the time rather than run-and-gun.

Looking at the next few days, expect the pattern to keep building rather than break wide open just yet. Wind and tide look cooperative for working the outer ledges — Halfway Rock, the water off Bailey Island, the drop-offs toward Cape Small — and the mackerel schools should keep tightening as the moon wanes toward new. If there's only one window to fish this week, target the outgoing tide around the new moon on September 10; that's when bait density, tidal push, and low light are most likely to line up and trigger the kind of surface activity the bay hasn't fully shown yet this fall. Until then, the steady approach — working structure on the tide changes, keeping an eye on diving birds, and not overcommitting to any one spot — is the smarter play than chasing rumors of a blitz that hasn't quite arrived.
