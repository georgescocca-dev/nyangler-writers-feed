# Bay water hits 79 and pushes the fluke right into Jones Inlet's lap
*New moon springs are juicing the current through Sloop Channel this weekend — and the fluke, at least, are cooperating.*

**FREEPORT, NY — July 10** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Great South Bay cooked hard last week. I had a thermometer reading 78.8 off the transom near the Wantagh Bridge on Tuesday, and by Thursday the shallow flats in South Oyster Bay felt like bathwater. That's too warm for fluke to sit comfortable on the flats, and they know it before we do — they slide for cooler water and better oxygen, which means channels, cuts, and the inlet itself. That's exactly what's happened. The bay bite went from soft to nearly dead on the shallow structure, and it's been replaced by a real push of fluke stacking into Sloop Channel and right through Jones Inlet, where the outgoing tide is dragging clean, upwelled ocean water in around 71 degrees. That ten-degree gap between bay and ocean water is doing all the work right now — it's a magnet.

This weekend changes the picture again. We're running into new moon springs, peaking around the 11th through the 13th, and that means bigger tidal swings and harder current through the inlet and the channel mouths. For fluke guys, that's a double-edged thing — more current means better drift speed for bucktails but also means you've got a narrower window before the tide gets too ripping to hold bottom. For bass guys, the new moon spring tide sets up a PM flood that stacks right into sunset, and that's the first evening high-water window we've had line up with dusk since before the June full moon. If you've been waiting for a shot at working bunker schools on a dropping light with a good tide under you, this is the stretch.

Fluke fishing has actually been the story of the last ten days, and it's honest, solid work — not a blowup, but consistent if you put in the drift time. The money water has been the deeper bends of Sloop Channel, 15 to 22 feet, and right in the throat of Jones Inlet itself on the last two hours of the outgoing, where the current funnels bait past the rock piles on both sides. Three-quarter ounce white and chartreuse bucktails tipped with 5-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets have been the ticket, dragged slow along bottom, short hops, not aggressive jigging — these fish are feeding on displaced spearing and bay anchovies getting pushed through with the tide, so a subtle presentation beats a violent one. Guys working squid strip and spearing combos on standard high-low rigs have done just as well when the current's really moving and you need the scent trail more than the action. Keeper fluke have been running 18 to 22 inches with the occasional 4 or 5 pound fish mixed in — nothing like the 7 and 8 pound class fish coming out of the ocean-side structure farther east, but respectable, and worth the trip if you're willing to make three or four drifts through the same stretch instead of running around chasing reports.

Striped bass have been more scattered than I'd like to report. The resident fish are still around — Jones Inlet itself, the rocks off Short Beach, and the pilings under Wantagh Bridge have all given up fish in the 24 to 32 inch range on bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, cast tight and let sit on the bottom during the last hour of the outgoing. But this isn't blitz fishing yet. No bunker schools getting run down on top that I've seen this week, which tells me the bigger fish are either still offshore working the bait pushed up by that NE wind, or they're holding deep in the channel edges waiting on cooler water. With the PM flood-sunset window reopening this weekend for the first time since the full moon, I'd be at the inlet or working the surf off Jones Beach right at last light through the top of the tide. If bass are going to show on top chasing bunker, that's when it happens — dusk, incoming water, calm surface. Bring a bunker spoon or a big topwater plug and be ready to switch to bucktail if you see nervous water but no actual blitz.

Weakfish are the fish everyone keeps asking me about, and I have to be straight with you — the water's plenty warm enough now, well past what should trigger a bite in the bay, but I haven't heard of a real weakfish caught in South Oyster Bay in over two weeks. Warm water alone doesn't do it. There's something else missing — bait density, maybe, or these fish just aren't running the numbers they used to. I'm not writing off a comeback, but I'd stop treating water temp as the green light. If you want a shot, work the deeper holes off Freeport on soft plastics on the bottom during a slow drift, early morning, but don't build a trip around it yet.

Porgies have been steady and unspectacular around the inlet rock piles and the jetty rubble off Short Beach — clam bait on standard rigs, nothing fancy, good for a bucket if you want dinner while you wait out a slow bass tide.

For the week ahead: I'd put my money on the fluke drift through Jones Inlet on the outgoing, working the deepest cuts before the spring tide current gets too strong to hold bottom comfortably — that window is tightest right around midday through early afternoon on the biggest tide days. And I'd have one evening this weekend booked for the inlet or Jones Beach surf right on that PM flood into sunset. If the bass show on the bunker the way the tide and moon suggest they should, that's the night it happens. If they don't, at least you're fishing the best conditions we've had lined up in a month, and that's better odds than most weeks give you.
