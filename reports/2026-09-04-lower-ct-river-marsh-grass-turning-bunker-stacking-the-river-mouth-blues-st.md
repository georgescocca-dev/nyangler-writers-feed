# Marsh Grass Turning, Bunker Stacking the River Mouth — Blues Steady, Bass Still Waiting
*Goldenrod's up on Great Island and the first bunker pods are pushing into the lower river, but the stripers are staged, not committed — yet.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — September 4** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The marsh grass out at Great Island is starting to go from green to that early bronze it gets right before the real turn, and the osprey platforms along the flats have mostly emptied out — the young birds fledged weeks back and the adults are already drifting south. That's the calendar talking, not the thermometer, and this time of year the calendar usually gets there first. Down at the river mouth, the water hasn't caught up to the marsh yet. It's still summer-warm at Saybrook Point and off the breakwater, low-to-mid 70s by feel, and that's exactly why the striper bite has been a staging bite instead of a blitz bite for the better part of two weeks now.

Here's the pattern the fleet's been talking about at the ramp: bunker pods have been thickening up in the lower river and pushing in and out of the mouth on the tide, but the bass haven't organized into that tight, panicked, surface-erupting mode yet. They're under the bait, they're around the bait, but the water's still soft enough that nothing's forcing the issue. We flagged this exact setup last week — said conditions would hold stable with gradual cooling working in the background — and that's precisely what's played out. Nothing dramatic, just the slow tightening you'd expect heading into the first week of September.

What changes that is the moon and the nights. We're building toward full around September 6-7, and that means stronger tidal push through the mouth and around Calves Island and the flats off Great Island — more current means more concentration, and concentration is what turns staged bass into committed bass. Pair that with the first real drop in overnight air temps, which cools the shallow flats and the marsh creeks faster than the main river channel, and you get the setup for the first legitimate blitz activity of the fall. It's not here yet. But the ingredients are on the table in a way they weren't three weeks ago.

While the big bass sort themselves out, the bluefish have carried the zone, and they've been dependable the way blues get dependable in early September — aggressive, close to the bait, and not picky. Snapper blues have been thick in the coves and side channels around Old Lyme and up into the Essex flats, hitting small gold and silver spoons, quarter-ounce Kastmasters, and gold Rebel-style minnow plugs worked fast on light spinning gear. That bite's been best on the last two hours of the incoming, when the young blues push bait up into the shallower water off the marsh edges. Adult blues, the two- and three-pound class, have been working the harder current at the river mouth itself — off the Saybrook breakwater and the outer edge of the DEP Marina channel — chopping bunker chunks and peanut-sized swimming plugs on the outgoing tide, particularly the last of the ebb when bait gets pulled out of the marsh and funneled through the narrower water.

Striped bass, where they've shown, have come on live eels and fresh bunker chunks fished on the bottom near current seams — the edge where the main river channel meets the flats off Great Island, and along the deeper water off Saybrook Point where the outgoing tide sets up a real seam against the incoming Sound push. These aren't blitz fish. They're one-at-a-time fish, mostly schoolie-to-mid-twenties class, taken by anglers willing to sit on the seam through a full tide change rather than run around chasing surface activity that isn't there yet. Anyone who fishes the mouth this week should plan around the last two hours of the ebb and the first hour of the new flood — that's when the current is strongest and the bait gets squeezed hardest.

White perch have quietly been one of the better bets for anyone who wants steady action without waiting on tide windows. The deeper holes up around Essex and the bends below Old Lyme have been giving up decent numbers on nightcrawlers and small chartreuse or white curl-tail jigs, fished slow along the channel drop-offs on light tackle. This is a fish that doesn't care much about the moon or the bunker schools building downriver — it's been a consistent producer through the whole late-summer stretch and should stay that way into fall.

Looking at the next several days, the smart play is patience with the stripers and reliability with the blues. If the nights keep cooling the way early September nights tend to, and the building moon does what building moons do to tidal flow, the bunker schools in the lower river should tighten up enough to trigger the first real blitz activity at the mouth sometime in the next week to ten days — that's the same window the regional bait-buildup pattern has been pointing to all along. Until that flips, work the current seams on the ebb, keep bunker chunks and eels in rotation for bass, lean on the blues for guaranteed bent rods, and watch the marsh. When Great Island finishes its turn to bronze, the river usually isn't far behind.
