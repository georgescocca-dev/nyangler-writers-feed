# Four hours, forty minutes to Veatch — and the bluefin were exactly where the tide said they'd be
*A long run east put us on slot bluefin working sand eels at the edge, with yellowfin mixing in and the season's first marlin flag showing color.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 18** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the dock at 3:40 in the morning, which is about the only way you make Veatch and get home before the fuel gauge starts arguing with you. I run a 105-mile leg out of Montauk to get to the good water on the northeast corner of that canyon, and at a 22-knot cruise that's just under five hours each way — call it nine and a half hours of running to fish maybe six. You do that math before you leave the dock, not after. Every captain out here does. It's not complaining, it's just what the canyons cost you to get here from this end of the island.

Worth it this time. We picked up the first bluefin around 8 AM on the troll — a spreader bar and a naked ballyhoo working the color change where green water butted up against the blue, right along the 50-fathom break on the northeast lobe. Fish went 39 inches, right in the middle of that 27-to-under-47-inch slot that's been governing everybody's day out here since the recreational fishery settled into its rhythm after the trophy closure. Two-fish limit, and we had our second by 10:30 — a 42-incher that came up under a bird-working bait ball, sand eels balled up tight and getting hammered from below. That's been the story all month at Block and out through Atlantis and Veatch both: the bluefin are keyed hard on sand eels and small bait, and they're not running big, but they're running steady. Nobody's telling stories about giants this week. They're telling stories about limits.

Yellowfin mixed in on the same water, which made for a good multi-species day without much extra running around. We picked up three in the 40-to-60-pound class on the troll — same spread, same color change, just a little deeper on the shelf where the water held closer to that 72-to-74-degree window. One came unbuttoned right at the transom, a solid fish that just straightened the hook on a green stick bar, and that's the one I'll be thinking about for a few days. You lose one like that and it sticks with you longer than the ones you put in the box. Night chunking at Atlantis has been the other half of this pattern — butterfish chunks fished on a slow drift over 55 to 70 fathom, small bluefin coming through steady after dark, with the occasional bigeye showing on the sounder deeper in the column. Nobody's icing bigeye numbers yet, but the marks are there on the meter, which tells you the deeper water is starting to hold fish worth staying up for.

We flagged this last week — said the new moon spring tide would push hard current across these shelf breaks, and that's exactly what set up this bite. The current on the edge at Veatch was running noticeably harder than it had been two weeks ago, enough that we had to adjust trolling speed to keep the spread tracking clean, and that current is what's holding the sand eels tight against the break instead of letting them scatter. As we ease toward the first quarter moon on the 21st, that current should soften some — which usually means the bait spreads out a little and you have to work harder to find the concentrated balls, but it also tends to calm the surface enough to fish comfortably longer into the afternoon.

The other thing worth mentioning — we saw our first white marlin flag of the season Wednesday, up near the Atlantis edge working a dredge on light wind and a slick calm morning. Didn't get a bite, the fish came up, looked, and went back down, which is about typical for an early-season marlin — they're scouting before they're committing. But that's the first one anybody on our radio chatter mentioned this year, and it lines up with what we'd expected: the billfish migration building through July, right on schedule. If you want a shot at one, keep a naked ballyhoo rigged and ready behind the dredge, because these early fish are shy and a switch-bait presentation beats a straight troll hookup most days.

Not every trip out there this week was clean. A boat running out of Block Island got into a stretch Tuesday where the wind built out of the southwest into the high teens by early afternoon and the bite went cold for two hours before it turned back on at the evening chunk — that's the kind of afternoon that tests whether you brought enough fuel to wait it out or whether you have to make the call to head in. We had enough in the tanks to sit tight, and it paid off, but I wouldn't plan a trip assuming that's how it goes every time.

Looking ahead, if the current keeps easing through the weekend and the wind stays out of the south under 15 knots, I'd expect the bluefin slot fishery to hold steady at both Veatch and Atlantis, with yellowfin numbers building slightly as that warmer water continues to sit on the edge. Hydrographer and Oceanographer are still a longer reach from these ports — closer to six hours each way from Montauk — but the sand eel pattern that's working the closer grounds should be setting up the same way further east as the season moves through late July. If I had a weather window this weekend, I'd run Veatch again before first light and work that same break where the color changes, with the dredge out for marlin and the chunk bags ready for after dark.
