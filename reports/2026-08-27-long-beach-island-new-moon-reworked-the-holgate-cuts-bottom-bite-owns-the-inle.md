# New Moon Reworked the Holgate Cuts — Bottom Bite Owns the Inlet Right Now
*Croaker, spot, and spike weakfish are cleaning up on bait rigs while fluke scatter across warm flats and the mullet build offshore.*

**SHIP BOTTOM, NJ — August 27** — _by Tommy Nguyen, Zone Writer_

The new moon tides this week did some real work on the bars. Anybody who walked the beach at Holgate or checked the drone footage off the north end knows the cut that's been forming behind the second bar since the last blow has deepened another foot or so with the stronger swings. That's not an accident — bigger tidal range means more water moving through those gaps twice a day, and moving water carves structure. The same thing happened at the Little Egg Inlet edge, where the sandbar off the northern tip has pushed further west, tightening up the channel that dumps bay water past Graveling Point. We flagged this last week — said the inlet bite would sharpen as tide flow strengthened off the new moon — and that's exactly what's playing out along Little Egg and the Beach Haven side of the inlet complex right now.

The bigger story for LBI this week isn't stripers, though. It's the bottom bite. Croaker, spot, and a scattering of spike weakfish are dominating simple high-low rigs baited with bloodworms and minnows, and they're outfishing the gulp-and-bucktail fluke crowd by a wide margin. That tells you something about where the water's at. Back bay and inshore ocean temps are sitting in the mid-to-upper 70s, which is fine for those bottom species but tough on fluke, who don't like to sit tight in warm, thin-oxygen water. Instead of stacking up on structure the way they do in cooler months, the fluke have spread out thin across the flats in Great Bay and behind Beach Haven, which is softening catch rates almost everywhere except the channel edges and the deeper cuts where a cooler bottom layer still holds. If you're set on fluke this week, work the edges of the Intracoastal channel off Ship Bottom in 12 to 18 feet, or drift the deeper water inside Barnegat Inlet on the last two hours of the outgoing — that's where the better grade fish are stacking against current, not out on the open flats.

The inlet itself is the smart play right now, and not just because of the new tidal range. On the outgoing, cooler bay water pushes out and mixes hard with ocean water at the mouth, and that mixing zone is where the activity concentrates. Fish croaker and spot on high-low rigs with squid or bloodworm strips in the deeper holes just inside Barnegat Light, particularly on the last two hours of the ebb when that cold-warm seam is most defined. Weakfish are mixed into that same bite — mostly spikes in the 12 to 15 inch range, but worth soaking a bloodworm on light tackle if you want a change of pace from the croaker parade.

Striped bass are stuck in the pattern you'd expect for this time of year. Warm surface water is pushing daytime activity onto structure and shutting down the aggressive daylight bite almost everywhere from here up through the Sound. This is a summer holding pattern, not the start of the fall run — the real push south doesn't typically show until nighttime temps start dropping hard, which is usually a mid-September story on this stretch of coast. That means your best shot at a keeper bass right now is a night program: work the jetty rocks at Barnegat Light or the deeper holes along the Holgate stretch after dark with a bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig, or swim a darter plug through the current seams off the inlet on the last of the ebb. Daytime bass are mostly holding tight to structure and not feeding heavy, so don't expect a repeat bite in the same spot at noon that you got at 5 a.m.

Bluefish are the one constant that hasn't slowed down. Snapper blues are thick in the back bay creeks around the Mullica and Graveling Point, and they'll hit anything shiny on light spinning gear — small metal, Kastmasters, or a bare bucktail teaser. Cocktail blues in the 2 to 4 pound range are mixing into the fluke and striper catches out front, especially around any bait pod that shows up on structure. If you're marking bait on the meter near the Ship Bottom channel edges, there's a good chance blues are under it even if the fluke aren't biting.

The thing to watch over the next week to ten days is the mullet. Bait schools are building along the beachfront from Beach Haven down toward Holgate, and that's the pattern that typically ignites LBI's first real surf blitzes of early fall. It hasn't fully kicked yet, but the pieces are in place — tightening bait, cooling nights on the horizon, and bass starting to think about staging. Keep an eye on the surf for the season's first false albacore too; late August into early September is the historical window once the bait tightens up along the beach, and albies love working those tighter mullet schools right in the wash.

For the next few days, expect the bottom bite to hold steady in the inlets on the outgoing tide, fluke to stay soft and spread out except on the channel edges, and the night striper program to be the more reliable bass play. Watch the surf daily for mullet activity — once those schools compress, the blitz window opens fast and it doesn't always wait for you to notice.
