# Goldenrod's Blooming, Snappers Are Thick — Stripers Wait for Last Light at the River Mouth
*The alewife run is a memory and the shad are long gone to sea, but the mouth still has a pulse if you fish the edges of the day.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — August 17** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

Goldenrod's coming up along the causeway out to Great Island, and the marsh grass has that deep, tired green it gets in mid-August before it starts turning. That's always been my marker for where we are in the season down here — the alewives ran up through here back in April, the shad and hickories pushed through on their way to the falls in May, and now we're deep into the summer holding pattern that settles over this river every year about this time. No dogwood, no herring push, no river temp climbing out of the 50s. Just warm, slow-moving water and a river that's shifted its whole personality from spring urgency to summer patience.

The Sound's been running warm this month — Kings Point up near 71, New London holding just under 70 — and the river mouth off Saybrook Point and Great Island tracks right along with that, probably a touch warmer given how shallow and sun-baked those flats get by midafternoon. That warmth is exactly why the daytime bite on adult stripers has gone quiet. Big fish don't want to work hard in 78-degree water with the sun straight overhead. They're sliding into the deeper parts of the channel — the hole off Selden Neck, the drop just outside the Ferry Landing in Essex — and holding there through the heat of the day, then pushing back out toward the mouth as the light fails.

That dusk push is where the real action's been. I've had two solid evenings working the last of the outgoing tide off Great Island, throwing a white Bomber Long A along the edge of the flat where it drops into the channel — that seam holds peanut bunker thick as pepper right now, and the bass know it. Same story working the breakwater at Saybrook Point on the tail of the ebb: a half-ounce white bucktail with a chartreuse trailer, dragged slow right along the rocks, has been picking up slot fish in that last hour of shooting light. It's not a blitz. It's two or three fish a trip if you put your time in at the right tide stage, which for me has meant fishing the last two hours of outgoing into the first hour of the new incoming, right around sunset.

What isn't subtle right now is the snapper blue bite. They're absolutely thick around the DEP Marina docks and stacked up off the Saybrook jetty, and any kid with a bobber and a piece of spearing is going to load the cooler. For anybody who wants a little more sport out of it, small Kastmasters or 1/8-ounce Deadly Dicks worked fast on light spinning gear will keep you bent all afternoon. We flagged a couple weeks back that this snapper push was a sign the adult class would keep building through late summer, and that's holding true — I've started seeing a few 3- and 4-pound blues mixed into the dusk striper bite off Great Island, chopping up the same peanut bunker schools. Give it another few weeks and those snappers will be respectable cocktail blues themselves.

White perch have been steady but you have to go find them — the deep hole below Selden Neck and the channel edge off the Essex waterfront, bloodworms or a chunk of nightcrawler on a simple bottom rig, fished right at first light before the boat traffic stirs everything up. Nothing dramatic, just a reliable dozen fish if you're patient and fish it quiet.

And if you've got kids or you just want an easy evening on the water, the blue crabbing in the marshes off Old Lyme — Lord Cove, Whalebone Creek — has been about as good as it gets this month. Chicken necks on a hand line off the DEP Marina dock or out of a canoe up in those back creeks, and you'll fill a bushel basket without much effort. That's the kind of low-stakes fishing this river does better than almost anywhere in the state right now.

Tide-wise, we're building back toward the full moon on the 28th, and that extra push should start moving more water and more bait through the mouth over the next week — I'd expect the dusk striper window to sharpen up a bit as that flow strengthens, since moving current is what's been concentrating fish at the edges of the flats all month. We called this pattern holding steady as long as the tidal flow keeps pushing bait downriver, and that's exactly what's happened — no real change, just the same reliable summer rhythm.

Looking ahead, I don't expect anything to change dramatically through the week. Daytime will stay slow and hot, dusk will keep producing modest but real numbers of slot bass off Great Island and the Saybrook breakwater, and the snapper blues will keep multiplying until some of them graduate into real fish. The bigger shift — when adult stripers start moving back into the river proper in numbers — is still a few weeks off, once that first real cool-down hits in September. Until then, fish the last light, fish the seams, and don't expect the river to hand you anything before sundown.
