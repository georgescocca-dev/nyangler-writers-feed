# Fort Trumbull holding at night, Gold Star pilings paying off at first light
*The Thames has gone nocturnal — daytime bass have shut down, but the tide-change windows are still producing if you know where the current stacks bait.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — July 24** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

There's a spot below Fort Trumbull where the ebb current pinches down against the old ferry landing and creates a seam you can practically see from shore on a calm evening — a slick line running maybe forty feet off the rocks where the moving water meets the eddy behind the point. Twenty years of watching that seam has taught me it's a decent barometer for the whole harbor. Right now, on a summer evening with the tide dropping, that seam is showing bait — small stuff, silversides mostly, getting pushed and balled up — but it's not showing much interest from anything bigger until the light really starts to go. That tells you everything about where we are in the season.

Water's sitting in the high 60s down at New London, 69.4 on the last read I trust, and that's enough to push the stripers into the same pattern we see every July — deep and nocturnal. Daytime fishing in the harbor and up the Thames has gone quiet. I fished the flats off Pleasure Beach midday Tuesday for about two hours and had exactly one follow on a white paddletail, no commitment. That's the story right now during daylight — fish are there, they're just not feeding hard until the sun's off the water.

Where it's actually producing is the classic three windows: dawn, dusk, and full dark, and the tide change matters more than the clock right now. We called this last week — said tide-change windows would keep producing through the stretch of stable weather, and that's exactly what's playing out. The best fish I've heard about and seen myself have come on the last two hours of the outgoing, right as it starts to slack, working the current seams around the Gold Star Bridge pilings. Bass are holding tight to the concrete, sitting in the slower water on the downcurrent side, and they'll eat a bucktail — half-ounce to three-quarter, white or chartreuse — dropped right against the structure and hopped slow along bottom in twenty to twenty-five feet. Had a solid 28-incher come off the third pier from the Groton side Sunday night on exactly that presentation, fished on the last of the ebb around 9:30.

Live eels are still the most consistent bait if you can get them, fished on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in that current, drifted along the deep channel edge on the Thames between the bridge and the sub base pier. Anyone doing the winter holdover program knows this water holds fish year-round if you find the right depth — right now that depth is twenty-plus feet, tucked into the thermocline that's built up as the surface has warmed. During the day those fish drop into that cooler water and sulk. At night they come up on the edges to feed, and that's your window.

Bluefish have been the more reliable daytime story, honestly. There's bait pushed up into the harbor — peanut bunker mixed with the silversides — and blues have been slashing through it off Ocean Beach and around the mouth of the river on the last of the incoming tide. Nothing like the blitzes they're seeing further down the Sound and out toward the Race, but respectable fish, two to four pounds, willing to hit poppers and small metal — half-ounce Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — worked fast across the surface. If you want steady bent-rod action without waiting for dark, that's been the move. I had six blues in forty-five minutes off the beach Wednesday morning, none huge, but good sport on light spinning gear.

Hickory shad have thinned out from where they were a few weeks back — that spring run up through the harbor is basically done for the season, though I've still seen a few scattered fish working bait near the mouth on the last hour of the flood. Porgies are still there if you want to put meat in the cooler — sandworms or clam on high-low rigs, drifting the deeper holes off Fort Trumbull and around the mouth of the harbor in eighteen to thirty feet, still producing steady if unspectacular action. Blackfish season's closed this stretch, so that's off the table until fall.

The moon's working against the tide strength some right now — we're between phases, not a big spring tide push, which means current isn't ripping the way it does around the new and full moons. That's part of why the bite windows are tighter and more tide-dependent than pure structure-dependent right now. When we get back into stronger tidal flow, I'd expect the Fort Trumbull rips and the bridge pilings to fish better across a longer window, not just the last two hours of the ebb.

Looking ahead, I don't see this pattern breaking unless we get a real cool-down or a stretch of wind that mixes the harbor and knocks that thermocline apart. Barring that, I'd keep doing what's working — night tides at the bridge, dawn and dusk at Fort Trumbull, blues on top water when the sun's up. If I only had one shot this week, I'd take the outgoing tide starting an hour before sunset at the Gold Star pilings and fish it through until it's fully dark, working eels or bucktails tight to the structure. That's where the current, the bait, and the fish are all lining up right now.
