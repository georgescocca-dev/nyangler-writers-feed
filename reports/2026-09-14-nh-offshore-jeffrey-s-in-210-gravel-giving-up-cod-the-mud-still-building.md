# Jeffrey's in 210: gravel giving up cod, the mud still building toward haddock
*Mackerel schools thick on the meter this week, bluefin still working the surface — but the giants haven't committed to the Ledge just yet.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — September 14** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Two-ten on the sounder, hard gravel edge on the west side of Jeffrey's Ledge, and that's where the cod have been sitting most mornings this week. Not stacked — you're marking scattered marks, two or three fish tight to bottom, then a stretch of nothing before the next hook of gravel — but enough to keep jigs and clam bait busy if you're willing to move the boat instead of anchoring up and waiting. That's been the story on the Ledge all week: mixed, honest, and worth the run if you manage expectations.

Water's held cool and clean out there, which is normal for mid-September in the Gulf of Maine — this isn't a warm-water-driven fishery like you'll hear about further south, and that's exactly why it stays consistent this time of year instead of swinging on some passing filament of Gulf Stream water. Mackerel have been thick on the meter over the deeper basins, big soft clouds of bait showing from 60 feet down to bottom in places, and that's the single best sign going into the back half of September. Bait holds fish. Where the mackerel sit, the groundfish and the tuna aren't far behind.

We flagged this pattern last week and the week before — said the Ledge would keep concentrating bait and holding the tuna bite steady through September rather than falling off a cliff, and that's exactly what's playing out. The giants haven't fully committed yet, not in the numbers you'll see once October cools things down further and pushes bigger fish onto the drop-offs, but there's been consistent surface activity working the edges of Jeffrey's — birds picking at bait balls, occasional boils, a few hookups on the troll pulling green machines and small tuna spreads through the mackerel schools mid-morning when the wind lays down. Nothing to call a blitz. But it's building the way it should this time of year, and that's the honest read.

On the gravel — the classic west-side edge of Jeffrey's and the harder bottom running up toward Cape Ann and Thatcher Island — cod have been coming on diamond jigs worked with a slow lift-drop, tipped with a strip of squid or a chunk of mackerel belly when the current's slack enough to let the bait sit still on the hook. Fish have been mostly mid-range, nothing enormous, but a few better ones mixed in on the tide changes when the current picks up and pushes bait tight against the structure. If you're jigging, keep the rod tip low and let the jig fall on a controlled drop — most bites out there come on the fall, not the lift, especially with the water this clean.

Haddock have been slower to show, which tracks with where we are in the season — the mud basins, particularly the deeper stretches toward Scantum Basin and the flats off Platts Bank, haven't fully turned on yet. A few boats picked at haddock on the softer bottom drifting clam bait and small jigs tipped with squid, working depths in the 180 to 220 range, but it's patchy right now rather than a sure thing. That'll change as the water cools further into October and the haddock move onto the mud in bigger numbers — it's a pattern that repeats every year out here, and there's no reason to expect this fall to be different.

Pollock have been mixed in with the cod on the gravel, taking the same jigs, usually a little higher off bottom — 20 to 30 feet up if you're marking suspended fish on the sounder. Hake have shown on the deeper mud edges after dark for the boats willing to make an evening run, though that's a smaller slice of the fleet this time of year with daylight shortening fast.

Conditions for the next few days look workable — the wind pattern hasn't been throwing anything nasty at the Ledge, and as long as that holds, the window stays open for both the groundfish drift and the tuna troll. The moon's swinging toward more tide movement in the coming days, which should help push bait tighter against structure and improve the gravel bite specifically — moving water on Jeffrey's almost always beats dead slack for cod. If you can pick a day with a moderate tide and calm enough seas to run the troll spread through the mackerel schools on the way out, that's the play: work the bait balls for tuna in the morning, then settle onto the gravel edge for cod once the sun's higher and the surface activity dies down.

Looking toward next week, expect the pattern to hold rather than shift dramatically — that's consistent with how this zone typically transitions through September, tapering gradually rather than flipping overnight. The real signal to watch is the mud bite building toward true haddock numbers, and whether the bluefin start showing bigger, more committed surface activity as we get closer to the traditional October window. Right now it's a solid, workable week rather than a banner one — worth the run out of Rye Harbor if you're realistic about picking through scattered marks rather than expecting to load the boat.
