# Peanut Bunker Packed Into the Boulders — Stripers Tight to the Rocks, Albies Still No-Shows
*The bait's pinned against the granite from Ram Island to Wamphassuc, the bass know it, and the first albie of the season still hasn't punched a card in Fishers Island Sound.*

**STONINGTON, CT — August 14** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

First light off Ram Island this week and the water over the boulder field was nervous — not boiling, just twitchy, the way it gets when peanut bunker are trying to disappear into rock they can't actually disappear into. I cut the engine forty yards out and poled the rest, the way my grandfather used to say you fish structure you can't afford to meet at speed. Sure enough, tucked into the pockets between the granite heads in six, seven feet of water, there were bass working the edges — not blitzing, just picking, the kind of quiet predation that tells you they've been there a while and aren't going anywhere.

That's been the story all week in the boulder country — Ram Island, Wamphassuc Point, the rock piles off Esker Point, out toward the edge of the Sound by North Dumpling. Peanut bunker and silversides have pushed hard against the structure, and the stripers are working them on the push of the tide, not the middle of it. I've had my best fly action on the last two hours of the incoming, when the current starts stacking bait against the up-current face of the rocks and the bass stop chasing and start ambushing. A 3-inch olive-over-white Clouser, weighted eyes, dead-drifted and then stripped short and fast right off a boulder edge, has been drawing the most committed takes. Light tackle guys are doing the same job with small white swim shads and soft-plastic peanut imitations worked slow over the same six-to-ten-foot water. Nothing enormous — mostly 22 to 28 inches, a scattering of overslot fish mixed in for those willing to grind it out through the slower stretches of tide.

Water's sitting in the upper 60s to low 70s through the central and eastern part of the Sound right now, warm enough that the bass aren't in any hurry, and that's part of why this bite has stayed steady rather than exploding — it's a slow, structure-anchored pattern, not a moving blitz. If you're used to blind-casting open water, this isn't that game. You need to read the rock, find where the current bends around a boulder and drops speed, and put the fly or the plug right in that seam. Miss the seam by six feet and you're fishing empty water in the middle of a productive tide.

Porgy fishing has been the quiet workhorse of the summer and it hasn't let up. Ram Island, the rock structure off Wicopesset, and the flats around North and South Dumpling are stacked with scup right now, and it's about as close to a sure thing as this fishery offers — sandworms or skimmer clam on a simple two-hook bottom rig, 15 to 25 feet of water, and you're bending rods. Black sea bass have been riding shotgun on the same structure, especially out toward the deeper rock closer to the Race, with squid strips producing keeper-class fish in the 30-to-40-foot range. Neither fishery is glamorous, but both have been dependable enough that I've sent more than a few slow-morning striper trips over to the porgy grounds just to save the day.

Fluke have been the tougher sell. Little Narragansett Bay's sand flats are holding some fish, but it's a grind — light bucktails tipped with Gulp in the 4-inch white or pink shad, drifted slow on the last of the outgoing, and you might scratch out two or three keepers over a full tide. I wouldn't build a trip around it right now. If someone in the boat wants fluke, fine, but I'm not planning mornings around that bite the way I am around the rocks.

Now — the question everyone's been asking me at the dock. No, I haven't seen an albie or a bonito yet, and nobody I trust has either, not here, not at Watch Hill Reef, not out toward Wicopesset Passage. We flagged the setup a week back — new moon on the 12th, building current, warm bait-rich water — and that part's held up exactly as expected. The tidal flow through the passages has been sharpening noticeably the last two days as the moon's pull builds, and there's real bait density out along the reef edges. A hammerhead sighting down in Rhode Island this week is the kind of signal that tells you the warm, forage-heavy water those fish like is sitting right off our doorstep. But the albies themselves haven't shown their hand in this corridor yet, and I'm not going to tell you they have.

If I had to bet, I'd put my money on the reef edges at Watch Hill and the rip lines off the east end of Fishers Island being the first place we hear about a hookup, likely within the coming week if this current keeps building the way it has. Until then, I'd keep the fly rod rigged for both jobs — small profile bunker flies for the bass in the boulders, and a bit of epoxy minnow pattern coiled up in the bag in case something with a sickle tail finally shows up on the reef. Worth checking those spots on your way out to the rock piles rather than making a special trip for a fish that hasn't arrived yet.
