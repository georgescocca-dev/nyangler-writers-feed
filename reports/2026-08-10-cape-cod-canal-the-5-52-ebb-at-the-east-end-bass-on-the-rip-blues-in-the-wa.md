# The 5:52 ebb at the east end: bass on the rip, blues in the wash
*Full-moon current cranked the Canal to better than four knots this week — the bite followed the water, not the clock.*

**BUZZARDS BAY, MA — August 10** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

Tuesday morning, 5:52, the ebb let go at the east end and by 6:15 the current meter in my head was reading better than four knots off the Herring Run. That's full-moon water for you — the kind of push that turns a lazy rip into a washing machine and pulls bass up off the bottom to feed instead of just holding station. I had two guys on stripers to 34 inches on that tide, both on 2-ounce Redfins worked right through the seam where the white water folds back on itself below the Sagamore side. That seam is everything right now. When the current's running this hard, the fish aren't spread out grazing — they're stacked tight on the edge of the fast water, using it like a conveyor belt, and if your lure isn't in that six-foot-wide window you're just casting at scenery.

This is peak-summer Canal fishing, and that means low light or nothing. I've said it for thirty years and it hasn't changed: once that water temp climbs through the 60s and pushes toward 70, the day bite mostly shuts off except right on the strongest stages of moving water. Dawn and dusk are carrying the whole show. The 5:52 ebb I mentioned was a dawn tide — that's not an accident, that's the pattern. Guys grinding it out at high noon on a weak tide are wasting gas. Give me the first hour of light on a hard-running ebb or the last hour before dark on a strong flood, every time, all summer.

The east end rip has been the more consistent producer this week, particularly on the last two hours of the ebb when the current really combs bait through the Herring Run rip and down toward the Cribbon Point side. Bluefish have been mixed in thick there — 3 to 6 pounds, blitzing on top hard enough that you can hear it from the bike path. Poppers and small metal — half-ounce Kastmasters — will get bit fast when the blues are up, but if you want the bass underneath, drop a bucktail, half-ounce to an ounce depending on the stage, and let it swing dead in the current before you start working it. That's a lesson that's cost me fish over the years — start jigging too soon and you rip it right out of the strike zone.

West end has been slower to turn on, and that's typical for this time of year. The current seams there set up better on the flood, when water's pushing in past Mashnee Island and stacking against the flats off Hog Island. I had a report of a nice fish, mid-30s, taken right at the top of the flood in that current seam on a swimming plug worked slow — Bomber Long A, bone color, fished just outside the fast water where it eddies. That west end bite tends to lag the east end by a tide or two because the water has to travel the length of the ditch, and this week that lag has been obvious. If the east end's been hot on the ebb, give the west end another hour or two of that same tide stage before you commit.

Fluke have been a bright spot for guys willing to work the deeper cuts along the edges — 4-inch white Gulp on a bucktail, dragged slow through the deeper holes off Mashnee and the Cribbon Point drop, has been picking up keeper-class fish in the 20 to 22 inch range on the slower stages of the tide, right around the last hour before slack when the current eases enough to hold bottom without a war going on.

Worth mentioning — we flagged this a week ago, that the bonito push would run RI breachways and Vineyard rips first, then Block and Montauk behind it. I'm hearing the first real scout reports now working up into the Buzzards Bay side near Wings Neck and Phinneys Harbor, which is exactly the doorstep for that Vineyard corridor pattern to spill into. Nothing inside the Canal itself yet — this ditch runs too much current and too much cold bottom water for bonito to hold — but if that pattern keeps building the way it's supposed to, don't be shocked if we start hearing about fish showing on the outside edges of Buzzards Bay proper within the next week or two.

Looking ahead, the moon's just tipped past full, which means the exaggerated current we've had all week — that four-knot-plus ebb, the hard flood stacking bait at the west end — is going to start easing back toward normal over the next several days. That's not bad news. Slightly less current usually means a slightly longer window to fish instead of one violent hour of peak flow. I'd focus the next week on the transition tides — the hour before max ebb and the hour after — when the current is strong enough to hold fish on the seams but not so brutal that you're fighting your own line the whole drift. Dawn at the east end, dusk at the west end, and don't be surprised if the bluefish crowd the bass out of the shallow water on the hardest pushes. That's the Canal in August — read the clock, read the current, and be standing in the right spot when the water decides to move.
