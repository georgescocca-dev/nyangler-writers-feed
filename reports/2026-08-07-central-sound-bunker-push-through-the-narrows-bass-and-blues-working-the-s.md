# Bunker Push Through the Narrows — Bass and Blues Working the Seams Underneath
*A hard thermal split between Kings Point and New London is squeezing bait through the Central Sound's tightest water, and the predators are riding the edges.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — August 7** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

_AI fishing-intelligence report. It was prepared from 0 supplied evidence records and 17 supplied analysis inputs; it does not claim firsthand fishing._

Before anybody talks about rods bent or fish in the box, look at what the bait's doing, because that's the whole story this week. Peanut bunker have been threading through the Stratford Shoal narrows in tight, nervous pods — the kind that ball up hard, break, reform, ball up again. That's not random behavior. That's bait getting squeezed by current and by something underneath it that doesn't want it to leave. Terns have been working low and fast over those same pods off Middle Ground Light, which tells you the bunker are getting pushed toward the surface, not just cruising through.

The reason the narrows are doing this comes down to the thermal split across the Central Sound right now. Kings Point on the western end is sitting at 74.8 degrees. New London, way out east, is reading 77.4. That's a real gradient for August, and it matters because the Sound physically pinches down through Stratford Shoal and the Middle Ground — the water has to speed up to get through that constriction, and bait riding a temperature edge gets concentrated right there. It's the same mechanism every year: the Sound narrows, the bait piles up, and the fish that have been spread out over open water suddenly have a reason to commit to one stretch of structure.

Striped bass have been answering that call on the Stratford Shoal rip, mostly slot-class fish in the 28 to 35-inch range, holding on the current seams where the rip breaks over the shoal. The pattern that's produced best is working the last two hours of the ebb into the rip, then again as the flood starts pushing back — those transition windows are when the bait gets disorganized and bass stop chasing and start eating. Snag-and-drop with a live bunker on a 4/0 treble has been the go-to when you can find a workable pod, but a white bucktail with a chunk of pork rind or a soft-plastic swimbait worked slow along the bottom edge of the rip has also been drawing strikes when the current's ripping too hard to hold bait naturally.

The Norwalk Islands bass circuit has stayed more of a grind — fish are there, scattered around the reef structure between Sheffield Island and the outer rocks, but it's covering water to find them rather than anchoring up and waiting. Bucktails in the half-ounce to one-ounce range, tipped with a strip of squid, have worked the drop-offs on the last of the outgoing tide. Charles Island off Milford has been the more consistent bluefish address — bunker schools pushing tight against the rockpile have triggered some genuinely violent topwater feeds, blues crashing bait right on the surface in the early morning hours before the wind builds. We flagged this pattern last week — said that wherever bunker concentrated, expect explosive topwater action as long as the schools stayed thick and the weather held stable — and that's exactly what's showing up around Charles Island and along the bunker-holding stretches toward Norwalk.

Fluke fishing has been a more honest, harder-earned bite. Drifts off the mouth of Port Jefferson Harbor and around Middle Ground in 25 to 40 feet of water have produced keepers, but you're working for them — a lot of shorts mixed in, and the bigger fish have wanted a slower drift than the current's been giving on peak tide stages. White and pink five-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, with a squid strip added for scent, have been the better combination than bare bucktail alone. Best window has been the last two hours of the outgoing before the tide goes dead slack — once the current stalls, the bite shuts down fast.

Porgies remain the dependable bread-and-butter option right now, holding tight to structure around the Norwalk Islands reefs and the Charles Island rockpile. High-low rigs with bloodworms or clam strips in 15 to 25 feet of water have kept rods bent steadily, even on the days when the bass and blues have gone quiet. It's not glamorous fishing, but it's honest fishing, and on a slow tide it's the move that keeps the day from being a skunk.

Blackfish are mostly a non-factor at this point in the season with the closure in effect across Sound waters — worth scouting structure now for the fall reopener, but not a target right now. Weakfish have shown up as an occasional bycatch in the deeper holes near Port Jeff at dusk, nothing to plan a trip around yet.

Looking ahead, the thing to watch is whether that thermal gradient holds. If Kings Point stays cooler and New London stays warm, the narrows should keep functioning as a bait funnel, and the bass and blues should stay stacked on the current edges through the coming week. If a blow mixes the water column and flattens that gradient out, the bite could spread thin fast, and you'd be back to hunting instead of intercepting. Given the stable pattern so far, the smart play is fishing the tide transitions at Stratford Shoal and Middle Ground while the setup holds, with a Norwalk Islands or Charles Island backup plan if the rip goes quiet.
