# Keyport's Bunker Blitzes Pull the Blues Tight While Fluke Keep Grinding the Channel Edges
*With a beat-up swell knocking the ocean side of the Hook around, I stayed inside all week and the bay paid me back — mostly.*

**PERTH AMBOY, NJ — July 13** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

I made the call Monday to keep the boat inside the bay for the whole week, and I'd make it again. There's a southeast swell rolling into the Bight — 2 to 3 feet at a ten-second period — and while that's nothing that'll sink you, it turns the Rip at the Hook into a washing machine you don't need to fish through when the bay itself is loaded with bait. So I skipped the ocean side entirely and put my time into Keyport, Flynn's Knoll, and the Navesink, and that's where this report lives.

The story of the week is bunker. Big pods of adult menhaden have been stacked off Keyport Flats and rolling down past Union Beach and Port Monmouth since late June, and by mid-July they're thick enough that you can see them nervous-watering from a quarter mile off — that oily slick, the gulls sitting on top waiting. Where you've got that much bunker balled up in shallow water in the heat of summer, you've got bluefish underneath it, and this week they found it. I watched three separate blitzes off the Keyport side Tuesday and Thursday mornings, right at first light before the wind came up, blues busting bunker on the surface hard enough to throw spray. Poppers and metal — I've been throwing a 1-ounce Kastmaster and a bunker-colored Super Strike popper — will get eaten the second they hit the water when it's happening like that. The trick is timing. Once the sun gets up over the trees and the boat traffic starts, the blitzes pull down and scatter, so if you want the top-water show, you need to be on the water by 5:30, not 7.

Stripers are mixed into those bunker schools but they're playing it smarter than the blues right now. This is mid-July heat — the resident bass aren't chasing bait on the surface in broad daylight, they're sitting under the school working the edges and picking off cripples. If you want them, don't fish the blitz itself, fish the perimeter. I've had better luck free-lining a live bunker on a 5/0 circle hook with just enough weight to keep it down 8 to 10 feet, drifting the edge of the bait school on the last of the incoming tide off Keyport and down toward Ideal Beach. Snag-and-drop works too if you've got the stomach for it — snag one on a treble, let it flutter down wounded, and hang on. The bass we've boated this way have mostly been schoolies and a few solid mid-20-inchers, nothing enormous, but it's honest action and it beats standing on the bulkhead getting skunked.

Fluke are still the most consistent player in the bay right now, and Flynn's Knoll has been the address. The drop where the Knoll spills into the Ambrose and Sandy Hook Channel — 25 to 35 feet — has been holding fluke all week, and the pattern hasn't changed much from June: 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with a 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet, dragged slow along bottom on the drift, working the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide when the current's still got some push but isn't ripping your bucktail sideways. Romer Shoal has been productive too, especially on the western edge where it falls into deeper water — that's been giving up a better grade of fluke, a few pushing 4 and 5 pounds mixed in with the usual 16 to 18-inch keepers. I'll be honest, it's not a five-fish-per-drift kind of bite most days — you're working for two or three keepers over a few hours — but the fish are there and they're eating with confidence when you find the right depth change.

The Navesink has quietly turned into the best-kept secret of the week. Weakfish have been showing up around the Oceanic Bridge and down toward Red Bank on the night tide, and if you've fished this river as long as I have you know that's when they show their teeth — right at dusk into full dark, on the outgoing. Chartreuse bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, worked slow along the bottom near the bridge pilings, have been the ticket. Nothing giant — most weakfish going 16 to 20 inches — but it's a legit bite that a lot of guys overlook once the striper run ends in spring, and it's worth the late-night trip if you're patient with the mosquitoes.

Porgies have been steady filler fishing on Old Orchard Shoal and around Romer Shoal on high-low rigs baited with clam or sandworm, decent-sized fish in the 1 to 2-pound range, good for keeping kids occupied while you wait on the tide to turn for fluke.

Looking ahead, if that southeast swell backs off like it's supposed to by the weekend, I'd take one more crack at the Rip before the bunker schools push farther down the bay — they always do eventually, usually once the water starts creeping past 78 degrees, which we're not far from. Until then, I'm staying inside: dawn blitzes off Keyport for blues, the Knoll and Romer for fluke on the incoming, and the Navesink after dark for weakfish. That's the bay right now — not blowing anybody's mind, but honest, workable fishing if you put your time in the right places at the right tide.
