# Halfway Rock's Ledge Holds Cod and Haddock While Mackerel Draw the Bass Shallow
*A neap tide settling into Casco Bay this week is stretching out the bite windows on the outer ledges — and the mackerel are doing most of the talking.*

**PORTLAND, ME — July 20** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

I was out past Halfway Rock on Tuesday morning, drifting the eighty-foot contour on the northeast face, and watched something I always look for this time of year — a pod of mackerel come up nervous and tight, dimpling the surface for maybe ninety seconds before something pushed them back down. That's the tell. When the mackerel get jumpy like that at first light, there's almost always a bass or two working underneath, and sure enough, on the next drift my rod loaded up on a bucktail I'd tipped with a mackerel strip, fished slow along bottom in about eighteen feet as the ledge falls off toward the deep water south of the Rock.

That's been the story of the week in Casco Bay — mackerel schools setting the table, and the rest of the food chain responding accordingly. The bay's water has stayed on the cooler side compared to what's happening south of Cape Cod, and that matters. Where warm shallow water down south has pushed keeper bass into a strict low-light and after-dark pattern, the Gulf of Maine influence here keeps our fish willing to feed through the middle of the day, provided there's bait around to trigger it. I've had good bass activity at ten in the morning this week off Peaks Island's eastern side and again in early afternoon along the Bailey Island ledges — something you don't see much of once the water down in Massachusetts Bay really warms.

Halfway Rock itself has been steady rather than spectacular for groundfish. Cod are holding on the deeper structure to the south and east of the Rock, in the seventy-five to ninety-foot range, and they're coming on diamond jigs worked with a slow lift-drop right off bottom, or on standard cod rigs baited with clam. Haddock have mixed in on the same drifts, a little shallower, sixty-five to seventy-five feet, and they've wanted a teaser above the jig — a small white or chartreuse fly seems to be drawing more strikes than the jig alone. It's not a fast bite. I'd call it a working bite: two or three keepers of each per drift if you're patient and willing to make several passes, not a bucket-filler. The fish are there, but they're not stacked the way I've seen them in some Augusts past.

The mackerel run itself has been a little inconsistent, which I want to be honest about. Some mornings off the Portland breakwater and around Cushing Island the schools have been thick enough to load a Sabiki rig in seconds. Other mornings this week they've been scattered and hard to locate, moving with the tide rather than holding in one place. My read is that the bay's forage is still shaking itself into its mid-summer pattern, and the mackerel haven't fully settled into predictable holding water yet. When you find them, though, they're doing exactly what they're supposed to — pulling bass up out of the depths and into range of bucktails, umbrella rigs, and live-lined presentations off structure like the Bailey Island ledges and the rocky shoreline running down toward Cape Small.

Bluefish have started showing in modest numbers inside the bay, working the same bait schools as the bass. This tracks with what's been developing along the whole coast this month — bluefish following bunker and mackerel wherever the forage concentrates, and as long as the weather stays settled and doesn't scatter that bait, I'd expect the pattern to hold and maybe build. We don't get the blitzes here that they're seeing farther south, but a few three- and four-pound blues mixed in with the bass on a mackerel school is a welcome variety, and they'll hit the same bucktails and small metal you're throwing for stripers.

Tide-wise, we're sliding into a first-quarter neap on the 21st, and that's worth planning around. Neap tides mean softer current on the outer ledges — Halfway Rock, the Harpswell approaches, the water off Two Lights — which typically widens the productive drift window rather than concentrating it tightly around the peak of the tide. If you've been frustrated trying to time a short window of hard current at the Rock, this is your week to fish it more loosely; the bite should stay workable through a longer stretch of the tide cycle rather than shutting off outside of an hour either side of the top.

Looking ahead, I'd focus effort on the Cape Small ledges and the deeper water off Bailey Island for cod and haddock, working the softer neap current with jig-and-teaser rigs, and I'd keep an eye out at first light around Peaks Island and the breakwater for mackerel activity — when you see that nervous water, get a bucktail or a live mackerel down into it quickly. The bay isn't giving up easy limits right now, but the pattern is sound, and a patient morning on the right ledge is still paying off.
