# August 17: Washington's Early Jump Has Run Its Course, and Now the Rest of the Fleet Caught Up
*Washington fired first this year like it always does, but the same warm pool that's stalling the southern canyons has finally caught up to us too.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON, NY — August 17** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Every August I do the same math. June is when Washington shows its hand — Gulf Stream positioning puts warm water on our edge weeks before the other canyons even think about waking up. July is when that head start turns into a real pattern, bigeye stacking on the temperature breaks while boats to our south are still waiting on their own water to sort itself out. August is the grind month, the one where the early jump either holds or gets erased. This year, by the middle of the month, I'm looking at erased.

Here's the honest version: Washington's not fishing bad, it's fishing average, and average in mid-August after the kind of June and July we had feels like a step backward even if it isn't one. The whole stretch of canyons is caught in the same squeeze right now — a warm pool sitting over the historically productive southern lumps that's pushed the bigeye bite into a real funk. I've talked to enough boats running our grounds this week to know it's not just us. The tournament fleet that worked this stretch a few weeks back struggled to find bigeye in any real numbers, and that tells you something when guys with good electronics and better instincts are coming back with thin fish counts.

What's working is what's always worked when the deep canyon proper goes soft — the 100-fathom line. Boats staying up on that edge instead of pushing into the deep water are outfishing the ones committing to the canyon interior. That's a positioning call, not a mystery. When the water in the canyon proper gets muddled by this kind of warm-pool intrusion, the bait and the predators that follow it tend to hold shallower, right on that shelf-adjacent structure rather than diving into the depths where the thermal layering gets confused. If you're running out this week, don't burn fuel and time pushing past that line hoping something better shows up deeper. It hasn't been there.

Wahoo and mahi are the more honest targets right now, and I mean that as a compliment to both species, not a knock on tuna. The weedlines and temperature breaks closer to the shelf have been holding real fish — wahoo coming on high-speed trolled rigged ballyhoo and deep-diving swimming plugs run at eight to ten knots along any hard color or temp change you can find, mahi stacked under whatever floating structure and weed mats you can spot working the same water. Bright skirted jigs and small poppers worked around the weed clumps have been drawing them up, and once you find one mahi under a mat there are usually five more with it — that part of the pattern hasn't changed all year. White marlin have been a legitimate bycatch on the troll spread out here too, though I wouldn't plan a whole trip around them right now — they've shown up enough on the temp breaks to keep the outriggers interesting, not enough to call it a target species this week.

Bluefin have been more of a rumor than a pattern out here lately — a few incidental fish on the troll, nothing consistent enough to build a trip plan around. If you're set on bigeye, I'd temper expectations hard right now. The deep canyon proper has been a low-percentage play for two weeks running, and burning a full tank to find out it's still soft isn't smart fishing, it's hope dressed up as a plan.

We flagged this coming — said back on the 7th to watch for that cooling signal pushing north and to expect steady conditions unless it did. It did, and here we are, the southern squeeze up on our own edge instead of staying somebody else's problem.

Looking ahead, the tide cycle is building back toward the full moon on the 28th, and that's usually when things get interesting again out here — stronger current means better bait concentration, and better bait concentration is usually what breaks a funk like this one. If the warm pool boundary slides back south in the next week, I'd expect the bigeye pattern to reactivate on the deeper structure fairly quick, Washington style — we tend to feel the good news early same as we feel the bad news early. If it holds where it's sitting, stick with the 100-fathom line, keep working the weedlines for wahoo and mahi, and don't force the deep water just because it's there. That's the call for right now, and I'll adjust it the minute the water tells me something different.
