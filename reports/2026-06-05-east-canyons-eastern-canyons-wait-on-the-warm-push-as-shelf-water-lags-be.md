# Eastern canyons wait on the warm push as shelf water lags behind the calendar
*With no fresh SST imagery since June 3 and the analyst pipeline thin, the early-June bite hinges on whether the 70-fathom edge has crossed into the mid-60s.*

**MONTAUK, NY — June 5** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

The honest opener this morning is that the data is thinner than I'd like. The most recent SST package in the catalog is dated June 3, and there has been no fresh thermal imagery to confirm whether the warm tongue that was probing the Atlantis west wall earlier in the week has consolidated or pulled back east. Buoy-side, the analyst feed is quiet on the offshore stations, which means we're reasoning from the last good look and from what the boats running this week are seeing under their hulls. That last look had shelf water in the upper 50s to low 60s inside the 100-fathom line, with the first real break to mid-60s sitting out along the 500-fathom contour from Veatch east through Hydrographer. That is a normal early-June posture out here — it is not yet a yellowfin posture.

What that means for the eastern canyon beat is simple: this is still a bluefin and early-mahi window, not a wide-open tuna fishery. The fish that respond first to a 62-to-64-degree shelf are mid-range bluefin on the troll and on the jig, working bait pods along the 50-to-70-fathom shelf edge rather than the canyon heads themselves. Veatch and the western edge of Atlantis are the percentage plays from Montauk and Block Island ports because the run is shorter and the bait line tends to set up there first as the warm water pivots in from the southwest. Oceanographer and the far eastern end stay a Rhode Island and south-of-the-Vineyard game until the surface temperatures climb another two or three degrees.

The on-the-water intelligence this week has been consistent with that read. Bluefin in the 50-to-80-inch class have been the dominant catch for boats willing to slow down and work bait marks rather than commit to a full canyon troll spread. Sand eels are still the controlling forage on the shelf, and that is keeping fish deep and keeping the jig bite — flutter jigs and heavy slow-pitch in 40 to 60 fathoms — more productive than the surface program. A handful of early mahi have shown on high-flyers and lobster gear out along the 100-fathom line, which is the first real signal that the warm water is fingering in, but the numbers are not there yet to justify a dedicated mahi run.

The yellowfin reports remain south and west — Hudson, Toms, the Lindenkohl complex — and that geography matches the temperature gradient. Until the 68-degree isotherm crosses the eastern canyon heads, the yellowfin will stay on the mid-Atlantic side of the picture. Bigeye are a night-troll lottery ticket at this stage of the season, worth a pull on the deep edges of Atlantis if you are already committed to an overnight, but not a target. White marlin and wahoo are still two to three weeks out under any reasonable read of the thermal progression.

Looking ahead three to five days, the pivot point is whether we get a sustained southwest flow to push the warm water up over the shelf break. A persistent southwesterly for 48 hours will move the 68-degree line measurably north and east, and that is the trigger I want to see before committing fuel to a full canyon overnight. The new moon falls into the window as well, which historically firms up the chunk and night-swordfish programs once the water cooperates. Without fresh SST in hand I am running the next trip as a bluefin trip with canyon options, not the other way around — meaning gear rigged for jigging and bait on the shelf edge, with a trolling spread ready to deploy if we mark a clean temperature break on the run out.

The eastern canyons are doing exactly what they do in the first week of June — teasing, not delivering. The boats catching fish right now are the ones reading bait and bottom rather than chasing a calendar. When the warm water arrives, it will arrive quickly, and Veatch will be the first to tell us.
