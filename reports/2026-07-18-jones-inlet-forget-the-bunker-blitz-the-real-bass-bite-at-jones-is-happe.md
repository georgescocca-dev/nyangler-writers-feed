# Forget the bunker blitz — the real bass bite at Jones is happening after the sun goes down
*Everybody's chasing bluefish under the diving gulls off the beach; I'm working the rocks at Short Beach at 10 p.m. because that's where the stripers actually are.*

**WANTAGH, NY — July 18** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

You want to know where every rod in a five-mile stretch of Jones Beach is pointed right now? At the bunker schools getting hammered by blues in broad daylight. And look, I get it — it's a hell of a show. But if you're only fishing that window, you're leaving the better fish in the water. I've been working the jetty rocks at Short Beach and the inlet edge after dark all week, and that's where the real striper bite is living right now.

Here's the why, and it's not complicated. The ocean off the beach is sitting around 73-74 degrees, and that's simply too warm for bass to want to feed hard in the sun. They're not gone — they didn't leave the zone — they just flipped the switch to nighttime feeding, same as they do every summer once the surface water gets into the low 70s. So while everybody's casting metal into the bluefish blitz at noon, I'm soaking eels off the rocks at Short Beach two hours either side of the tide change, after 9 p.m., and putting bass in the 20 to 30 pound class on the sand at my feet. Live eel, no weight if I can get away with it, just enough current to swing it into the current seam off the jetty tip. If the tide's really moving I'll add a small egg sinker ahead of a swivel. Bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig work too if eels are hard to come by at the shop — fresh cut, not the frozen stuff, makes a real difference right now.

Daytime, the blues are legitimately good and I won't tell you to skip them. Bunker pods are pushing tight to the beach from Short Beach on down toward the inlet, and when the birds pile up you'll see blues busting bait right in the wash. Bucktails, Kastmasters, anything silver and fast — cast into the froth and hang on. Just don't expect bass mixed in during those hours. They're down deep or tucked into the shadow line under the jetties, waiting for dark.

Inside the inlet, fluke have been the one honest, dependable bite all week regardless of what the surface temp is doing. Sloop Channel and the deeper cuts along the Jones Inlet structure are holding keeper fish in that 18 to 22 inch range, with a few pushing 4 pounds. I've been drifting the last two hours of the ebb, working white bucktails tipped with 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets right on bottom in 15 to 22 feet, slow drags with a pause — that pause is when they eat. The current edges where the channel spills out toward the ocean are holding the better grade of fish. Don't waste time on the flats mid-day when the sun's beating down; the fluke tuck into that deeper, cooler water and that's where you need to put your bait.

Now the weakfish — I said last week the calm, warm evenings would bring that bite back to life, and that's exactly what's happening in South Oyster Bay. Guys working the deeper holes off Freeport and around the Wantagh Bridge channel edge on quiet nights are picking up weakfish in the 18 to 24 inch class on small soft plastics — pink or chartreuse 3-inch shads on light jigheads, worked slow just off bottom. It's not a fast bite, and I wouldn't build a whole trip around it yet, but it's real, and if this stretch of calm weather holds, it should keep building through the end of the month.

We also flagged the new moon tide pushing hard current through the inlet, and that's exactly what set the table for this week's bunker blitzes — that ebb flow flushed bait right out into the surf zone where the blues were waiting on it. That current is easing off now as we come into the first quarter moon on the 21st, which is actually good news for the fluke guys. Softer current means better bucktail presentation and less scope on your line, so I'd expect the inlet fluke bite to sharpen up over the next few days rather than fade.

Looking ahead, if the ocean stays flat and warm like this — and the buoy off the Bight is still reading rough with that leftover 2 to 3 foot swell — I'd keep my daytime energy on the bunker blitz for blues and save my best efforts for the dark hours at the jetties for bass. If we get a stretch of onshore wind that stirs things up and drops the surface temp even a couple degrees, watch for the bass to push back into feeding during last light and first light instead of full dark — that's usually the first sign the pattern's about to shift toward fall timing. Until then, I'm not chasing the crowd on the beach at noon. I'm home sleeping so I've got the energy to fish the rocks when the real fish come out to eat.
