# Forget the Wash — The Real Bite Is Behind the Jetty Rocks
*The ocean's a washing machine right now, so I'm working the inlet current seams — and the weakfish are finally giving me something to talk about.*

**FREEPORT, NY — July 17** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Every guy with waders and a rod tube strapped to his back is out on the open beach right now, flogging the wash at Jones Beach like it's September. I get it — muscle memory, tradition, the whole nine. But I walked out to the water Tuesday morning, looked at that ocean, and turned right back around. Three-plus foot swell stacked up on a four-second period — that's not surf, that's a washing machine. Short, junky, close-together waves like that blow out the sandbars and scatter bait instead of pinning it. You want to soak a bucktail in that soup, be my guest. I'm fishing the rocks.

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear: the ocean side of this whole stretch, from Short Beach out past the Jones Beach water tower, has been miserable most of this week. That swell energy doesn't care about your casting arm. But flip around to the inlet side and it's a different world. Jones Inlet is doing what it always does when the front gets ugly — it's holding bait and fish in the channel and along the jetty rocks because the water's calmer, it's structured, and it's warm. I've been working the outgoing tide off the West End 2 jetty, right where the rocks meet the main channel, and that seam has been steady for stripers in the mid-20s to low 30s. White bucktail, half-ounce to an ounce depending on how hard the tide's ripping, tipped with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet, cast up-current and swung down along the rocks like you're working a river seam. Dusk into dark has been the window — that last two hours of the ebb when the sun's dropping and the current's still got some shoulder to it.

Short Beach has been quietly good too, but you have to work the back side, not the front. The sod banks along the bay side, where the marsh creeks dump out on the ebb, are pushing warm water loaded with peanut bunker straight into the inlet. Bass are stacking on those edges to ambush that bait as it gets flushed out. I had a stretch Thursday evening where three bass in a row came off a floating peanut bunker imitation worked just outside the mouth of one of those creeks — nothing giant, 24 to 28 inches, but steady, and that's more than I can say for the open beach guys I talked to at the ramp, most of whom went home with nothing.

Fluke has been a pick, not a slaughter, out in Sloop Channel. I've been drifting the deeper bend in 14 to 18 feet on the tail of the outgoing, dragging a chartreuse bucktail with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet trailer nice and slow along bottom. For every keeper you're throwing back three or four shorts, but the ones that count have been decent — 18 to 22 inches, one five-pounder that made the trip worthwhile. If you're going to fish it, go on the last two hours of the outgoing when that current is still moving enough to keep your bait working but not so hard you're standing on 3 ounces of lead just to hold bottom.

Now here's what's actually got me fired up this week — and I don't say this lightly after years of writing this fish off. Weakfish are showing up in South Oyster Bay again, real ones, not the dink 12-inchers you catch by accident. I picked up two solid weakfish, 19 and 21 inches, working the mud flats off Cedar Creek on bucktails tipped with a squid strip, fished right at last light on the tail of the ebb when that water's calm and the current's easing off the mud bottom. It's not a blitz. I want to be straight with you — it's a handful of fish over a handful of trips, and you need to be precise about tide and light to connect. But weakfish have been ghosts in this bay for the better part of a decade, and seeing two real fish in one evening off Middle Bay mud is the kind of thing that makes an old-timer like me pay attention. If you've got a bay boat and some patience, it's worth a look right now, dusk tide, small bucktail or bare jig tipped with squid or a strip of fluke belly, worked slow right on bottom.

Blues have been mostly a bay and canal story — snapper blues are thick around the Wantagh Bridge pilings and up in the Freeport canals, little guys on tiny Kastmasters and gold spoons, good action if you've got a kid who wants bent rods all afternoon. Bigger cocktail blues have shown intermittently inside the inlet itself on bunker chunks fished on the bottom during the outgoing, but it's spotty — some days you get three, some days none.

Looking ahead: if that ocean swell settles down even a little this weekend, I'd expect some of that beach crowd to start connecting again, especially on bunker schools if they show along the outer bar. But until the period lengthens out and that washing-machine action calms, I'm staying inside — working the jetty rocks and channel edges on the ebb, and keeping an eye on those weakfish. That's where my rods are bending this week, and that's where I'd point you too.
