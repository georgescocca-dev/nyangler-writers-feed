# 7-Degree Wall at 39°32'N Puts Yellowfin on the East Side of the Hudson
*The temp break has parked itself east of the canyon all week, and the boats willing to run the extra ten miles are finding tuna and mahi stacked on the color change.*

**MANASQUAN INLET, NJ — August 14** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Seventy-one point four on the inshore edge of the break, seventy-eight point six about eight miles east of it. That's a 7.2-degree jump in less than ten miles of longitude, and it's sitting almost exactly where I marked it a week ago — roughly 39°32'N, 72°05'W running down through 39°26'N, 72°18'W, which puts it draped right over the east wall of the Hudson and pushing out past the Dip. We flagged this last week — said the warm filament feeding the yellowfin bite would hold in place east of the canyon, and that's exactly what it's done. No retreat, no wobble. If you fished the color change this week, you were in the right spot.

The yellowfin have been sitting tight to that break in 600 to 900 feet of water, working bait pushed up against the thermal edge rather than roaming the whole canyon face. Green machines and cedar plugs in the shotgun and long rigger positions, ballyhoo naked or lightly skirted in blue/white and green/yellow on the shorts, trolled at 6 to 7 knots right along the seam where the blue water climbs over the green. That's not a random pattern — chlorophyll on the inshore side is still murky with upwelled canyon water, and the tuna are using the visual and thermal edge as a highway for bait. Once you find fish, drop the spread and switch to a slow chunk line with butterfish — several boats working the Claw and the water just east of it have done well soaking chunks on circle hooks once the sun gets low and the fish stop showing on top.

Mahi have been riding shotgun on that same break. Nothing giant — mostly schoolies in the 3 to 8 pound range, with the occasional gaffer mixed in — but they're reliably stacked under any floating structure or weed line that's drifted into the warm side of the wall. If you're marking a temp jump and see any junk floating on the surface, slow down and pitch a bucktail or a spoon before you run past it. That's been the difference between a two-fish mahi day and a fifteen-fish mahi day this week.

Bigeye have shown up after dark on the chunk, mostly boats anchored or drifting slow right along the edge in that 900 to 1,100 foot range east of the canyon proper. It's not a wide-open bite — more a handful of quality fish for guys willing to soak butterfish chunks through the night and stay disciplined about scent trail and drift speed — but for mid-August that's a legitimate program, and it's the kind of thing that tends to build as the warm water sits in place longer.

What I haven't seen this week is a confirmed white marlin bite or a wahoo report worth putting in print, and I'm not going to invent one just because it's the season for it. The temp structure is right for both — that kind of clean thermal edge usually pulls a few billfish up shallow to sun themselves on baitfish schools — but nobody's called it in yet, so treat that as a possibility, not a promise. Same with swordfish. If you want to gamble on a daytime drop, the timing is classic mid-August: 1,200 to 1,600 feet along the edge of the canyon, squid strips or a dead squid rigged on an 18/0 circle hook under a light stick, fished on the slower moving side of the thermocline where the bottom temp holds cooler and steadier. Nobody's reported one landed this week, but the water column supports the pattern, and this is the stretch of the season where it usually starts producing.

Tide-wise, we're building current into the new moon, and that's sharpening things up rather than blowing them out — bait's getting concentrated instead of scattered, which tracks with what we expected heading into this window. Wind's been manageable, nothing that's kept the fleet off the edge, and the sea state out past the 100-fathom line has stayed fishable enough to work a full trolling pattern without getting beat up.

Looking ahead, the call is simple: as long as that warm pool holds its ground east of the canyon, the program stays the same — run the extra distance, find the 75-to-79-degree side of the break, and work the color change with a mixed troll-then-chunk approach. If the filament slides west toward the canyon face itself, which happens some years in late August, the fish should come with it and shorten the run for everybody. Watch the color change position day to day rather than trusting last week's numbers blindly — canyon breaks like this can slide five or ten miles in 48 hours if the current underneath shifts. Best case, it holds through the weekend and even sharpens with the building tide. Worst case, it slides east again and you're back to a longer run, but the fish themselves haven't shown any sign of pulling out. For now, if I had one day to fish this week, I'd run straight for 39°30'N, 72°10'W, idle down until I found the temp jump on the meter, and start pulling a spread right on that line before first light burns off.
