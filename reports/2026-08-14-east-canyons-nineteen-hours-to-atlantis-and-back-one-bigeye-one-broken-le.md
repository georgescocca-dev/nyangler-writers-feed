# Nineteen Hours to Atlantis and Back — One Bigeye, One Broken Leader, and a Warm Edge Worth Chasing
*The canyon run out of Montauk cost us daylight and diesel, but the temp break at Atlantis is finally doing what it's supposed to do in mid-August.*

**MONTAUK, NY — August 14** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the dock at 4 a.m. Tuesday with 400 gallons in the tanks and a weather window that was supposed to hold until Thursday. It didn't quite hold — the swell built out of the southeast faster than forecast — but by the time it mattered we already had a fish in the box, so I won't complain about it.

Atlantis is a hundred-plus mile run from Montauk depending on where you jump off the shelf, and at eighteen knots that's the better part of six hours one way before you even wet a line. I run the numbers before every trip like it's church — forty gallons an hour cruising, more once you're trolling with the riggers full, and you don't leave the inlet without knowing exactly what a slow day costs you in diesel. This one cost us close to nine hundred dollars in fuel round trip. The bigeye we boxed at 2 a.m. paid for about half of it. That's the honest math of running this water — you don't do it for the fuel receipt, you do it because some weeks the canyon owes you and this felt like one of those weeks starting to turn.

We worked the east wall of Atlantis after dark, chunking butterfish over a temp break that was holding right around 70 to 72 degrees on the warm side — a clean, defined edge, not a smear, which is the kind of structure that actually holds fish instead of just holding bait. We had two runs on the bigeye rod before midnight, lost the first one on a chafed leader about ten minutes into the fight — my own fault, should've re-tied after the first bluefin scrape earlier in the day — and connected clean on the second. Eighty-some pounds, came up hard and stayed deep most of the fight, which is usually how the bigger ones behave out there. No yellowfin in the chunk slick that night, which surprised me a little given the bait we were marking, but the squid and butterfish balls were thick enough on the sounder that I expect that to change inside the week.

Daytime trolling on the way out and back gave us two yellowfin in the 25 to 35 pound class, both on a green-and-yellow bar pulled off the corner ahead of a naked ballyhoo, fish came from the edge of a whale-and-bird pile working bait about six miles short of the canyon proper. That's been the tell all season out here — find the whales, find the birds sitting low over nervous water, and you're usually within a mile or two of where the tuna are stacked underneath. We marked good bait balls the whole way out this trip, which is more than I can say for a lot of July runs, and it tells me the warm water that's been building east of the mid-Atlantic canyons is finally spreading into our zone the way we thought it would. We said as much last week — that the current pushing that warm pool east showed no sign of breaking down inside 72 hours — and here it is, sitting right on the edge at Atlantis exactly where we expected it.

Veatch has been the harder call this month. It's a longer haul out of the Rhode Island ports — Point Judith or Newport boats are looking at something closer to seven hours each way at a reasonable cruise, which means an overnighter is close to mandatory unless you've got a fast boat and a short attention span for sleep. The reports I'm hearing secondhand from guys running that direction have been mixed — some yellowfin on the troll during daylight, a few white marlin raised but not stuck, nothing that sounds like a sure thing yet. I wouldn't discourage anyone from making that run this week, but I'd go in with realistic expectations and a Plan B chunk bite lined up for after dark in case the trolling stays quiet.

Hydrographer and Oceanographer are still the longer commitment — you're talking a full day's run each way out of the RI ports or an even longer push from Montauk, and at that distance you're burning enough fuel that a slow trip really stings. I haven't been out there myself this week and I'm not going to pretend I know something I don't. What I can tell you is the same warm-water signature that's been building at Atlantis has been drifting east all month, and if that pattern holds, Hydrographer and Oceanographer should start firming up behind it inside the next week or two rather than ahead of it. That's usually how it plays out this time of year — the warm edge moves west to east through the canyon chain, and the bite follows it on a lag.

Wahoo and mahi have been incidental so far this trip — a couple of small mahi around a weed line on the way in, nothing worth planning a whole trip around yet, though that usually changes as August pushes toward September and the blue water settles in for good.

If I had one more trip in me this week, I'd run back to Atlantis rather than push further east. The temp break is defined, the bait is there, and the bigeye showed they're willing after dark. Veatch is worth the gamble if you've got the range and the patience for an overnight, but I'd treat Hydrographer and Oceanographer as next week's play, not this week's. Watch that warm edge — if it keeps sliding east the way it has all month, the whole chain lights up in sequence, and there's no sense burning the extra fuel before the water tells you it's ready.
