# Neap Tide, Fat Porgies: New Haven Breakwalls Turn On While Bass Go Nocturnal
*Slack current on the first-quarter neap put sea bass and porgies stacked tight to the rock, but the stripers want the dark now.*

**NEW HAVEN, CT — July 21** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Tuesday, half-tide out, I dropped a two-hook rig baited with fresh skimmer clam into eighteen feet of water off the outer New Haven breakwall and felt that unmistakable thump-thump-thump before the rod even loaded up. Came tight to a porgy that pinned the scale past two pounds — the kind of fish that makes the net operator on the party boats do a double take. That's been the story all week on the rockpiles: when the current goes soft, the porgies and sea bass stack up tight to structure and forget to be careful.

We're riding into the first-quarter neap right now, and if you fish this beat the way I do — vertical presentations over broken bottom — this is exactly the water you want. Less current means less scope on your line, which means you're actually fishing the rocks instead of dragging bait across sand trying to find them. I worked the inside corner of the New Haven outer wall on the last two hours of the outgoing and never had to go heavier than a 2-ounce bank sinker. Porgies to two and a half pounds, a mixed bag of sea bass mostly in the 14 to 16 inch range with a couple that pushed past 17, all on clam and green crab fished right on bottom. Falkner Island's south side rockpile did the same thing on the incoming Wednesday morning — same depth range, same story, slightly bigger average sea bass. One of my regular dock buddies iced a sea bass out there that had real shoulders on it, not far off the class fish coming out of the eastern Sound structure lately.

Stratford Shoal is fishing well too, though you have to time it right. That current rips through there on anything but the neap, so this window is a gift — anchor up-current of the high spots on the shoal at low light, drop clam or squid strips, and let it sit. I had a triple-header there Thursday with two anglers aboard, porgies both times, nothing huge but steady enough that we never had an empty hook for more than two minutes.

Now for the bad news, or at least the complicated news: the stripers have gone quiet in the daylight. Water's sitting in the low 70s across the western basin — Kings Point read almost 71 the other day — and that's enough to push keeper bass into a strict low-light and after-dark pattern. Charles Island's sandbar, which has been money on the flood all June, is basically dead by 9 a.m. now. I fished it twice this week at first light with a white bucktail and Storm swim shad, and both mornings the bite was over in about forty-five minutes flat once the sun climbed. Got two schoolies and a fish that just cleared 28 inches on the second morning, working the drop-off on the south side of the bar right as the flood started pushing bait over it. If you want bass off that bar right now, you need to be there before the sun, or come back after dark and fish the same structure with a bunker chunk soaking on the bottom. That's the play until the water cools a notch.

Fluke have been the pleasant surprise. They're doing exactly what you'd expect this time of year — sliding off the softer back-bay mud and setting up on hard structure and channel edges where the current keeps things moving. Milford Gulf has been solid on the last of the outgoing, drifting 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails right along that channel drop. Nothing giant, mostly fish in the 16 to 19 inch range, but a steady pick, and I heard of a couple pushing 20 inches coming off the harbor mouth structure near the breakwalls on the same tide stage. We flagged a couple weeks back that back-bay fluke would push toward inlet mouths and structure as the water warmed, and that's precisely what's happening now — if you're still working the mud flats expecting summer numbers, you're behind the fish.

Bluefish have been a non-event in my zone so far, which surprises me a little given how loud the bunker-driven blitzes have been to our west. I haven't seen any real bait pods pushing into New Haven Harbor or along Hammonasset yet, though the warm water and the bunker moving up the coast make me think it's a matter of time, not a matter of if. Keep a wire leader and a Hopkins jig in the boat just in case — if a bunker school wanders into the Sound proper, that bite can turn on fast and ugly.

Looking ahead, I'd keep working the neap window on the rockpiles through the weekend — Falkner Island and the Stratford Shoal high spots especially, since that current relief won't last once we build back toward the full moon tides. For bass, plan your trip around dawn and dusk, not midday, and don't be afraid to fish Charles Island bar well after dark with a chunk bait if you want a crack at something better than a schoolie. Fluke guys should keep grinding the channel edges at Milford Gulf on the moving water rather than anchoring up hoping fish come to you. It's not an easy week, but it's a honest one — the fish are exactly where the water temperature and the tide say they should be, you just have to fish the right window for each species.
