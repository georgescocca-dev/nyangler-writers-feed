# Conimicut goes quiet at noon, loud at midnight — scup stacked, bass gone nocturnal
*Fluke are finally pushing into the bay proper as the new moon tide builds and the bass slide deep to beat the heat.*

**PROVIDENCE, RI — August 8** — _by Vinny Ciervo, Zone Writer_

I was down at the Fields Point pier Tuesday morning watching a guy in a Carhartt jacket — in August, mind you — pull scup after scup off the bottom on a high-low rig, and he says to me, "Vinny, this is the easiest twenty minutes of fishing I've had all summer." That's about right for where the bay's at right now. The scup bite is the one thing you can set a watch to. Everything else takes a little more thought.

Buckeye Brook ran dry on herring weeks ago — that whole spring parade that pulls the first big bass into the upper bay is long over, and the fish that came in on it have scattered into their summer pattern. That means less schoolie chaos around the herring ladders and more of that deep, sulking, nighttime bite that Narragansett Bay gets every August when the surface water goes flat warm and the stripers drop into the holes to sweat it out. I've been marking fish in 25 to 35 feet off Nyatt Point and along the Prudence Island shoreline where the bottom drops fast, and they're not up chasing bait in daylight — they're down there waiting for dark.

If you want those fish, you're fishing the last two hours of daylight into full dark, working the outgoing tide when the current pulls bait off the flats and funnels it past the points. Eels are still the closer for me — a foot-long live eel on a 3-way rig, just enough weight to hold bottom in that current off Conimicut, fished slow and patient. Guys doing well at night are also dragging umbrella rigs slow along the edges of the channel off Prudence, picking up fish in the mid-20s to low 30s, nothing giant, but real bass, not schoolies. Daytime, your best shot is early — first light — before the sun gets up on the water and pushes everything down. After that it's a scup and fluke game until dusk.

And the fluke — that's the story I like this week. They've been pushing harder into the bay proper than they have all summer, showing up in Greenwich Bay and along the flats off Conimicut Point in 15 to 20 feet. Nothing wall-hanger size, mostly 16 to 19 inches with a few keepers mixed in, but there's enough of them now that a slow drift with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a half-ounce bucktail, tipped with a strip of squid, will put fish in the box. Best water's been the last of the incoming into the top of the tide, when there's still current moving bait but the water hasn't gone dead slack. Once it goes slack off Greenwich Bay the bite just shuts off — I've sat through it enough times to know not to fight it, I just pick up and run to the scup grounds instead.

Speaking of which — the scup are thick right now on every rocky ledge in the bay that holds current. Nyatt Point, the rip off Conimicut, the rocks along Rocky Point, the Prudence Island shoreline — anywhere there's structure and moving water, there's scup stacked on it. Squid strips or sandworms on a two-hook bottom rig, small hooks, light enough line that you can feel the tap-tap before they steal your bait. This is the easiest meal in the bay right now and it's basically foolproof — kids, first-timers, guys who don't know a herring from a hardtail can all catch a limit of scup off these ledges this week.

Black sea bass are riding shotgun with the scup on the same structure, a bonus fish more than a target, but I've heard of a few decent ones — 15, 16 inches — coming off the rockier stretches around Prudence on the same rigs. Weakfish are still a ghost story in this bay. I hear the occasional report out of Greenwich Bay on a soft plastic worked slow near dusk, but it's not something I'd plan a trip around — more like something you stumble into.

We flagged this last week — said the building tide into the August 12 new moon would sharpen up the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's starting to happen. The tidal range is stretching out day by day, and you can feel it standing on the rocks at Conimicut — more push, more current, more bait getting moved around on the points. That's good news for the ledge bite and it should tighten up the night bass pattern too, since more current means more bait getting swept past those deep holes where the stripers are holding.

Looking ahead, I'd keep working the same program through the weekend — scup and fluke by day, bass by night — but I'd pay close attention once we get inside 48 hours of the new moon. If that tidal range keeps building the way it's shaping up, the points and rips are going to get more aggressive, and that's usually when the bass push up out of the deep holes to feed a little earlier in the evening instead of waiting for full dark. I wouldn't be shocked to hear the first reports of bluefish showing up mixed into those bait schools either — they've been building steam out toward the Sound, and this bay doesn't usually miss out for long once that starts. Worth keeping a wire leader in the box just in case.
