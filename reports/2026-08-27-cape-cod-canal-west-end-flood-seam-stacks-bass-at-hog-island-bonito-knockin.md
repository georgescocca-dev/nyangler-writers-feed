# West End Flood Seam Stacks Bass at Hog Island; Bonito Knocking at Wings Neck
*Warm surface water has pushed the real bite into the dark hours and the hardest current, with steady bluefish and the first bonito scouts working the Buzzards Bay side.*

**BUZZARDS BAY, MA — August 27** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

The Canal's running close to its top gear this week — call it 4 to 4.5 knots at the peak of the stronger tide stages, and with the moon building toward full over the next few days, that number's only going up. That current speed is the whole story right now. Surface water everywhere from the Sound up through Buzzards Bay is sitting warm, and that's pushed the daytime bite down and into the shade, but the Canal doesn't care what the surface temp says. When that current starts ripping, it stacks bait regardless, and that's why the ditch is still outfishing the open water on both ends.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the night bite would be the most reliable play through the coming tide cycle, especially on the bigger current stages, and that's exactly how it's playing out. East end and west end both, the money windows are running dusk through the first couple hours of dark, and again in that gray light before sunrise. Daytime hours are producing bluefish as the dependable bycatch — cocktail-sized blues chopping up top-water plugs and slicing leaders — while the bass have slid into deeper current and structure until the light drops.

On the east end, off Scusset Beach, the pattern's been the last two hours of the ebb into the top of the slack. That's when the rip off the point organizes and bait gets pinned against the seam instead of getting flushed straight through. Guys working that window after dark have done well on 9-inch Redfins and jointed swimmers fished slow and dead-drifted through the seam, letting the current do the work instead of fighting it. A few schoolie-to-mid-30-inch bass have come off that rip on the last two nights, mixed with bluefish up to 8 pounds that keep grabbing gear meant for stripers.

West end is where the better fish have shown. The flood pushing into Buzzards Bay stacks current seams off Hog Island and along the edge toward Mashnee Island, and that's become the go-to for the bigger bass this week. Bucktails in the 2- to 3-ounce range, white or olive, tipped with a strip of sea worm or a 6-inch white Gulp swimming mullet, worked with a slow lift-and-drop through the seam right as the flood builds, have been drawing the better bites — 30- to 38-inch fish, with a handful pushing over that mark on the stronger tide nights. The key there is working the inside edge of the seam, not the fast center current — the bass are sitting in the slower water tight against the disturbance, not fighting the main flow.

Fluke have had a rough go of it everywhere this summer with the warm, thin water spreading fish out instead of concentrating them, and the Canal's periphery is no exception. The flats around Mashnee and the channel edge off Hog Island are still the better bet — there's a cooler bottom layer holding there that the open flats don't have. Gulp in white or pink on a light bucktail, dragged slow along the channel drop, has picked at keeper fluke in the 18- to 21-inch range, but it's a pick, not a program. Anyone leaning hard on fluke this week should expect a mixed bag rather than a limit.

Bonito haven't shown inside the Canal itself yet, but the signs pointing that way are stacking up fast. Point Judith and Block Island already have fish, and the bait's been tightening along the beaches and rips on the Buzzards Bay side — Wings Neck and Phinneys Harbor are the places to watch first. We said last week to expect this push to move west toward Buzzards Bay and the Vineyard corridor as water temps peaked, and that's precisely the direction it's heading. If the pattern holds, the first Canal-adjacent bonito sightings should come off Wings Neck within the next week to ten days, likely on the same small silversides and bay anchovies the blues have been chopping.

Looking at the next few days, the building moon is going to sharpen everything. Bigger swings mean harder current, and harder current on the Canal means better-defined rip lines on both ends and less guesswork about when the bite turns on. If there's only one window to fish this weekend, make it the west end on the building flood after sunset — Hog Island seam, bucktail on the drop, patience through the slower water inside the disturbance. East end guys should lean on the last two hours of the ebb into slack at Scusset, working the rip with a slow presentation rather than a fast retrieve. Daytime hours will keep producing bluefish as a consolation prize while the real program stays after dark until the nights start cooling off in earnest, which is still a couple weeks out on the calendar.
