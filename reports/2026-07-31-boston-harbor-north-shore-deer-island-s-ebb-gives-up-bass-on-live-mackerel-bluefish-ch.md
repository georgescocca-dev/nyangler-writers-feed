# Deer Island's Ebb Gives Up Bass on Live Mackerel, Bluefish Chop Up the Flats
*With the harbor pushing 74 degrees, I'm fishing structure at first light and last light and letting the bluefish have the middle of the day.*

**BOSTON HARBOR, MA — July 31** — _by Sara Whitfield, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday night sitting at the wheel with a chart light and a cup of coffee: this week I'm staying inside the harbor early and pushing out to Nahant and the Cape Ann ledges when the sun climbs. The reasoning's simple. Surface water in the inner harbor is sitting in the mid-70s, which is above where a striped bass wants to work hard during daylight. That doesn't mean the fish left — it means they've gone vertical, they've gone nocturnal, and they've gone to the coldest, deepest structure they can find within range of the bait. Deer Island Light and the flats off Spectacle Island have both of those things: a hard channel edge, current that rips on the ebb, and enough depth to hold cooler water underneath the warm skin on top.

We flagged this pattern last week — called for steady, unspectacular conditions to hold through the stretch, and that's exactly what's played out. Nothing dramatic, but consistent enough that the guys putting in the pre-dawn hours are eating well.

The program at Deer Island has been live mackerel on a three-way rig, 20 to 30 feet down, fished tight to the edge where the flat drops into the main channel. Start that drift right as the tide turns to outgoing — you want the first hour and a half of ebb, when bait gets swept off the flat and stacked against the drop. I've had schoolie-to-mid-30-inch bass show up consistently in that window, with the better fish coming right at first light before the sun gets up over the Boston skyline. Eels work too if you can't get mackerel — same depth, same drift, slower retrieve.

Flounder are still cooperating on the same flats once the tide goes fully slack — small hooks, sandworms or clam bellies, right on bottom in 15 to 20 feet off the Deer Island side. Nothing to write home about size-wise, but steady enough for a bucket of dinner if you're patient and fishing light.

Bluefish are the other half of this story, and they're the fish benefiting most from this warm surface layer. They don't care that it's 74 degrees — they thrive in it. I've had them chopping bait on top off Winthrop Flats and along the Deer Island shoreline in the middle of the day, which is exactly when the bass go quiet. Poppers and metal — Kastmasters, Deadly Dicks — thrown into visible surface commotion have been producing fast, aggressive strikes. If you see gulls working tight and nervous water, that's your window; it doesn't last long before the school moves.

Out on the North Shore, Nahant's rocky points — Bass Rocks side and the ledges off East Point — are still the better bet for evening bass. I fished the last two hours of light there Thursday on an incoming tide and connected on three fish to 28 inches working a white/chartreuse bucktail along the boulder edges, letting it fall into the pockets between rocks and hopping it slow. Cooler Gulf of Maine water keeps that shoreline more honest through daylight than the harbor, so it's worth the run if you want a shot before dark instead of after.

Up toward Gloucester and Cape Ann, the structure fishing has been steadier than flashy. Halfway Rock and the ledges off Salt Island are holding mackerel in decent numbers on diamond jigs worked 30 to 50 feet down — good bonus action while you're scouting for bass activity on top. Cod and haddock reports off the deeper Cape Ann ledges have been thin this time of year, which tracks — that fishery wants cooler water and more current push than we're getting on this easing tide cycle. I wouldn't make a dedicated trip for groundfish right now; treat it as a bycatch bonus while jigging mackerel.

South toward Plymouth and Duxbury Bay, the pattern mirrors the harbor — warm surface water pushing better bass activity into dawn, dusk, and full dark. Duxbury Bay's flats have given up some solid schoolies on soft plastics worked slow along the channel edges as the tide starts moving, but daytime action has been spotty at best. If you're fishing that water, plan around the tide change, not around convenience.

Tides are easing off the full moon push toward neap over the next several days, which should soften current speed a touch — expect slightly less violent rips at spots like Deer Island and the Nahant ledges, but also less concentrated bait, so don't be surprised if the bite windows shrink a little in length even as they stay just as reliable in timing. Wind's been stable out of the southwest, no fronts on the immediate horizon, so I don't expect a hard reset to this pattern. If I had one day this week, I'd take the early ebb at Deer Island with live mackerel, then run up to Nahant for last light on bucktails. That's where the structure, the current, and the temperature break all line up in my favor right now.
