# New moon springs load up the Race Point rip as stripers and mackerel stack the structure
*Bigger tides and a cooling nudge from the northeast have put bait back on the bars — the bite's real, but you've got to work for it.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — July 10** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

Last week the Bay was still shaking off the full-moon hangover from late June — slack current, warm skinny water, bass scattered instead of stacked. That's typical after a big moon pushes through; the current lanes go soft for a few days and the fish spread out looking for their own comfort zone instead of getting pinned up against structure. We had a few days of light southwest air that let the surface warm more than I like to see in the harbors, and the stripers responded by sliding out to deeper, cooler water off Peaked Hill Bars and staying down instead of showing on top.

That's changing this week. We're rolling into a new moon — peak alignment lands right around July 11-13 — and that means spring tides building fast, bigger swings top to bottom, and a lot more push through Race Point and the Peaked Hill Bars rip. At the same time we've had a few days of wind out of the northeast, which is exactly the kind of pattern that stirs the deeper, colder water up onto the bars on the Outer Cape side. When that happens the sand eels and mackerel get pushed shallow, and the bass follow. I've seen this same setup plenty of times in July — new moon current plus a northeast nudge on the water column, and the rip goes from lazy to loaded inside forty-eight hours. That's the window I'm watching hardest right now.

On the water, the Race Point rip has been the most consistent piece of structure in the Bay this week. Guys drifting live mackerel on wire, or working bucktail-and-tube combinations through the seam where the outgoing current meets the deeper water off the Point, have been finding bass in the twenty-eight to thirty-eight inch range, with a few better fish mixed in early and late in the tide. The best window has been the last two hours of the outgoing into the start of the slack — that's when the bait gets funneled and concentrated instead of just streaming through. Once the tide goes fully slack the bite shuts down hard, and you're better off repositioning than sitting on dead water waiting for it to turn back on.

Peaked Hill Bars has been more of a night and dawn game. The eel bite there has been steady but not stacked — guys putting in the time after dark, drifting live eels along the edge of the bar where it drops into deeper water, have been picking off stripers in that same mid-thirties range, with the occasional bigger fish showing right at first light before the sun gets high. It's not a fill-the-box bite right now. It's a patience bite. You might make four or five drifts before you connect, but when you do, it's usually a fish worth the wait.

Billingsgate has been a mixed bag. On the higher stages of the tide, especially the last of the flood into the top, there've been bass working the edges of the flat where the bait gets pushed up against the shoal — light tackle guys throwing paddletails and soft-plastic jerkbaits have done well sight-fishing those fish in skinny water, but it's weather-dependent. You need calm enough water to see the fish working, and we haven't had that every day this week. When the wind's been up, Billingsgate has been mostly quiet.

Bluefish have shown in patches off Sandy Neck, mostly smaller fish in the three to five pound range but plenty of them when you find the school. Poppers and Deadly Dicks worked fast across the top on the outgoing have drawn some violent strikes, particularly in that hour right after the tide turns when the bait gets pushed out past the bar and the blues pile on it in the open water. It hasn't been a blitz — more like scattered pods that show for twenty minutes and then move — but if you're mobile and willing to run the shoreline looking for diving birds, you can find them.

Cod fishing has held up on the deeper structure off Race Point, sixty to ninety feet, jigging diamond jigs tipped with strips of squid or drifting clam baits over the rockier bottom. It's not the kind of numbers game you get in the fall, but there've been a few nice fish in that eight to twelve pound class coming over the rail for guys willing to put in bottom time between the striper drifts. Fluke have mostly stayed tight to the channel edges in Barnstable Harbor and around the deeper cuts near Sandy Neck as the flats warm up — white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the channel drop, have been the go-to, with keeper-sized fish coming on the last two hours of the outgoing when the current's still got some push to it.

Looking ahead, if the northeast wind holds even a little through the new moon build, I'd put my time on the Race Point rip on the outgoing, especially the couple days around peak springs when the current's strongest and the bait gets concentrated hardest. That's historically been the best combination out here — big tide plus a cool push equals bass stacked tight instead of scattered thin. If the wind swings back southwest and lays down, I'd shift toward Peaked Hill Bars at night and early dawn, since that pattern tends to hold up better in calmer, warmer conditions. Either way, I wouldn't sit on one spot all week and hope — this Bay rewards guys who move with the tide, not against it.
