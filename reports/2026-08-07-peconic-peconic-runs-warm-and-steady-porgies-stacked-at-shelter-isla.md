# Peconic runs warm and steady — porgies stacked at Shelter Island, fluke holding the channels
*The bay's shallow water is pushing past 75, and while that's slowing the bass, it's turned porgies and weakfish into the summer's most reliable bite.*

**SHELTER ISLAND, NY — August 7** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

_AI fishing-intelligence report. It was prepared from 0 supplied evidence records and 17 supplied analysis inputs; it does not claim firsthand fishing._

The Peconic runs its own clock, and right now that clock says summer in full stride — shallow flats pushing well past 75 degrees by afternoon, the kind of warmth that makes porgies fat and lazy and turns fluke into channel-dwellers that won't budge off structure until the tide forces the issue. This is the flip side of the bay's early-spring advantage. Back in April and May, the Peconic warms faster than the Sound and the bass show up first. By August, that same shallow structure that gave us an early start is running hot enough to push the bigger predators toward deeper water and moving current, while porgies, fluke, and weakfish — species that don't mind the heat — take center stage.

Shelter Island's rock piles and reef structure remain the most dependable porgy water in the region, and nothing about that has changed this week. The stretches off Cedar Island, the drop-offs along the north side, and the mixed bottom near Coecles Harbor are holding good numbers of porgies in the 15 to 25 foot range. High-low rigs baited with sandworms or clam strips, fished on the last of the incoming into the top of the outgoing, continue to produce the most consistent action — that brief slack window when the current eases lets the rig sit still long enough for porgies to commit instead of just picking at bait. Size 6 to 8 hooks are outproducing bigger iron; these fish are feeding but not aggressive, and a smaller profile gets more hookups per soak.

Fluke are exactly where we flagged them last week — holding tight in the bay's connecting channels and showing little inclination to wander with the water this warm. Robins Island channel, the cuts off Jessups Neck, and the deeper water off Cedar Point continue to be the most productive drift lines, generally in 20 to 35 feet. White or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on ¾-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along bottom on the outgoing, are still the go-to combination — slow retrieves matter more now than they did in June because these fish aren't chasing hard in the heat. A few keepers over 20 inches have come off deeper bends where current stacks bait against structure, but the overall pattern remains steady rather than explosive: consistent shorts with occasional keepers mixed in, which is honestly about right for early August in water this warm.

Weakfish have been showing in the same shallow coves that hold porgies, particularly around Hashamomuck and the protected water off South Jamesport Beach on the North Fork side. These are day-use, sand-and-shell bottom areas that warm quickly and hold bait, and weakfish will slide into them on the outgoing tide chasing that warmer water pushed out of the marsh creeks. Bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, or soft plastic paddle tails fished slow near bottom, have been drawing strikes in the last two hours of daylight — that low-light window when weakfish get more comfortable working shallow. Nothing overwhelming here, but a legitimate bite for anyone willing to work the dusk tide change.

Bluefish are the wildcard, and where bunker pods have concentrated — Orient Point, the mouth of Plum Gut, and the deeper edges off Greenport — the blitzes have been genuinely violent. Topwater poppers and heavy metal thrown into visible surface commotion have produced fast, aggressive strikes, exactly the kind of feeding pattern that holds as long as the bunker stay thick and the weather stays settled. That's proven true again this week; wherever the bait balls up, the blues show up right behind them.

Striped bass are the species asking the most patience right now. The shallow bay water is simply too warm for consistent daytime action, so the better fish have pulled toward structure and moving current — the deeper channel edges near Sag Harbor, the north side drop-offs at Shelter Island, and the strong tidal push through Plum Gut and Orient Point after dark. Live eels or bunker chunks fished on the bottom during the peak of the outgoing, particularly through the Gut where current runs hardest, have been the most reliable approach. Daytime bass fishing in the open bay has been inconsistent at best — worth a look on bucktails around structure edges, but don't expect the aggressive activity you'd see in cooler water.

Looking ahead, expect this pattern to hold as long as the current heat wave sits over the bay. If a cold front pushes through and knocks temperatures down even a few degrees, look for bass to push back into the shallower flats and for the fluke bite to open up beyond the channels. Until then, I'd spend my time on Shelter Island's porgy grounds during the tide change, work the channels for fluke on the slow drift, and save the Gut for after dark when the current runs hard and the bigger bass are willing to work.
