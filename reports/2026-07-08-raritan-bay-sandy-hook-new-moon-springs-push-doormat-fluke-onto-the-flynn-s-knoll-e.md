# New-moon springs push doormat fluke onto the Flynn's Knoll edges as bunker draw blues into the bay
*A 12-pound-plus fluke crossed the scales up the harbor last week, and the building spring tides say the bite off Sandy Hook is about to get stronger before it gets easier.*

**HIGHLANDS, NJ — July 8** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay was doing that mid-summer thing where it looks flat calm on top and is actually working hard underneath. Wind stayed manageable out of the southwest most days, which kept the surface clean, but the tides were still on the smaller neap side, so current on the edges of Ambrose Channel and around Flynn's Knoll was lazy — enough to move bait, not enough to really stack it. The bay's been holding in that mid-to-upper 70s range for a couple weeks now, which is right on schedule for July, and it's already done the thing it always does: pushed a good chunk of the fluke out of the shallow bay flats and into the channels and inlet structure where the water stays a few degrees cooler and the current keeps things moving.

That's about to change. We're building toward a new moon that lands mid-week, and the spring tides tied to it are going to peak hard Saturday through Monday — bigger swings, stronger current, more water moving through the Narrows, Chapel Hill Channel and the cut at the Rip. That's the kind of tide that reloads the structure. When the flow picks up like that, sand eels and spearing get swept off the Knoll and Romer Shoal, and the fluke that have been sitting deep and lethargic wake up and start feeding hard on the drop and the push. I'd circle Saturday morning and Sunday afternoon on your calendar — that's when the new-moon tide is strongest and the fish should be most willing to chase.

Fluke has been the story of the month, and it's not close. The best fish are coming off the edges of Flynn's Knoll and the drop-off along Romer Shoal in 25 to 35 feet, working the last two hours of the outgoing into dead low. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails are doing the damage, tipped with a strip of squid if the current's really moving — you want that bait staying in the strike zone a beat longer, not just flying through on the drift. Bay anchovies and spearing on a standard fluke rig are still catching fish inside the bay proper off Keyport Flats, but the size has dropped off there — mostly 16 to 18 inchers with the occasional keeper mixed in. The real weight is showing up where the channel meets structure. A doormat pushing 12-plus pounds got weighed up the harbor a few weeks back, and while that's not our water exactly, it tells you the size class is out there right now in this system. I haven't heard of anything that big off the Hook yet this month, but the conditions producing it — warm bay water pushing fluke to hard structure on strong tide — are exactly what's setting up here.

Striped bass in July in the bay are always the tougher conversation, and this year's no different. The bulk of the migratory fish are long gone north, and what's left are holdovers and residents working the cooler pockets. The Rip at Sandy Hook is still the most consistent spot — first light and last light, live bunker or chunked bunker on a fish-finder rig, worked right in the wash where the current collides. A few guys are picking at 28 to 34 inch fish there on the dawn tide change, nothing to write home about but real and consistent if you're willing to be there before the sun. At night, the deeper water in Chapel Hill Channel has given up a few better bass to guys drifting bunker chunks on the bottom, especially on the last of the outgoing when the current slows enough to hold bottom without a ton of weight.

Bluefish have been the more reliable fun this week, and that's thanks to the bunker. Big pods of peanut and adult bunker are stacked up off Keyport and along Great Beds, and the blues have found them. Poppers and single-hook metal — half-ounce to one-ounce depending on how nervous the bait looks — worked through the edges of the bunker schools at first light has been drawing blitzes, some fish pushing 8 to 10 pounds. If you see the bunker balling up and nervous on the surface, that's your window — it doesn't last long before the school scatters and the blues drop back down.

Porgies are doing their steady July thing on the humps around Romer Shoal and Flynn's Knoll — bloodworms or clam on a high-low rig in 20 to 30 feet, no real tide preference, just soak it and wait. Good action for a mixed bag day when the fluke bite is slow.

Weakfish, I'll be honest, are a non-story again. The bay's been warm enough for weeks now to theoretically hold them, but the reports just aren't there — a few scattered fish here and there, nothing like a real push. Warm water alone isn't doing it this year; something else needs to line up, and it hasn't yet.

Looking ahead, the new moon springs peaking this weekend are the whole ballgame. If that stronger current does what it usually does, I'd expect the fluke bite on the Knoll and Romer Shoal edges to sharpen up noticeably by Saturday, and the dawn bass-and-blues combo at the Rip should get a boost too as more water funnels through on the bigger swings. My plan is Flynn's Knoll on the Saturday outgoing for fluke, then over to the Rip Sunday at first light to see what the bigger tide pushed through. If the fluke don't cooperate, the porgy grounds are always the fallback — steady, dependable, and never a bad way to spend a July morning on the bay.
