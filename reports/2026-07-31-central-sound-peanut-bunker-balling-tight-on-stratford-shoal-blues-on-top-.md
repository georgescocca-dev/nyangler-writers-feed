# Peanut Bunker Balling Tight on Stratford Shoal — Blues on Top, Bass Gone Deep and Dark
*The bait's telling the story of a Sound split in two: warm surface water pushing stripers into basements while blues and porgies feast up top.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 31** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spent Tuesday morning off Middle Ground Light with the binoculars up before the rod ever came out of the holder, and what I saw explained the whole week. Peanut bunker — dime to half-dollar size — balled up tight against the rip current, nervous, pushed shallow, with terns working them from above and something bigger working them from below. That something turned out to be bluefish, not bass, and that's the tell right now. When the birds are diving and the water's boiling but you're not marking stripers under it, you know the thermal layer's doing exactly what it's been doing all month.

Kings Point is reading 70.9 degrees, New London's sitting at 69.4, and that gradient across the Sound is real — it's warmer the further west and shallower you go, cooler as you swing east toward the Race. We flagged this pattern last week: striped bass are a low-light player only as long as surface temps hold in the mid-70s, and that's held true to the letter. The bass haven't left, they've just gone to the basement. I'm marking good concentrations of them 30 to 45 feet down on the meter around Stratford Shoal and the deeper drop just north of Charles Island, but they're not interested in anything during daylight hours unless it's dead-drifted right on their nose.

The program that's actually working is the one I've been running for two weeks now: bunker chunks or a whole fresh eel on a fish-finder rig, fished on the bottom during the last two hours of the outgoing tide and into the top of the incoming, right at first light or after dark. I had two fish over 30 inches Thursday night drifting eels off the east side of Stratford Shoal in about 38 feet, current still moving decent even on this softening tide cycle. That's the other piece of this — we're coming off the neap and the current through the deeper channels has been sluggish, which concentrates bait less aggressively than it should this time of year. As the moon builds back toward the August 12 new moon, expect that current to sharpen up again, and when it does, the bass should tighten up on the structure edges instead of spreading out.

Daytime, if you want steady action instead of the grind-it-out night program, the blues are your play. They don't care about the warm water — they love it — and they're stacked on the same bait schools the bass are avoiding. Norwalk Islands has been the most consistent water for this: run the channels between Sheffield and Cockenoe, look for the diving birds, and throw poppers or Hopkins tins into the melee. Fish are running two to five pounds, occasional bigger ones mixed in, and it doesn't take finesse — just get something shiny in front of them on the retrieve and hang on.

Fluke are the honest, dependable piece of this puzzle right now. Not exciting, but real. I've had good drifts off Port Jeff Harbor mouth and along the edge of the Middle Ground flats in 25 to 35 feet, working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid. The keepers are mixing with plenty of shorts — for every legal fish you're probably culling three or four undersized, so it's a numbers game, but the structure-and-drift pattern is holding steady the way it usually does in late July. Slow the drift down as much as you can stand; the water's warm enough that these fish aren't chasing anything fast.

Porgies have been the quiet standout. Anchor up on any rocky patch — the reef structure off the Norwalk Islands, the rockpile edges around Charles Island, even inside Port Jeff Harbor near the breakwater — and small hooks with sandworm or clam belly will load the cooler fast. This is about as easy as Sound fishing gets right now, and it's a good call if you've got kids or guests aboard who just want bent rods.

What's got my attention heading into next week isn't any of that — it's what I haven't seen yet but expect to any day. The bait's been building all month, the water's warm enough, and this is exactly the calendar window where bonito start showing on the western edges of the Sound, usually first as scattered reports around the Race and working their way toward Stratford Shoal by mid-August. I haven't marked any yet, but I'll be watching the bait balls closer than usual this week — when bonito show, they show fast and they show subtle, a different kind of nervous water than blues make. If a cold front pushes through in the next few days and knocks the surface temp down even a degree or two, that could be the trigger. Until then, I'd fish the tide changes hard for bass, hit the birds for blues, and keep one eye on anything that looks like small, fast fish slashing through the peanut bunker instead of just crashing them.
