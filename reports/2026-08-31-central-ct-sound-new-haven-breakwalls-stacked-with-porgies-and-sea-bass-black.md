# New Haven Breakwalls Stacked With Porgies and Sea Bass, Blackfish Starting to Show
*The rockpiles are producing all day long while stripers work the Charles Island bar on the flood and bluefish chase bait everywhere else.*

**NEW HAVEN, CT — August 31** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Three guys working the outer wall at New Haven Harbor Tuesday morning had a five-gallon bucket half full of porgies before nine o'clock, and mixed into that bucket were three sea bass pushing two pounds — the kind that make you check your knot twice before the next drop. That's been the story on the rockpiles all week: steady, dependable, the bread-and-butter bite that Central Sound structure fishing is built on, with the added bonus that blackfish are starting to nose into the mix as water temps ease off their summer peak.

The Sound's holding warm — Kings Point up around 71, New London closer to 69 — and that west-to-east gradient means the New Haven-to-Milford stretch is still fishing like late summer, not like the shoulder season yet. That's good news for porgies and sea bass, which want that stable warm water over structure, and it's part of why the bite on the breakwalls, Charles Island rockpile, and the Falkner Island reef complex has stayed so consistent through the back half of August. Blackfish are a step behind — a few keeper-class tog are coming off the deeper humps around Stratford Shoal on green crabs, but that's an early trickle, not the main event yet. Give it two, three more weeks of cooling water and that fishery turns on in earnest.

Stripers are the more complicated story right now, and honestly, they're behaving exactly the way we said they would last week — this is a summer holding pattern, not the fall run. Fish are stacked on the Charles Island sandbar, especially on the last two hours of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing, when current sweeps bait off the bar edge into deeper water. Live eels and chunked bunker fished on the drop-off have been drawing slot-class bass in the 26 to 34 inch range, with the occasional bigger fish mixed in after dark. But it's not blitz fishing — it's a tide-window bite, and if you show up at the wrong stage you'll wonder what everyone's talking about. The real push, when bunker schools finally concentrate enough to trigger blitz-level feeding, is still likely a couple weeks off, tracking the moon toward full and the first real drop in overnight temps come mid-September.

Bluefish, on the other hand, have been the most dependable predator in the zone, and that's tracking exactly with bunker density like we flagged — wherever the bait pods are thick, from the mouth of the harbor out past Charles Island to the pocket water off Hammonasset, the blues are on them. Casting metal — quarter to half-ounce Kastmasters, or bucktail-tipped diamond jigs — into visible pods has been producing fish in the 3 to 6 pound range, with some better ones mixed in off Falkner Island where the bait's been holding tighter. Poppers worked over the surface pods at first light have been just as effective and a lot more fun on light spinning gear.

Fluke have softened up the way they always do this time of year, and that's the seasonal transition we called for — not a collapse, just fish sliding off the open flats and tucking into structure and deeper channel edges looking for cooler, better-oxygenated water. The Milford Gulf has been the most consistent producer, with anglers working white and chartreuse Gulp paired with 3/4-ounce bucktails slow along the channel drop, picking through shorts to find the occasional keeper in the 18 to 20 inch range. It's fair fishing, not fast, and the window for it is closing as September moves in — worth a trip if you're patient, not worth planning a whole day around.

For the porgy and sea bass crowd, the program hasn't changed and doesn't need to: top-and-bottom rigs with sandworms, clam strips, or grass shrimp, dropped straight down over the rockpiles at New Haven's east and west breakwalls, the Charles Island reef, and the Falkner Island structure. High tide and the two hours around it have been the strongest window, with porgies stacking thick enough that doubles are common and sea bass mixed in on almost every other drop. Stratford Shoal is worth the run for boats that can handle the current — deeper water there is holding bigger sea bass, some pushing three pounds, on squid strips fished tight to bottom.

Looking into Labor Day week, expect more boat traffic and more pressure on all these spots as the holiday pulls people onto the water, but the underlying pattern should hold — warm water keeps the porgy and sea bass bite steady, bluefish stay locked to bait, and stripers keep working the Charles Island bar on tide rather than committing to anything bigger. Watch the moon build toward full over the next several days; as tidal flow increases, that's usually the trigger that starts concentrating bait harder in the back bays and bar edges, and that's when the striper bite tends to step up a notch. If you've only got one trip in you before the holiday, fish the breakwalls on the high and don't overthink it — that's where the guaranteed action is right now.
