# August Heat Pushes Stripers to the River Mouth Seam, Cocktail Blues Show at Great Island
*With the Sound running warm and slack, the only relief on the Lower Connecticut is where fresh water meets salt — and that's where the fish are stacking.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — August 10** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

Joe-pye weed is thick along the Old Lyme marsh banks this week, that dusty purple you get in the dog days, and the osprey chicks off Great Island are up on the wing now, testing those long legs over the flats before the whole family starts drifting south. That's always been my clock for where we are in the season — not the calendar, the birds and the weeds. And what they're telling me right now is that we're deep into the slow, heavy part of summer, when this river stops being a highway for migrating fish and starts being a refuge for the ones that already live here.

The shad are long gone — that run wrapped up back in May, and by the time the joe-pye blooms there isn't a roe shad or a hickory left worth chasing above Essex. What we've got now is the August pattern I've watched unfold on this river for fifty years running: stripers pulling off the open Sound and tucking into the cool seam right at the river mouth, where the outflow off Saybrook Point meets the saltier water pushing up on the flood. That thermal break is the whole game right now. The Sound itself is running warm — mid-70s out toward the mouth, pushing higher on the open water — and fish don't want to sit in that if they don't have to. The river mouth, especially on the last two hours of the ebb when all that cooler upstream flow is pouring out past the breakwater, gives them a lane of relief they can hold in without burning energy.

I've been working that seam hard on the outgoing, anchoring up just off the Saybrook Point breakwater rocks in 15 to 20 feet and drifting bunker chunks back into the current on fish-finder rigs, letting them settle right where the darker river water and the clearer Sound water are visibly grinding against each other. That current line is easy to spot on a calm morning — you'll see the color change and a little foam streak riding it. Stripers have been holding right on that edge, mostly schoolies and a few respectable mid-20-inch fish, and the bite's been tightest in that low-light window — first hour of light and again the last hour before dark. Midday, with this heat sitting on the water, it's been dead slow out there unless you can find deeper structure holding shade or a strong push of current. That's not me sugarcoating it — daytime action off the Point has been tough, and anybody telling you different this week probably hasn't been out there at noon.

Up around Essex and the DEP Marina, the white perch bite has stayed steady, which is the one thing you can almost always count on here in August. Small pieces of bloodworm or a sliver of nightcrawler on a light spinning rig, fished right off the marina docks and the deeper edges of the channel on the last of the incoming tide, has been putting perch in the 8 to 10-inch range in the boat pretty reliably. It's not glamorous fishing but it's honest fishing, and on a hot afternoon when the stripers have shut down, it's given plenty of folks something to show for their time.

Now here's the piece worth talking about — the cocktail blues. We said a week back that the peanut bunker were spreading through these back bays and that it'd start showing up in the bluefish within a couple weeks. It's early yet, but I'm already seeing the front edge of that pattern build. Snapper blues have been thick in the marsh creeks off Great Island, chopping bait on the surface in the skinny water at the edges of the flats, and I've had a couple reports of bigger cocktail blues — 14, 15 inches — mixing in with them on the last of the ebb where those creek mouths dump into the main river channel. Small Kastmasters or a bare bucktail jig worked fast through the froth has been the ticket for the snappers, and if you slow down and work the deeper cuts with a slightly heavier metal, you'll start separating out the bigger cocktail fish from the pack. Give it another week or two and I expect that pattern to really fill in as more peanut bunker push down from upriver.

Blue crabs, for what it's worth, are thick as I've seen them in years in the shallow coves around Great Island and up into the Lieutenant River — if you've got a couple of hand lines and some chicken necks, that's a fine way to spend a slow afternoon while you wait for the tide to turn.

Looking ahead, the moon's been full and the tides have had some real push to them, which has been sharpening up those dawn and dusk windows nicely at the river mouth. As we come off full moon over the next several days, that current strength eases back some, and I'd expect the bite windows to settle back into a more standard rhythm rather than the sharp, short spikes we've had. If we get a stretch of cooler nights — even a couple degrees off overnight lows — I'd look for that river mouth seam to get even more attractive to stripers, and I wouldn't be surprised to see slightly better daytime action follow. Until then, I'm planning my trips around first light and last light at Saybrook Point, and keeping an eye on Great Island for that cocktail blue pattern to keep building through the rest of August.
