# Twotree Fluke Bite Rebuilds as Tide Fills In — Black Point Stacked With Sea Bass
*The neap slack finally broke, and from Niantic Bay to Hole-in-the-Wall the drift boats are putting keepers in the box again.*

**NIANTIC, CT — July 31** — _by Danny Colón, Zone Writer_

Ran into one of the Niantic fleet mates filling the bait tank Tuesday morning and he didn't even let me finish my coffee before he started talking fluke. Said the last three days out of the bay have been the best stretch since the Fourth — fish stacked up on the edges of Twotree Island Channel in 35 to 45 feet, and for the first time in a couple weeks the current actually had some shoulder to it. We flagged this exact thing last week — said the softened neap current was holding this zone back and it'd come around as the tide cycle rebuilt, and that's precisely what happened. Once that current started moving real water again, the fluke that had been sitting on their hands started sliding up onto the drop-offs to feed.

The program right now is textbook Twotree: white or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4 to 1-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow along the channel edge on the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the high. Keepers are mixing with a lot of shorts, so it's not a five-minute limit trip, but guys are grinding out three, four, five keeper fluke a rod in a half day when they hit the tide right. Black Point has been the same story in slightly shallower water, 28 to 35 feet, with the better fish coming off the sandy pockets between the rock piles rather than sitting tight to structure. If you're bouncing bucktails there, don't be afraid to let it hop bottom on the drift — the bigger fish have been eating on the pause, not the retrieve.

Sea bass are the sure thing right now, and I mean that in the honest sense — not blowing smoke, just telling you what's biting. Black Point reef and the rock piles off Hole-in-the-Wall are loaded with them in 30 to 50 feet. Jigging Spro bucktails tipped with squid or fresh clam is putting good numbers of 14 to 17-inch fish in coolers, with a scattering of shorts you'll be tossing back all day. Bring extra clam — they'll strip a hook clean in ten feet of current if you're slow to reset. Porgies are riding shotgun on the same drifts, chewing sandworms and clam on high-low rigs anywhere there's shell or rock, and they've been a nice bonus for anybody who wants a mixed bag rather than a species-specific trip.

Striped bass are the interesting piece this week, because Eastern Sound water is actually running a touch cooler than the rest of the Sound — New London's reading around 69.4 degrees, versus the low-to-mid 70s you're seeing farther west toward the city. That's still inside the window where bass will feed with some confidence during the day, not just at first and last light. It's showing on the water — guys working bunker schools off McCook Point and the mouth of the Niantic River at dawn are still connecting on slot fish up to 30-something inches, working white bucktails and small bunker spoons through the edges of the pods. But once the sun gets up and that shallow water warms, the bite shuts down fast and the better fish slide off into deeper, current-swept water and don't show again until dusk. If you want a real shot at a bigger bass, fish live eels or chunked bunker on the outgoing at Hole-in-the-Wall after dark — that's been the more consistent producer for size the last two weeks, even if the numbers are lower than the dawn bunker bite.

Bluefish have been riding shotgun with those bunker schools, and when they show up they show up hard — cocktail blues to slammers blitzing bait right on the surface off Black Point and the outer bay in the early morning. Poppers and metal like a Kastmaster or a Hopkins will get bit fast when they're up, but it's a short window before the school moves or sounds.

Now here's the piece everybody at the dock has started whispering about. We put this zone on early bonito and albie watch a few days back given how close we sit to the Race and Fishers Island corridor, and there's chatter starting to circulate about bait behavior out that way that's consistent with the first scouts showing up. Nothing confirmed yet, no verified fish on the beach, but the water's setting up the way it usually does right before those first false albacore show — warm stable water, good bait concentrations, current lines sharpening up as the tide rebuilds toward the August 12 new moon. I wouldn't plan a whole trip around it yet, but if you're out around the Race or working the rips off Fishers, keep a light spinning rod rigged with an epoxy jig or a small Deadly Dick within reach. That's the kind of thing that shows up with zero warning.

Looking ahead, the tide's only getting stronger as we build back toward the new moon, which should keep concentrating bait and fish around structure at Twotree and Black Point. If we get a passing thunderstorm or two this week — pretty typical for early August — expect a day or two of disrupted current and dirty water before it settles back into rhythm. My plan for the next few days is simple: fluke and sea bass on the day tide, bunker schools at first light for stripers, and an eye on the horizon for that first albie sign.
