# Three Weeks, No Wobble: The 100-Fathom Line Holds and So Does the Tuna
*A warm filament pinned along the Hudson wall keeps bluefin and yellowfin trolling steady, but the deeper bigeye bite stays a coin flip after dark.*

**MANASQUAN INLET, NJ — September 21** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Three weeks running now, the warm filament pushing across the canyon has held its lane along the 100-fathom line without sliding east or west more than a mile or two on any given day. That's the number that matters this week — not a single SST reading, but the consistency of the break itself. When that edge stays put for that long, bait stacks along it instead of getting pushed around, and boats working the Hudson wall from the Claw down through the Dip have been finding tuna in roughly the same water day after day instead of hunting a moving target. We flagged this exact risk back on the 11th — said the filament's position was the single biggest variable to watch for this zone — and three weeks later it's still the answer to why this bite has been steady instead of streaky.

The run out has been more comfortable than ugly. Buoy 44025 out in the Bight was reading 3.9-foot seas at 5 seconds midweek with water around 71°F on the surface closer to shore — a bumpy but manageable ride for boats making the trip from Manasquan, Shinnecock, Jones, and Moriches. Once you clear the shelf and get onto the canyon edge itself, the water cleans up and turns that deep cobalt blue that tells you the filament's warm core is doing its job pushing bait up onto the troll line. The Harvest Moon phase this week has sharpened the tide swings out there too, and the stronger current has kept bait pinned tighter to structure along both the Claw and the Dip rather than spreading thin across open water — good news for anyone running a spread instead of hunting blind.

Yellowfin have been the most dependable player in the spread. Boats trolling green and yellow machines, small Ilanders behind ballyhoo, and cedar plugs mixed into the pattern have been picking up fish in the 25 to 45-pound class along the edge in 100 to 250 fathoms, mostly working the color change where the filament's warm water meets the cooler flank. That cooler-flank pattern is worth noting — the bluefin specifically have stayed scattered along those cooler edges rather than balling up tight inside the warmest core, which tracks with what we said was likely back on the 18th. Vertical jigging with 200 to 300-gram speed jigs dropped on marks holding bait has worked better for bluefin than blind trolling through the middle of the warm water — those fish are sitting on the margins, not the center, and the jig bite has been the more consistent producer for boats willing to idle and mark fish instead of just pulling.

Bigeye have shown up but the bite stays what it's always been in September — a night game, and an inconsistent one. Chunking bunker and butterfish on the drift after dark near the Dip has produced fish in the 60 to 100-pound range for boats willing to put in the hours, but for every good report there's a boat that sat on bait all night and never got bit. If bigeye is the target, this is a lottery-ticket play right now, not a sure thing — worth doing if that's the specific goal, but don't build the whole trip around it.

Wahoo and mahi have been the wildcard part of the spread. High-speed trolling with deep-diving lures and planers along current seams near the canyon edge has turned up wahoo in the 30 to 50-pound class for boats running that program specifically, though it's not an every-day fish — more of a bonus when the spread crosses the right rip. Mahi have been holding around any floating structure or weed lines that collect along the edge where the filament pushes debris and bait together; smaller fish mostly, in the 5 to 15-pound range, but a welcome fill for the fish box when the bigger stuff goes quiet. White marlin have been scattered through the same water, showing on naked ballyhoo and small dink baits worked behind the spread, mostly as incidental catches rather than a targeted bite — the kind of thing that happens when the yellowfin spread is doing its job and a marlin decides to join in.

Looking at the next several days, the pattern should hold as long as that filament stays where it's been. The near-term outlook favors more of the same — steady yellowfin trolling along the wall, scattered bluefin on the jig around the cooler edges, and a bigeye bite that stays hit-or-miss after dark. The bigger question is the back half of the week and into next: any real cooling signal moving through the region typically starts tightening up structure everywhere from the Bight down through the mid-Atlantic, and if that filament finally starts to slip, this is the kind of setup that can contract fast toward the true canyon edges rather than spreading bait out across the wider troll zone. For now, boats should keep working the Claw and the Dip while the water's holding shape, and anyone planning a run late this week should check the latest position of that break before committing — if it's still sitting where it's been, the trip's worth making. If it starts to move, be ready to shift with it rather than fish where it used to be.
