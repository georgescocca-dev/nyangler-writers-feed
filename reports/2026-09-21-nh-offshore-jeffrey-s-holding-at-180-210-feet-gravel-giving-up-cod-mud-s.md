# Jeffrey's holding at 180-210 feet: gravel giving up cod, mud still stingy on haddock
*The ledge is settling into its fall pattern, but the haddock bite on Scantum's mud hasn't turned on the way the gravel has.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — September 21** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Jeffrey's Ledge is sitting right where it should for the third week of September — 180 to 210 feet on the main hump, gravel edges holding fish, mud basins still soft. That's the split that matters right now. Boats working the gravel shoulders on the west side of the ledge, the harder bottom where the current breaks and stacks bait against the rise, are finding cod in decent numbers, mostly school fish in the 6 to 12 pound range with the occasional 20-pounder mixed in. Move fifteen minutes east into the softer mud basins toward Scantum, and the haddock that were supposed to be thick by now are patchy — a dozen keepers on a good drift, then twenty minutes of nothing.

Water's sitting in the low 60s out there, which is normal for this time of year and not a red flag by itself, but it's enough to keep bait spread instead of balled up tight, and that's the difference between a stacked haddock drift and a scratchy one. We flagged this exact pattern last week — said the zone would hold steady with typical mid-September behavior rather than break hard one way or the other, and that's exactly what's played out. No collapse, no explosion, just a slow grind that rewards guys who know which piece of bottom they're sitting on.

Cod fishing has been the more consistent story. The gravel patches on the northwest corner of Jeffrey's and the hard edges running down toward Platts Bank are the go-to right now. Standard approach: diamond jigs, 8 to 12 ounces depending on drift speed, tipped with a strip of clam belly or a chunk of fresh mackerel if you've got it, worked with a short hop off bottom and a pause. The fish are sitting right on the structure, not up in the water column, so anyone fishing too fast through the drop is going right over their heads. Bait rigs with skate belly or clam on a two-hook high-low setup are still producing on the slower tide stages, especially around the last two hours of the ebb when the current lays down enough to hold bottom without a ton of lead.

Haddock have been the frustrating half of the week. Scantum Basin and the mud stretches off Tillies Bank are the historical go-to, and there are fish there — the reports coming back aren't skunks, they're just thin. Clam-baited rigs on standard cod gear are taking a mix of haddock and pollock, but the ratio's been running more pollock than haddock on a lot of drifts, which tells you the bait isn't concentrated enough yet to pull the haddock into tight pods. Best window has been first light through mid-morning, before the wind builds and before boat traffic starts pushing multiple drifts over the same piece of structure.

Pollock have actually been the pleasant surprise, especially up around the pinnacles closer to Cape Ann and off Thatcher Island, where boats working mid-water with jigs — bucktails or soft plastics worked 20 to 40 feet up off the bottom — have been into some quality fish in the 10 to 15 pound class. That's a nice change of pace on a slow groundfish stop and worth a few drifts if the cod bite goes quiet.

On the tuna side, it's exactly what you'd expect heading into the back half of September — scattered, opportunistic, tied to whale activity rather than a hard warm-water push. There's no filament of warm water holding a concentrated bite out there right now, so the fish that are around are following bait, not temperature. Boats have been marking fish near whale pods working bait balls along the north end of Jeffrey's and out toward the Stellwagen approaches, but it's not a pattern you can set a course for — it's a pattern you have to hunt. Birds diving, whales surfacing, bait getting nervous on the surface — that's the tell, and when you see it, get a spreader bar or a couple of daisy chains in the water fast because the window doesn't stay open long.

Looking ahead, the next few days should hold close to what we've had — moderate winds, no major front pushing through to scramble things, and water temps easing down slowly rather than dropping hard. That favors the gravel bite continuing on cod while the mud stays inconsistent for haddock until the bait tightens up, which usually happens as the water cools another few degrees into early October. That's also when this fishery turns a corner — the giant bluefin start showing heavier and more consistently around the ledge as herring and sand eels ball up tighter under the whales, and boats that put in the time now watching for surface activity are the ones who'll be ready when that push arrives. If you've only got one day this week, work the gravel on Jeffrey's early with clam-tipped jigs, and keep an eye out toward the north end and the Stellwagen approaches for any whale activity worth running on.
