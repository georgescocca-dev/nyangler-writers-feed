# A Lost Sword at Oceanographer and the Call That Confirmed It
*The daytime squid drop we flagged last week held up, and the fleet's fuel math says it's worth the extra hour to Oceanographer's east wall.*

**MONTAUK, NY — September 14** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

The radio traffic Tuesday afternoon told the story before any boat hit the dock. A charter working the east wall of Oceanographer Canyon came up on 68 looking for a gaff boat, said they'd had a broadbill up boatside on a daytime drop and lost it at the leader — hook pulled clean after a twenty-minute fight on a rod nobody expected to bend that hard before noon. That's the kind of call that gets repeated at the fuel dock for a week, and it should. We flagged this exact bite last week — said the swordfish activity out east was worth watching closely — and here it is, still going, still on live squid, still happening in daylight instead of the midnight drops guys ran all summer.

That's the standout story in this zone right now, and it's worth understanding why it's holding. The warm filament that's been feeding yellowfin and mahi along the edge hasn't broken down, and where that warm water sits up against the canyon structure, it's pushing squid and small forage up onto the bottom contours where broadbill feed during the day instead of waiting for dark. Oceanographer's the one producing right now. Whether that spreads west into Hydrographer or down into Veatch and Atlantis over the next week is the question every captain running out of Montauk, Block Island, and the Rhode Island ports is asking each other on the ride out.

Bluefin remain scattered across the zone, which matches what the rest of the coast has been seeing — no single concentration, just fish showing in ones and twos on trollers working the temperature breaks between Veatch and Atlantis. Yellowfin and mahi are the more dependable meat right now, holding on the warm edge where the color change is sharp enough to see from the wheelhouse — that blue-to-green seam is still the first thing worth running to before wetting a line.

For the swordfish game, boats working Oceanographer are running the standard daytime rig — a fresh or frozen squid skirted or wrapped, dropped on wind-on leader with 60 to 80 pounds of lead depending on drift speed, worked down to whatever depth the sounder's showing marks, often deep on the contour where the bottom breaks toward the canyon wall. Patience is the whole game. These aren't fish you jig for — you drop, you wait, you watch the rod tip, and most days you wait longer than you'd like. The lost fish this week is proof there are swords willing to eat during daylight right now, but proof also that hook sets on a slow, deep bite need a firm hand and a clean drop-back before you come tight.

On the troll, yellowfin are coming on the usual spread of cedar plugs, small Islanders, and skirted ballyhoo pulled along the warm-water edge, with mahi showing up as a bonus around any floating structure or weed line inside that same water. Nothing about the yellowfin bite has been fast this week — boats are working for a handful of fish rather than a double-digit day, and that's consistent with scattered bait rather than a concentrated push. If the mahi are stacked under a good weed line, that can turn into the better fish of the day fast, so it's worth slowing the troll and working any floating grass thoroughly before moving on.

The run itself is the other half of this business, and it doesn't get easier this time of year. Out of Montauk, Oceanographer and Hydrographer both sit at distances that put you into serious fuel math before you clear the dock — figure a long day even in decent conditions, and budget accordingly if you're running a boat that isn't set up for offshore range. From Block Island or the Rhode Island ports, Veatch and Atlantis sit a bit more favorably on the compass, which is part of why that fleet leans that direction while the Montauk boats have been the ones reporting from Oceanographer this week. None of these are runs you make on a maybe — check the weather window both ways, not just the ride out, because a flat morning offshore with a building afternoon sea has caught more than one boat on the way home.

Looking ahead, the moon is working toward new over the next several days, which historically tightens up current on the canyon edges and can sharpen the bite window for both the swordfish drop and the tuna troll — worth watching for current speed picking up on the wall at Oceanographer specifically, since that's where the sword bite has been living. If the pattern holds and that warm edge doesn't slide off the structure, the smart play through the weekend is still Oceanographer for the daytime squid drop with Veatch and Atlantis as the troll option for yellowfin and mahi on the warm side of the break. If the swordfish action does start showing at Hydrographer or Veatch, that would say the whole system is holding steady rather than just parked in one spot — and that's the sign worth watching for before committing a long run to a single canyon this week. Best case, the warm filament holds through the weekend and the bite spreads. Worst case, it stays isolated to Oceanographer and the rest of the fleet keeps burning fuel on tuna alone. Either way, check sea state on both legs before you leave the dock — the ride home has ended more good days than the fishing ever has.
