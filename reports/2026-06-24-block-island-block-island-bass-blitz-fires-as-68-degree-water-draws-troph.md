# Block Island bass blitz fires as 68-degree water draws trophy stripers to the ledges
*Mid-40-inch class fish crushing bucktails and flutter spoons as perfect conditions align.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — June 24** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

The stars aligned this week around Block Island, and the striped bass responded exactly as they should. Water temps hit that magic 68-degree mark that draws big fish from hundreds of miles away, and the Southwest Ledge has been absolutely electric with trophy-class bass.

I've been watching the buoy readings climb steadily, and when that 44025 station showed 68.2 degrees with dead calm conditions, I knew we were in for something special. The fish have been stacked on the ledge in 45 to 60 feet, and they're not the schoolies we've been picking at all spring — these are legitimate cows, fish pushing the mid-40-inch mark that make your drag scream.

The bite has been all about presentation and timing. Flutter spoons dropped right on top of marked fish have been absolutely deadly. I watched one angler work a 3-ounce flutter spoon in a slow, erratic drop through a school of bass suspended at 35 feet, and the fish crushed it so hard it nearly pulled the rod from his hands. The key is letting that spoon helicopter down through the water column — these fish want something that looks wounded and easy.

Bucktails have been equally productive, especially white and chartreuse combinations in the 1 to 2-ounce range. The trick is working them just off the bottom with a slow, steady retrieve. Don't overthink it — these bass are here to feed, and they're not particularly finicky about presentation when the conditions are right.

The North Rip has been producing as well, though the fish there seem more scattered. I've been finding bass in the 25 to 35-foot range, suspended in the water column and feeding aggressively on the abundant bait that's been pushed up from deeper water. The cooler, oxygenated water around the island is acting like a magnet for both bait and the bass that follow them.

What's really encouraging is the quality of fish we're seeing. These aren't just numbers — we're talking about genuine trophy bass, the kind of fish that make the long run to Block Island worthwhile. I had one angler describe a fish that fought so hard and took such long runs that he thought his 10-pound test was going to snap. That's the kind of power you get from a mature female striper in prime condition.

The bite has been best on the moving water, particularly during the last two hours of the outgoing tide. The fish seem to position themselves along the ledge edges where the current creates upwellings and concentrates bait. I've been marking schools of sand eels and small bunker, and wherever you find that bait concentrated, you'll find bass underneath.

Bonito have also started to show up in the mix, which is always a good sign. These speedsters are typically the first to arrive when conditions are prime, and their presence usually means the bigger predators aren't far behind. I've seen a few taken on small metal lures worked fast through the surface layers.

The weather pattern has been absolutely perfect — light winds, calm seas, and that high pressure system keeping everything stable. The water clarity around the island is exceptional right now, that gin-clear blue that tells you the ecosystem is healthy and oxygenated.

Looking ahead, I expect this bite to continue strong through the weekend and into next week. The water temperatures are holding steady in that optimal range, and the bait concentrations show no signs of dispersing. The new moon cycle we're entering should create some serious tidal movement that will only enhance the feeding opportunities.

If you're planning a trip, focus on the Southwest Ledge and the North Rip during the moving water. Bring flutter spoons in multiple weights, bucktails in white and chartreuse, and don't forget a few topwater options for early morning and late evening. These fish are here, they're feeding, and they're the kind of bass that make Block Island the summer Mecca it's always been.
