# Wreck 4108 at 140 Feet: Sea Bass to 4 Pounds, Mahi Working the Pot Lines
*The tide's ripping harder than it should for August, but the fish that matter are still exactly where twenty years of numbers say they'll be.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 10** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

Wreck 4108 sits in 140 feet on the inside lip of the Mud Hole, and it's been holding sea bass to four pounds since the back half of July. That number's in my book from a dozen seasons of marking it, and it hasn't let me down this year either — but I'll tell you straight, the window to fish it clean has gotten narrow. We're coming off a full moon and the current on that wreck at peak tide is moving too fast to hold bottom with anything lighter than an 8-ounce bucktail. I've been running out there for the last of the morning slack, before the tide builds, dropping 6-ounce jigheads tipped with white Gulp Grubs and letting them settle right into the wreck's lee side. That's where the bigger fish are stacked, sitting just above the thermocline where the cooler water holds the bait tight to structure. Once the tide turns hard around mid-morning, I'm off it — no sense burning gas fighting a drift that's dragging your rig sideways across open sand.

Barnegat Ridge has been the more forgiving structure this week. Sixty to ninety feet of water, less current punch than the Mud Hole wrecks, and the sea bass there are smaller on average but steadier — a lot of 14 to 16-inch fish mixed with the occasional keeper pushing three pounds. Axel Carlson Reef and Sea Girt Reef are fishing the same way right now: clam-tipped bucktails or a Gulp swimming mullet on a 3/4-ounce jig, dropped tight to the reef balls on the drift. Shrewsbury Rocks has been quieter than I'd like for mid-August — more short fish than keepers — but it's still worth a stop on the way out if you're running past it anyway.

The story that's actually got my phone ringing this week is the mahi around the pot buoys. There's a real push of warm water sitting on the shelf right now — I was reading almost 79 degrees on the offshore buoy east of the Ridge, which lines up with what I'm seeing on the color change out past the twenty-fathom curve. That warm tongue has pot buoys and loose lobster gear loaded with bait, and where there's bait under a buoy in August, there's mahi cruising the shadow line underneath it. I've had guys casting small poppers and bucktails right up against the buoy and getting hit before the lure even settles. If you find one holding fish, work it slow and quiet — don't run the boat right over it, ease up current and drift down on it. Mahi in the 3 to 8-pound class have been the norm, with a few better fish mixed in for guys willing to put in the buoy-hopping time. It's not every buoy — you might check ten and find fish under two of them — but when you connect, it's worth the search.

Out toward the Klondike and the edge, the yellowfin sign has been real but spotty. The warm eddy positioning that's been feeding the mahi bite is also pulling some school-size yellowfin up onto the temp break, and a couple of the boats running that far have put together respectable days chunking butterfish on the color change, with small tuna spreads — Green Machines and cedar plugs — picking up fish trolling the edge between drops. But I want to be honest about this one: it's a run-and-gun proposition right now. You're covering extra miles to find where that break sits day to day, and not every trip out there connects. If the eddy holds its position through the week the way it has the last several days, that pattern should keep producing. If it slides, the fish move with it, and you're back to searching.

On bluefin, I'll give it to you straight — the Mud Hole lumps have quieted down from where they were a month ago. The bigger class of fish that was working those lumps in June and early July has largely pushed north, and what's left is school-size bluefin passing through early, usually right at first light on bait pods before the boat traffic pushes them down. If you want a shot, be on the water before sunrise with butterfish chunks ready to go the second you mark fish on bait. By eight o'clock, that window's usually closed.

Looking ahead, the moon's starting to ease off full, which should settle those wreck currents back into a more workable rhythm by midweek — good news for anyone planning to fish the deeper Mud Hole structure without fighting a firehose tide. Seas have been running modest, in the 2 to 3-foot range with a manageable period, which is fishable for the run to the Ridge or the edge, but keep an eye on the afternoon sea breeze — it's been building stronger by midweek lately and can turn a comfortable morning run into a sloppy afternoon comeback. If that warm water holds its position off the shelf, I'd keep working the pot lines for mahi and picking away at the wrecks for sea bass on the calmer tide windows. If it pushes further offshore, the yellowfin bite might improve as the break tightens up — worth checking before you commit to the long run.
