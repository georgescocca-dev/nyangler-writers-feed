# New Haven Breakwalls Hand Over Porgies and Sea Bass While Bass Stack Under the Bait
*A slack neap tide is turning the rockpiles precise again, and bunker pods off Charles Island are still pulling blitzes out of the blue water.*

**NEW HAVEN, CT — August 7** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Drop a chunk of green crab down eighteen feet off the New Haven Harbor breakwalls right now and you won't wait long. Porgies are stacked thick on the inside rocks, and mixed into them are sea bass that keep grabbing the bottom hook before the top one even settles. That's the kind of two-species tangle that makes bottom fishing fun instead of a chore, and this week the drift finally slowed down enough to fish it properly.

We flagged this last week — said the coming neap tide would ease the grip on structure-dependent spots, and that's exactly what showed up on the breakwalls and at Stratford Shoal. Current that was ripping through on the spring tides two weeks ago has gone soft and manageable, which means less split shot, tighter drifts, and baits sitting where you actually put them instead of getting swept off the rock face. For anyone who's fought a three-ounce sinker just to hold bottom on the outgoing, this is the window you wait for.

The porgy bite along the outer breakwall stones has been steady on the last two hours of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing — that current change stirs bottom activity without blowing the drift out. High-low rigs with size 4 or 6 hooks, green crab or squid strips, fifteen to twenty-five feet of water. Sea bass are mixed right in on the same drops, especially where the rock pile drops into deeper pockets on the harbor side. A bucktail tipped with a strip of squid, hopped slow off bottom, has been drawing the better sea bass — fifteen to seventeen inches, with enough shorts in the mix that you're culling, not scratching.

Blackfish have started showing more consistently on the same structure, working the crevices where the rockpile meets sand. Crab baits fished tight to the rocks on a slower drift are getting bit, though it's a patient game — more missed bites than hookups most trips, which is normal for this stage of the season on the inshore piles.

Out at Stratford Shoal, the deeper humps — forty to sixty feet — are holding a better class of sea bass, and the eased current has made it possible to actually stay on the structure instead of getting dragged off it in one pass. Squid strips or clam belly on standard sea bass rigs, dropped right to the edges where the shoal falls into deeper water, has been the play. It's not a bent-rod-every-drop bite, but anyone willing to reposition and re-drift a few times over the same piece of bottom is finding fish worth keeping.

Charles Island is where the excitement's been, though, and it's been more about what's cruising over the sandbar than what's biting bottom. Bunker pods have been thick off the bar on the flood, and wherever they concentrate, the surface has been exploding — bluefish first, tearing through the edges of the bait, with striped bass working underneath picking off the stragglers. We called this pattern building two weeks back, tied to bunker schools holding thick in stable weather, and it's still playing out almost exactly that way. Topwater plugs and poppers thrown into the froth are drawing violent bluefish strikes, while swimming plugs and soft-plastic swimbaits worked a little deeper and slower are the better bet for the bass sitting under the commotion.

The sandbar itself is exposing more at dead low with the neap tide's smaller swings, so anyone working it on foot has a wider window to cast the edges before the water comes back over. Best window has been the last two hours of the flood into the top of the tide, right as bunker push up tight to the bar and get pinned against it.

Falkner Island has had spotty reports of bluefish activity working bait pods on the surface, similar to Charles Island but less consistent — worth a look if you're running past it, but it hasn't been the sure thing the inshore bar has been this week.

Over at Milford Gulf, fluke fishing has been a mixed bag. Drifting the deeper channel edges with white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails has produced keeper-class fish in the eighteen to twenty-two inch range, but you're covering a lot of bottom for each fish. The Sound's water has stayed warm through this stretch — readings across the broader region have been running mid-to-high 70s — and that warmth seems to be spreading fluke out rather than concentrating them, so it's a numbers game on the drift, not a spot-lock situation.

Hammonasset has been quieter on the report side this week, though the shallow flats there tend to hold bait and warm fast in the afternoon sun, and that's usually enough to draw feeding fish in on the tail end of the ebb even when nothing dramatic is happening offshore.

Looking ahead, this eased neap current should hold through the next few days before the tides start building back toward the next new moon, so there's a real window right now to fish the rockpiles with precision before the drift picks back up and turns technical again. If the bunker pods stay thick off Charles Island — and there's no sign yet they're thinning — the topwater window there should keep producing on the flood tides through the week. I'd fish the breakwalls early for porgies and sea bass while the tide's soft, then swing out to the bar for the last of the incoming when the bait starts showing on the surface. Worth having both plans ready; this pattern won't last forever, but it hasn't shown signs of breaking yet either.
