# Manasquan Rocks at Dawn: Blues on Top, Bass Ghosting the Runoff
*Two months of dependable bluefish carried the beat again this week while stripers stayed buried in dirty water and short daylight windows.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 17** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Five-fifteen, North Jetty at Manasquan, and there were already six rods stuck in the rocks before the sky turned from black to gray. That's normal for August. What's not normal is watching three of those guys hook up in the first fifteen minutes on nothing fancier than a bunker spoon dragged across the current seam where the inlet dumps into the ocean. Three-to-four pound blues, chopping through peanut bunker that have finally pushed tight to the beach after weeks of getting pushed around by runoff. I've been walking these rocks since before some of these guys had licenses, and I'll say it plain — the bluefish have been the one thing you could count on all summer, and this week didn't break that streak.

We flagged this two weeks back — said the blue bite would hold as long as the bunker stayed tight to the beach, and here we are, still doing it. Snapper blues are thick as gnats in Shark River and up in the back stretches near the Belmar bridge, which if you've fished this beat as long as I have, you know is the tell. Snappers this dense in mid-August mean the adult class fish keep showing through Labor Day and into the albie window. I had a kid next to me at Shark River Inlet on Tuesday morning slinging a Kastmaster into a snapper blitz so thick the water looked like it was boiling — no exaggeration needed, just a lot of one-pound fish eating everything that moved.

Bass have been the harder conversation. We've had close to eight inches of rain dump on this stretch over the last month, and that runoff has turned the nearshore water the color of chocolate milk from Sea Bright down through Avon. Stripers don't love feeding through dirty water in daylight, so what you're left with are tight windows — first light and last light, when the water's still cool enough and the light's low enough that a bass will commit. I worked the Sea Bright rocks Thursday at 5:30 with a black Danny swimmer and picked off one schoolie, maybe 22 inches, working the pocket on the north side where the jetty breaks the current on an incoming tide. That's one bass in ninety minutes of casting. Not the kind of morning you write home about, but it's honest, and it's what's out there right now. If you want a bass on this beat this week, fish the last hour of the incoming or the first hour of the outgoing, right at the jetty tips where the dirty water gets strained a little cleaner passing over the rocks, and don't expect volume.

Fluke have quietly been the better story for the guys working boats out of Belmar and Manasquan. Warm water this time of year makes fluke lazy, and lazy fluke want a bigger meal without much effort — that's why the oversized baits are outproducing the small stuff. Guys drifting the Manasquan Inlet channel on the last of the outgoing, working 4- to 5-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails tight to the rock piles on either side of the inlet, have been putting three and four keepers in the cooler on a good drift — nothing like the LBI reports but respectable for water this warm. Depth's not deep in there, maybe 15 to 20 feet on the channel edges, but that's where the current concentrates the bait against structure, and fluke don't have to work hard to eat.

Blackfish and porgies are still off-season conversation for another few weeks, so I won't waste your time on them — but keep an eye on the calendar, because once the water starts to slide the other direction in September, the Sea Bright and Manasquan wrecks close in will start producing again and it always sneaks up on guys who weren't paying attention.

Here's what I'd do with the week ahead. The moon's building toward the full on the 28th, which means tide strength is going to pick up steadily through the weekend — good news for both the bass and the fluke, because stronger current means better bait concentration at the jetty tips and inlet mouths. If I had one morning to fish, I'd take the last hour of a strong outgoing at Manasquan's south jetty, right around first light, throwing a darter or a bigger swimmer through the white water where the rocks meet the sandbar. That's historically where the bigger bass show first once the tide gets some push behind it, especially if the runoff finally clears out with a stretch of dry weather. Blues will keep eating anything that moves regardless — that part of the program isn't changing anytime soon. Watch for false albacore too. It's a touch early yet for this stretch, but if the water temps ease off even a degree or two heading into September the way they usually do, Shark River Inlet and the Spring Lake jetties are where they'll show first, and when they do, small metal like a half-ounce Deadly Dick is the play.

Bottom line for this week: blues are the sure thing, fluke are the pleasant surprise if you've got a boat, and bass require patience, dirty-water patience, fished right at first light with low expectations and a willingness to walk more rocks than usual to find the one pocket that's holding fish.
