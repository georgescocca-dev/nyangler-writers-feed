# Halfway Rock on the Turn: Bass Ride the Current Edges, Groundfish Hold Deep
*Mid-July has the bay's current edges doing the work — stripers riding the seams, cod and haddock steady on the slack below Halfway Rock.*

**PORTLAND, ME — July 17** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

I was out past Peaks Island Tuesday morning, drifting toward Halfway Rock on the tail of the ebb, when I watched the water go from chop to glass in about ninety seconds. That's the tell I've learned to trust after thirty years out here — the moment the current lets go, right before slack, when the bait that's been pinned against the ledge for six hours suddenly has nowhere to hide. Terns dropped on it first, then the surface dimpled with mackerel showing themselves, and under all of that, on the sounder, the kind of soft, structured mark that means cod and haddock are holding tight to the rock rather than roaming.

That's been the pattern all week, and it's a good one if you're patient about it. Halfway Rock in 50 to 70 feet is still producing groundfish on the last hour of the ebb into slack — jigging diamond jigs and 6-ounce Norwegian jigs tipped with a strip of mackerel belly, worked slow off bottom, has been the ticket. Nothing spectacular in numbers, but steady enough that a couple hours around the rock this week put keeper haddock and a mix of legal and short cod in the box for the boats I talked to at the co-op. The moment the tide starts to run hard again, that bite shuts down almost like someone flipped a switch, so timing the slack matters more than anything else you do out there.

The stripers have shifted their address a little from where they were in June. Instead of working the open water off Portland, they've tucked into the current seams around the island guts — the gut between Peaks and Long Island, the backside of Bailey Island where the tide rips past the ledges, and the mouth of the Basin off Cape Small where warm, bait-rich water pours out on the ebb. That warm water pushing out of the shallow coves and marshes is doing exactly what it always does in mid-July — it's dragging silversides and juvenile pogies with it, and the bass are stacked right on that edge where warm meets cold. I've had the best luck working white and chartreuse soft plastics, a 4-inch Slug-Go or a paddle-tail swimbait on a light jighead, cast right up against the current line and let the tide sweep it through rather than retrieving hard against it. Bucktails in the ¾-ounce range work too if the current's running enough to keep them off bottom.

Bailey Island's backside has been the more consistent of the two for slot fish — 26 to 32 inches — particularly on the first two hours of the incoming, when bait gets pushed up against the ledges and the bass pin it there. Cape Small is more of a gamble right now; when the tide's right and the mackerel are thick, it's some of the best structure fishing in the whole bay, but I've had two trips out there this week where the bait just wasn't home and it was quiet water for three hours. That's the honest read — Cape Small is boom or bust this time of year, and you need to see bait working the surface before you commit to it.

Mackerel themselves have thinned out from the tight schools we had in late May and June, but they haven't left entirely. Small pods are still showing off Diamond Island and along the eastern side of Peaks, mostly early morning before the sun gets high. Jig with a mackerel tree, six hooks, no weight needed if they're near the surface, and you can fill a bucket in twenty minutes if you find them. That bait is exactly what's fueling both the striper bite along the ledges and, I suspect, some of the bluefish that have started showing this week — the first real bluefish reports I've had all season, mostly smaller choppers in the 2 to 4 pound range, snapping leaders off Two Lights and along the outer edge of Peaks on the last of the flood. Nothing to build a whole trip around yet, but worth having a wire leader in the box if you're working topwater plugs along that shoreline.

Water's been clean this week, no runoff issues from the rivers, and the wind has mostly cooperated — light out of the southwest most mornings, which keeps the outer ledges fishable. If that holds through the weekend, I'd put my time on Halfway Rock for the groundfish around the two slack windows each day, and I'd work the Bailey Island backside on the early flood for bass. If the wind swings onshore and kicks up a swell, the outer rock gets sloppy fast and I'd shift entirely inside to the Harpswell channels and the guts around the Calendar Islands, where the structure breaks the wave and the bass will still be working the current regardless of what's happening outside.

The next push worth watching is whether that bluefish showing turns into a real run. If the water keeps warming the way it has, and the bait stays thick along the eastern shore, I wouldn't be surprised to see bigger blues push into the bay by early August, the way they have in years when the mackerel linger into midsummer. For now, it's a solid week if you fish the tide stages correctly and an average one if you don't — which, out here, is usually the whole story.
