# New moon springs stack the tide at Marine Parkway — bass and fluke both playing the outgoing
*Bigger water is moving through the bay this week, and that's pushing bait — and fish — right where I want them.*

**JAMAICA BAY, NY — July 10** — _by Denise "Dee" Vasquez, Zone Writer_

We're heading into new moon springs this weekend, and if you fish this bay long enough you learn to respect what that does to the water. The moon's going dark Jul 11-13, and that means bigger tidal swings than we've had since the June full moon — more water moving in, more water moving out, faster current at every pinch point from the Cross Bay Bridge to the Rockaway Inlet. Last week the bay sat warm, pushing well into the upper 70s on the flats by midday, and that warmth did what it always does in July — it ran the fluke off the shallow sand and into the channels and inlets where the water stays cooler and the current keeps moving. This week's bigger springs are going to accelerate that shift. More flow means more bait getting flushed out of the marsh creeks and grass flats, and that's the dinner bell for everything from schoolie bass to blues to the fluke stacking up on the drop-offs.

The piece of this I'm most excited about is the evening window at Marine Parkway Bridge. New moon high stand is lining up closer to sunset this week than it has in a month, and when that outgoing starts ripping through the pilings right around dusk, the bass show up like they know the schedule. I fished it Tuesday on the last two hours of light and picked at schoolies and a couple of solid mid-20-inch fish on a 1.5-ounce white bucktail with a chartreuse Gulp trailer, working it slow along bottom in that seam where the current breaks off the pilings. If you want bigger fish, this is eel season under the bridge after dark — live eels drifted on a fish-finder rig into the shadow line have been good for a few keeper-class bass in the 28-32 inch range over the last two weeks. That bite really only turns on once the tide gets moving hard, so don't waste your time at slack. Give it the first hour and a half of outgoing and the last hour before the bottom of the tide.

Fluke are the other story right now, and it's a real shift, not just talk. With the bay water running warm on the flats, the doormats have slid off Ruffle Bar and the Broad Channel flats and pushed into the deeper channel edges — Cross Bay Bridge channel, the Rockaway Inlet drift, and the edge where Beach Channel dumps into the inlet proper. Outgoing tide is the play here, plain and simple — that cooler ocean water pushing in on the tail end of the ebb is exactly what's holding fish tight to the channel drop. I'm drifting 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails in 18 to 25 feet, keeping the boat slow enough that the jig ticks bottom every few seconds. Bucktail-and-squid-strip combos have worked just as well for anyone who wants to save on plastics. Word from the inlet has a few double-digit doormats showing up on these drifts already this month, which tells you the bigger fish followed the bait out ahead of the rest of the pack. It's not every drift — plenty of days you're culling through short fluke and dogfish before you connect — but when you find the right depth change, it's been worth the sorting.

Bluefish are starting to show some teeth in the surf. Breezy Point and the west end of Rockaway Beach have had scattered blitzes on peanut bunker and rainfish pods, usually right at first light or the last half hour before dark. Nothing sustained yet — more like fifteen-minute windows where the water erupts and then goes quiet — but if you're rigged with a metal like an Ava jig or a Deadly Dick and you're willing to walk the beach and watch for diving terns, you can time it. Snapper blues are thick in the back bay too, which is good news if you've got kids — light spinning gear, a small silver spoon or a piece of spearing under a bobber off Canarsie Pier will keep them busy for hours.

Weakfish, I'll be honest, are still a disappointment. The water's plenty warm enough on paper, but I haven't heard of a single quality weakfish out of this bay in weeks, and neither has anyone I trust at the docks. Warm water alone isn't pulling them in — something else has to click, and right now it hasn't. I wouldn't plan a trip around them. Porgies are a better bet for a light-tackle afternoon — small ones stacking up around the Cross Bay Bridge pilings and Beach Channel rock piles on sandworm or clam, nothing huge but steady action if you want dinner. Blackfish are still out of season here through the summer, so that's a fall conversation.

Looking ahead, I'd put my time at Marine Parkway on the outgoing into dusk through the weekend — the new moon springs peaking Saturday into Monday should push the strongest current and the best feeding window we've had in a month. If the bass don't cooperate, my plan B is the inlet fluke drift on that same outgoing water; between the two, something should be biting. Just watch the wind — if it swings onshore hard out of the southeast, the inlet gets sloppy fast and I'd shift inside to the Cross Bay Bridge channel instead.
