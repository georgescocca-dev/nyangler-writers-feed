# Manasquan Rocks at First Light: Blues Busting Bunker, Bass Gone to Bed
*The bunker showed up thick this week and the bluefish found them in broad daylight — the bass, meanwhile, want nothing to do with sunlight.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 21** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Five-fifteen Tuesday morning, north jetty at the inlet, and there's already three guys spread out on the rocks like it's a religious service. One of them — didn't get his name, wasn't going to interrupt a hookset — is doubled over on a metal-lip swimmer working the incoming current seam off the tip. He lands a bluefish that would've been respectable in October, mid-July be damned. Behind him a kid maybe nineteen years old is chunking bunker on a fish-finder rig into the same seam and getting bounced around by cocktail blues before his bait even settles. That's been the story all week from Manasquan north to Sea Bright, and it's not subtle.

The bunker showed up thick off the beach — pods stacked so tight off Belmar and Spring Lake you could see them nervous-watering from the boardwalk before the sun cleared the horizon. Where there's bunker like that in July, there's blues, and this week they came up shallow and stayed up shallow, blitzing in full daylight instead of waiting for the low-light windows they usually respect this time of year. We flagged this pattern holding last week — said as long as the high pressure stayed put and nothing scattered the bait, the blitz would keep rolling and start spreading up toward Sandy Hook. That's exactly what's happened. I had reports of bunker pods getting worked over as far north as the Sea Bright rocks by Wednesday, and the Reservation stretch was seeing gulls stacking up mid-morning, which if you've fished that beach long enough you know means something's happening under them.

The blues have been running mixed — plenty of cocktail-size fish in the 2 to 4 pound range, but enough slammers pushing 8 to 10 pounds mixed in that it's worth carrying a wire leader even if you hate the way it kills your action on a popper. Poppers and darters have been the move when you can see fish working the surface — a white Super Strike darter or a Gibbs pencil popper thrown past the edge of the blitz and worked back through it will get bit fast. When the surface action cools between waves of bait, Ava 27s and 47s jigged straight down off the jetty rocks into 15 to 20 feet of water have been picking off blues that dropped under the bunker schools.

Now here's the part that's been frustrating the guys who came out expecting stripers with the blues — the bass have gone quiet in daylight, and it's not a mystery why. Water's sitting in the low 70s along this stretch, warm enough that the bass want no part of feeding hard under a bright July sun. They're still around — I know guys who worked the Manasquan jetties an hour before first light and after last light this week and connected on schoolies to 28 inches on bunker chunks and darker Bomber swimmers worked slow through the current break at the tip. But if you're fishing 9 to noon expecting a bass blitz next to the blues, you're going to be disappointed. This is a low-light and after-dark fish right now, full stop, and that's going to hold as long as this warm, calm stretch of weather sits on top of us.

Fluke have been the quiet workhorse all week while everyone's chasing bunker noise. Shark River Inlet has been steady on the last two hours of the incoming, drifting white and chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails along the channel edges in 15 to 22 feet — nothing enormous, mostly 16 to 19 inches with the occasional keeper pushing 20, but a consistent pick if you can find moving water. We noted a couple weeks back that back-bay fluke would start pushing toward inlet mouths and ocean structure as the water kept warming, and that's basically what's happened at Shark River and off the Manasquan jetties — the ocean-side structure bite has gotten steadily better while the shallow back-bay stuff has slowed down some.

We're rolling toward the first-quarter neap now, which is going to soften the current push through these inlets over the next few days. That's actually good news for the bass — a slacker tide means those low-light windows at the jetty tips stretch a little longer and the bait doesn't get ripped through as fast, giving stripers a better shot at working a current seam without burning energy fighting it. If I'm picking a window this weekend, I'm on the Manasquan north jetty an hour before first light on the tail of the incoming, throwing a darker swimmer or a bunker chunk soaked tight to the rocks. Blackfish and porgies are still around the deeper structure off Belmar and Asbury for anyone who wants a change of pace, though nobody's rushing that bite while the bunker show is running. Watch the bait — if the pods stay put and this high pressure holds, the blitz should keep sliding north toward the Hook and the bass will keep doing their business after dark. If the wind swings and scatters that bunker, all bets are off and we're back to soaking bait and hoping.
