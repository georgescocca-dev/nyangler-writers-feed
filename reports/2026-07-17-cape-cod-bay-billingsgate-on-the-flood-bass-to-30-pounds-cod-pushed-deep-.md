# Billingsgate on the Flood: Bass to 30 Pounds, Cod Pushed Deep for Summer
*Mid-July heat has scattered the bass and sent the cod off the shoals, but the flood tide over Billingsgate and the rips off Race Point are still giving up fish to the patient boat.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — July 17** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

Wednesday morning I had the boat idling off Billingsgate an hour before the top of the flood, watching the fish finder paint bait stacked tight to the bottom in eighteen feet. No birds working, no surface commotion, just that quiet hum on the sounder that tells you something's down there worth a look. I dropped a live pogie on a three-way rig, let it settle to the sand, and within five minutes had a bass pulling drag toward the deeper edge of the flat. Thirty pounds, give or take, came over the rail heavy and dark, the kind of fish that's been living on the bottom structure instead of chasing bait on top. That's been the story this month — the big ones are here, but they're not showing themselves the way they do in June. You have to go down and get them.

Water's warmed up enough now that the mackerel schools that held the bass tight to Race Point through late spring have thinned out and scattered east toward deeper, cooler water off the backside. I was out there Monday on the last of the ebb, working the rip where it breaks over the bar, and instead of the tight bait balls we had a few weeks back, it was patchy — small pockets here and there, enough to draw fish but not enough to hold a school in one spot for long. We picked up three bass in the low twenties on live mackerel fished on a fish-finder rig, twenty to twenty-five feet down, letting the current carry the bait naturally through the rip face. Slow going, but quality fish, and every one of them came right on the edge where the moving water meets the slower inside water — that seam is still the money spot, tide stage doesn't matter as much as finding where that color change and current break line up.

Peaked Hill Bars has been more about bluefish than stripers the last two weeks. Mid-morning, an hour either side of the tide change, we've run into surface blitzes — blues in the six-to-ten-pound class ripping through pogie schools, water churned white for a hundred yards. Topwater poppers and metal-lip swimmers thrown right into the chaos will get bit on almost every cast when it's happening, but it's not an all-day thing. It flares up, burns hot for twenty minutes, then shuts off just as fast when the bait scatters or sounds. If you see the birds working there, don't wait — get on it, because it won't last.

Sandy Neck and the mouth of Barnstable Harbor have been the more consistent producer for anyone who doesn't want to chase a moving bite. Fluke fishing there has held steady on the sand-to-grass edges in twelve to twenty feet, drifting white Gulp on quarter-ounce jigheads with a strip of squid for scent. Nothing huge — most fish going fifteen to eighteen inches with a few keepers mixed in — but it's a bite you can count on through the middle of the day when the sun's high and the bigger fish elsewhere have gone quiet. Best water's been on the last two hours of the incoming, when the tide's pushing bait up onto the flat edges and the fluke are sitting up to intercept it.

Cod is where I have to level with people. The shoals inside the bay — Billingsgate, the humps off Wellfleet — just don't hold cod once the water warms through July like it has. Those fish have pushed off to deeper structure, and inside the bay proper you're not going to find a real cod bite until the fall cools things back down. If someone's dead set on cod right now, I'd point them toward the deeper edges out past the bay mouth, not waste a summer morning jigging the shallow humps hoping for something that's already left.

Tide-wise, we're building toward the new moon, and that bigger swing should push more water through the rips at Race Point and give the bait somewhere to concentrate again instead of drifting loose. If that happens, I'd expect the mackerel to tighten back up and pull the bass with them — that's the pattern I've watched play out for thirty years out here, big tides reorganize the bait and the fish follow. Doesn't always happen exactly on schedule, but it's worth being out there on the days around the new moon to find out.

For the next several days, my plan is simple: mornings on Billingsgate over the flood for the bigger bottom-hugging bass, middle of the day on the Sandy Neck flats for a reliable fluke pick, and I'll keep an eye on the birds off Peaked Hill in case the blues fire up again. If the tide swing does what I think it'll do, Race Point should tighten back up by the weekend and be worth another run at the rip. Bring more bait than you think you need — pogies and mackerel have both been a little harder to net clean this month, and a dry well will end your day faster than any tide change will.
