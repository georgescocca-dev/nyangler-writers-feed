# Sea Bright Rocks at First Light: Blues Chewing, First Bass Whispers
*The bluefish are keeping the jetty rats honest while the first teaser stripers start following bait into the wash.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 31** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Dawn patrol this week has a familiar late-August look to it — six, eight guys spread across the Sea Bright rocks and the Manasquan jetty before the sun clears the horizon, most of them throwing metal. Ava 17s, Kastmasters, the occasional guy still married to a needlefish out of habit more than results. The blues have been the honest fish. Cocktails to four pounds, some pushing five, chopping bunker pods that have been sliding down from Raritan Bay and settling along the beachfront from Monmouth Beach through Sea Bright on the outgoing tide. That bunker push is the thing to watch right now — when those schools get thick and pinned against the beach, that's usually the flare that lights the real fall bass run. We're not there yet, but the smoke's starting to show.

Ocean temps offshore are sitting around 71 degrees with a modest three-to-four-foot swell out of the south, which has kept the surf a little lumpy but not unfishable — just enough push to keep the white water working the front of the rocks at Sea Bright and Spring Lake without turning it into a washing machine. Inside the inlets it's calmer and that's where the fluke guys have been putting in the hours. Tides at Manasquan and Shark River are running normal late-summer cycles, and the better window for everything right now is that last hour of the outgoing into dead low, when bait gets pinned against structure and everything with fins lines up to eat.

We flagged this last week — said to expect steady but unspectacular blues and fluke through early September before any real striper signal shows, and that's exactly the shape of it right now. Fluke fishing has softened the way it always does this time of year. Warm water in Shark River and up in the Manasquan back bays is pushing the better fish out toward the inlets and the ocean-front sod banks looking for cleaner, cooler water. The bite isn't dead, it's just concentrated. Channel edges on the outside of the Manasquan Inlet rock piles, working four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails dragged slow along the bottom, have been the most consistent producers — fish to four pounds, nothing huge, but enough keepers mixed with shorts to make the drift worth running on a falling tide. Shark River Inlet has been similar, better on the last two hours of the ebb when the current pulls bait out past the Belmar side.

Bluefish remain the fish you can count on. They're stacked on bunker from Sea Bright down through Asbury Park, and the jetty game has been simple — get there before first light, throw something big and loud, and expect company. Poppers and bunker-pattern swimmers worked over white water at the tip of the Sea Bright rocks have drawn violent strikes at gray light, tapering off hard once the sun gets up over the dunes. Spring Lake and Belmar jetties have shown the same pattern on the right tide stage, generally the first two hours of the incoming when bait gets pushed up tight against the rocks. Bring pliers you don't mind losing — this bunch has been chewing through leader material like it's nothing.

Stripers are still the whisper, not the shout. A handful of schoolies and a few teeners have started showing at last light and first light around the Manasquan jetty and the Shark River Inlet rocks, working the edges of bluefish activity and picking off stragglers out of the bunker chop. Nothing to build a trip around yet, but it's notable — this is usually how it starts. Guys throwing bunker spooks and darters in the low light are picking up the occasional fish, and a couple of reports out of the Sea Bright stretch mention bass following bunker pods right into the wash on a north wind. If that Raritan Bay bunker keeps building and starts sliding south in real numbers over the next week or two, that's when this shifts from whisper to blitz — the moon's building toward full, tidal flow is picking up, and that combination is usually what flips the switch on the first real fall push.

Porgies have been a quiet, dependable side dish for anyone fishing bait rigs off the Manasquan Inlet rocks and the Shark River Inlet bridge — bloodworms and clam on standard porgy rigs in 15 to 20 feet, better on the tail end of the incoming. Nothing exciting, but a good way to fill a cooler while waiting on the bass to show real interest.

Looking at the next few days, expect more of the same shape: blues on the beachfront early, fluke holding tight to inlet structure and channel edges, and stripers staying in that tantalizing, not-quite-there mode. If you've only got one morning to spend, put it at first light on an outgoing tide at either the Manasquan jetty or the Sea Bright rocks, work bunker-imitation plugs through the wash, and don't be shocked if a bass shows up in the middle of a blitz. The albie run working its way west out of Rhode Island hasn't reached this stretch yet, but keep an eye on the horizon toward Shinnecock and the eastern Sound over the next week or two — when that shows, it tends to arrive here not long after.
