# SW Ledge Slack at 9:40 — Bass Down Deep, Doormats Up Top, Bonito Still a Ghost
*The current window is shrinking into the neap, the fluke bite is peaking on the rips, and the first albies of the year are close but not here yet.*

**GALILEE, RI — August 3** — _by Ava Thomas, Zone Writer_

Slack at SW Ledge hit right around 9:40 this morning, and I had exactly eleven minutes before the new push started stacking bait against the rock. That's the game out there right now — short windows, hard current, and you either time it or you waste a tank of gas marking fish you can't get bucktails down to. We're running into the neap on August 5-6, and you can already feel the ledge softening a half-hour earlier than it was two weeks ago. That matters more than people think, because SW Ledge doesn't fish on a clock, it fishes on how much water is actually moving across that 35-to-55-foot structure. Less push means less bait getting pinned, means the bass get lazier about showing themselves in daylight.

And that's exactly the pattern I've been telling clients to expect. We flagged this a few days back — said that as the tides softened, the bass would compress into the strongest current hours and pull onto the deepest, coolest structure, with night eels at the Gut and the bridges becoming the move. That's precisely what's happening. Daytime trips at the Ledge are producing, but you're working for it — jigging diamond jigs and 2-ounce bucktails tight to bottom, mixing in the occasional live scup when we can net one off the rockpile. The fish are there, holding on the down-current side of the boulders in 40 to 50 feet, but they want it presented slow and dragged, not ripped. Once the sun drops and that current builds back through the evening tide, the same fish move up onto the flat and you can work bunker chunks or eels on a light three-way rig without needing to be a surgeon about depth control. That's been our best two hours of the week, hands down — 7:30 to 9:30 in the evening, stripers to 34 inches, a couple pushing 20 pounds, all on fresh-cut bunker fished just off bottom.

Sandy Point at the south end of the island is still the most consistent bass water we've got after dark. The rip there doesn't care about neap tides the way the Ledge does — it's got its own current signature off the tip, and it's been holding fish tight to the drop where the sand gives way to deeper water. Eels drifted through that seam on the last two hours of the outgoing have been steady, nothing giant, mostly 26 to 32 inches, but consistent enough that I'd call it the safer bet if you only get one shot at a night trip this week.

Daytime, fluke's been the better story, honestly. We're sitting right in the middle of the peak window for doormat-class fish, and Block Island's channel edges and rip lines are holding up their end. Best fish of the week came off the edge of the rip running north of North Light — a six-and-a-half-pound flattie on a white Gulp Swimming Mullet paired with a 3/4-ounce bucktail, worked slow on the drift in about 28 feet. The pattern's simple: find where the sand meets structure, drift it with the tide instead of the wind, and keep contact with bottom without dragging bucktail through the rocks. We've had multiple keeper drifts a day out there, mixed with a healthy number of shorts you've got to sort through, which is normal for this time of year but does slow you down some.

Sea bass have stayed dependable on the same structure — the wrecks and rock piles off Point Judith and the deeper reef pockets south of the island keep coughing up 14-to-17-inch fish on squid strips and Gulp on high-low rigs. Nothing spectacular, but it's the kind of bite you can lean on when the bass window doesn't line up with your trip.

Now, the question everyone's asking at the dock: where are the bonito? Not here yet, not in any real numbers. I've had a couple of quick, unconfirmed flashes off Clay Head chasing bait on the surface, gone before anyone could get a cast in, and that's about it. The water's warming into the right range for it — surface temps pushing into the mid-70s regionally, and that's exactly the kind of break that usually kicks the first scout fish loose off Block Island. Given how things have lined up the last few seasons, I'd expect the real scattered reports to start within the next week to ten days, likely showing first off the southeast corner near Clay Head or out around the East Grounds where the current stacks bait against the drop. If you're itching to get ahead of it, keep small epoxy jigs and Deadly Dicks rigged and ready, because when they show, they show fast and don't stick around long enough for you to re-tackle.

Looking ahead, I'd play the neap the way it wants to be played — lean on the low-light bass bite at Sandy Point and the Ledge rather than fighting slack water in daylight, keep the fluke gear rigged for the rip lines while that bite is still hot, and start checking the surface off Clay Head every trip out, because that first bonito of the season doesn't announce itself with a phone call.
