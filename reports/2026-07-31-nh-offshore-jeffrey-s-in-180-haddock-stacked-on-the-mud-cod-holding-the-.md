# Jeffrey's in 180: haddock stacked on the mud, cod holding the gravel edge, first bluefin breaking on Stellwagen's approach
*A steady week of numbers fishing on the Ledge, with early bluefin scouting the bait balls off the southern end.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — July 31** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

One-eighty feet on the sounder, soft mud from the transducer down to the bottom, and the haddock marks stacked up like a layer cake eight to twelve feet off bottom. That's been my number all week on the flats north of the Ledge proper, working the mud basin between Jeffrey's and the edge of Scantum. Haddock this time of year want that soft bottom and they want it in depth — anywhere from 165 to 195 feet has been holding fish, and the good marks come up thick right around the top of the tide when the current slacks enough to hold a straight drift.

The rig hasn't changed and it doesn't need to: high-low bottom rigs, size 3/0 circle hooks, clam bellies or squid strips, six to ten ounces of lead depending on drift speed. When the current's really moving — and with the tide building back toward the August 12th new moon it has been moving — I've had to go to twelve ounces just to hold bottom on some drifts. Haddock have run a clean 20 to 26 inches, with enough shorts mixed in that you're culling through fish, which is a good sign for the fall. Limits haven't been automatic but a determined boat working the mud has had no trouble putting a solid box together in three or four hours.

Cod is a different conversation, and it's a gravel conversation. The edge where the mud gives way to hard bottom and boulder — that transition zone running along the western flank of the Ledge in 90 to 140 feet — is where the cod have been sitting tight to structure. This is jig water more than bait water right now. Six and eight-ounce diamond jigs, tipped with a strip of squid, worked with a short hard snap off bottom, has been outproducing static bait rigs two or three to one when the fish are actively feeding. When they're not, a whole clam on a circle hook soaking right on the gravel will still pick off a few keepers. Sizes have been mixed — a lot of 24 to 30 inch fish with the occasional 15-pounder mixed in, usually right on the steepest part of the drop where the gravel rolls into deeper water.

Pollock have been riding shotgun on both bites, which is typical for this time of year — they sit up off the bottom over the same structure holding cod and haddock, and they'll jump all over a diamond jig worked on the fall rather than the bottom bounce. If you're marking fish suspended 20 to 40 feet up off a gravel pile, that's pollock water, and a faster retrieve with the jig will separate them from the cod underneath.

Hake fishing has quieted some compared to June, but the basin still produces after dark. Scantum's deep water — 250 feet plus — has had a few boats working chum bags and diamond jigs tipped with squid at night with decent results, though it's not the numbers game it was a few weeks back. If you want hake specifically, plan a late-evening trip and be patient; it's a slower bite but the fish run good size when they show.

Now the part everyone's been asking about at the dock — bluefin. We flagged this last week, said the pattern would hold through the week, and it has. The warm surface push that's been running unusually far north for late July has kept a mackerel and pogy show working the approaches to Stellwagen, and where there's bait stacked under whale activity, there's tuna working underneath it. I've had reports of school and medium bluefin breaking on bait balls south of the Ledge, working the same warm edge that's been feeding the canyon bite further south. This isn't the giant push yet — that's an October program on the Ledge proper, when the mackerel schools get dense and the big fish move in tight to feed hard before the water turns. Right now it's scouting fish, singles and small pods, working the surface in the morning glass and disappearing once the wind builds a chop. Trolling small spreads of green machines and bird rigs around visible bait and diving birds has connected with a few, but it's not a program you can set your watch to yet.

Mackerel themselves have been reliable bait fish, schooling up in that 30 to 60 foot range on structure edges, and a Sabiki rig dropped through a school will fill a livewell in ten minutes most mornings. That's been the ticket for guys wanting live bait insurance if a bluefin shows itself.

Looking ahead, I don't expect much to change through the next several days. The tide keeps building toward the new moon, which should sharpen up the drifts on both the mud and the gravel and probably push haddock a little tighter to the bottom during the strongest push. If the bait holds on the southern approaches to Stellwagen, I'd expect the bluefin scouting reports to keep trickling in, but I wouldn't plan a trip around them yet — plan it around the mud and the gravel, where the numbers have been dependable, and treat any tuna sign as a bonus.
