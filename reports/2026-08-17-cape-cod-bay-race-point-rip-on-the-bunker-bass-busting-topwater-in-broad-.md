# Race Point Rip on the Bunker: Bass Busting Topwater in Broad Daylight
*Humpbacks are marking the bait for us this week, and the stripers haven't needed the low light to feed.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — August 17** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

Monday morning, half tide, I had the boat idling off Race Point waiting on a pot of coffee to cool when a humpback came up forty yards off the port rail and blew a breath you could smell. Didn't even flinch the guy in the bow seat — he'd already seen three whales working that same stretch of water since sunup. Where the whales are punching through bait balls, the bass are usually right underneath cleaning up what gets missed, and that's exactly what we found. Peanut bunker balled up thick over the deeper water off the Point, and forty yards inside them, stripers busting on top at ten in the morning like it was dusk. That's not normal for August in the Bay — usually this time of year the fish tuck into low light and you're grinding dawn patrol. But with bunker stacked this dense, the bass are overriding the sun and feeding whenever the bait's there for the taking.

We flagged this pattern building last week — said the blitz would hold as long as the bunker schools stayed in the Bay, and here it is, still running strong into the third week of August. Race Point rip has been the epicenter. On the last two hours of the flood, we're drifting the edge in 30 to 50 feet, marking bait thick on the meter, and working live mackerel on a fish-finder rig or an umbrella rig with tubes when the fish are deeper in the column. When they come up and start showing on top, that's when you put the spinning rods down and pick up something you can throw — 9-inch Slug-Go in bone or albino, or a topwater popper like a Guppy or a Gibbs pencil, worked with some hesitation in the retrieve so it looks hurt. Fish have been running mostly schoolie to mid-20-inch class, but we've pulled a few over-slot fish, low 30s, out of the deeper edge of the rip where the current really rips through on that flood stage.

Peaked Hill Bars has been holding its own version of the same show. That stretch of shoal water gets skinny fast, and with the bunker pushing through, bass are pinning bait against the bar itself. Best window there has been the last of the outgoing into dead low, when the structure funnels everything moving through it into a tighter lane. Walking the edge with soft plastics on a light jighead, letting it swing with the current rather than horsing it back, has out-produced straight retrieves. Bluefish have been mixed into almost every one of these blitzes too — mostly 3 to 6 pounds, plenty of teeth marks on the plugs to prove it. If you're not okay losing a lure now and then, tie on wire or expect to re-rig.

Down toward Billingsgate, the shoals are holding structure-oriented bass and a heavier concentration of bluefish than we've seen up at the Point. That sunken island complex has always been a magnet for bait getting pushed around by tide, and this week's been no different — bunker stacking on the flats at the edges, blues working them hard on the last two hours of the flood. Live-lining a bunker on a stinger rig has been the most consistent producer there, though metal — a Deadly Dick or a Kastmaster in the 2-ounce range — will get bit just as fast when they're up top. Snapper blues, meanwhile, are thick in the back creeks and estuary mouths around Barnstable and Brewster, exactly the kind of building block we said to watch for a couple weeks back — small fish now, but that's usually a sign the adult class keeps filling in behind them as the month runs out.

Sandy Neck has been the better bet if you want a mixed bag without chasing blitzes. Fluke fishing on the flats there has been steady, not spectacular — working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails on the incoming has put keepers in the box, mostly 16 to 19 inches, with the occasional fish pushing 4 pounds. Striper activity along that beach has been more blitz-driven too, same bunker pattern showing up tight to the sand on calm mornings.

One oddity worth mentioning — there was a sailfish spotted just outside the Bay off Chatham this week, which tells you the water's running warmer and stranger than typical for mid-August. I wouldn't build a trip around chasing something like that, but it's worth keeping an eye out for anything unusual showing up in your spread or your chum slick.

Cod fishing has stayed quiet, and I'm not expecting that to change soon. The deeper structure off Billingsgate and out toward the Truro grounds needs the water to cool off before those fish move back onto it in numbers — that's typically a late-September into October pattern for us, and this year, with the water running warm the way it has, I'd guess it slides even later than usual.

Tides are building back toward the August 28 full moon, which should tighten up the current on both Race Point and Peaked Hill Bars and could sharpen the bite even further, assuming the bunker schools hold their ground. The one thing that would break this pattern fast is a hard wind shift pushing that bait off the beach and out of the Bay — worth watching the forecast before you commit to a full day on the water. If it holds calm through the weekend, I'd be back out on that flood tide off the Point with a scoop of mackerel and a topwater rod within reach.
