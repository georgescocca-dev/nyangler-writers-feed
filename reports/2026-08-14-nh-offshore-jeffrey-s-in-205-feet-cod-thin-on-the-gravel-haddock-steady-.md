# Jeffrey's in 205 feet: cod thin on the gravel, haddock steady over the mud
*Groundfish stayed textbook for mid-August while the first real bait pods showed up topside on the ledge — a preview of what October usually brings.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 14** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Two-oh-five feet on the sounder this morning, working the northeast corner of Jeffrey's Ledge where the gravel kicks up hard against the mud line. That transition is everything out here. Drop a jig six feet off bottom on the gravel side and you're fishing for cod. Drift twenty yards west onto the softer mud and you're suddenly in haddock country. Same tide, same boat, two different fisheries separated by a bottom change you can watch happen on the chart plotter.

Cod have been thin but honest this week — not a stacked bite, but a real one. We've been picking fish to 8 and 10 pounds on the gravel edges in 190 to 220 feet, jigging 12-ounce diamond jigs tipped with a strip of squid on the drop. The key has been working the up-current side of the humps where the gravel piles into little ridges — cod sit tight to that structure and won't chase far, so short, sharp jig strokes right on bottom beat a big sweeping retrieve every time. Water at depth is still holding in the mid-60s, which is right where you want it for this fishery to stay honest through late summer. No complaints there, but no gangbusters day either — figure one keeper cod for every three or four you handle, with a lot of shorts mixed in that need to go back quick.

Haddock have actually been the more consistent player over the softer bottom toward Scantum Basin. Standard rig — a high-low with clam bellies or squid strips — fished right on the mud in 180 to 200 feet has been putting steady fish in the box, most running a pound and a half to three pounds, with the occasional slob pushing five. Haddock don't care much about current the way cod do; they sit and feed on that mud almost lazily, so a slow drift with light chum in the water has out-produced anything aggressive. If you're marking soft, rolling marks a few feet off bottom on the machine, that's your school — don't chase them, let the drift bring you back through.

Pollock have been mixed in on both bottoms, which is normal for this time of year, and we've picked a few nice hake off the mud edges on bigger, slower-worked jigs after dark on evening trips. Mackerel showed decent early in the week off Cape Ann on the way out, enough to load a bait tank without burning much time, though they've thinned some as the week's gone on — worth checking before you commit a whole trip to jigging bait, but don't count on it being a sure thing every morning.

The bigger story this week, honestly, is what's happening on top of the water rather than what's coming off the bottom. We've started seeing real bait pods building over Jeffrey's — tight balls of sand eels and pogies pushing up on the surface, gulls working hard, and a few boats reporting single bluefin showing on top without much commitment. That's the pattern I watch for every August. It's early yet — the real giant bluefin push on this ledge doesn't typically load in until October, when the bait gets dense and the fish start setting up to feed hard before they push offshore — but this kind of bait buildup in mid-August is exactly the setup that leads into it. Worth keeping an eye on if you're planning tuna trips later in the season; I wouldn't burn fuel chasing singles right now, but I'd note where the bait's concentrating, because that's usually where the giants show up in six to eight weeks.

We said last week to expect steady conditions with no major shifts through this stretch, and that's exactly how it played out — no fronts came through, no water temp swings, and the gravel-mud split held its pattern all week without disruption. That kind of predictability is actually good news for planning trips; when the pattern's steady, you can fish it with confidence instead of guessing.

Tide-wise, we're working through the cycle a few days past the new moon, and the stronger exchanges have kept bait moving and marks a little more spread than they'd be on a slack neap week. That's part of why the haddock bite on the mud has stayed more consistent than the cod bite on the gravel — haddock tolerate that current better and hold position, while cod get pickier about exactly where they'll sit when the water's really moving.

Looking ahead, if the bait pods keep building over the ledge and the mid-60s water holds at depth, I'd expect the groundfish pattern to stay right where it is — steady haddock on the mud, honest but not heavy cod on the gravel. Platts Bank and Tillies Bank are both worth a look if Jeffrey's gets crowded or the drift isn't setting up right; both have been holding similar mixed haddock and pollock action on their respective mud and gravel patches. I'd keep a longer look toward Cape Ann and Thatcher Island for weather windows, since the next stretch of the season is really about picking clean days to get out and watch that bait build — because when it finally tightens up in earnest, that's when this ledge turns into a different fishery altogether.
