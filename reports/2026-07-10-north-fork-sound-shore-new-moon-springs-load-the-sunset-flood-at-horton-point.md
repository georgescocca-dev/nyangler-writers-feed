# New moon springs load the sunset flood at Horton Point
*Bigger tides are stacking bait against the rocks just as the light goes down — the bass know it, even if the weakfish still don't.*

**MATTITUCK, NY — July 10** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

Last week the moon was working its way down toward new, and you could feel the tides building a little more push each day. Wind sat out of the northeast more than not, which is the wind that does us favors on this shore — it stacks water against Horton Point and Rocky Point instead of blowing everything flat and dead. The Sound stayed steady, not hot, not cold, just holding — the kind of stable water that doesn't spook anything but doesn't light a fire under it either. Meanwhile the bay side down toward the Peconics got up into the high 70s and pushed the fluke out of the back creeks and into the inlets and the open Sound, which is exactly the kind of thing that fills Mattituck Inlet with fish that used to be somebody else's problem.

This week the moon goes new on the 11th, and that means spring tides — bigger swings, more water moving, more current wrapping the structure. The piece that matters most for us: the PM flood is lining up with sunset now, first time it's done that since the June full moon. Peak alignment runs the 11th through the 13th. That's not a small thing on this shore. When the flood and the last hour of light hit together, the bait gets squeezed up against the rocks at Horton Point and Rocky Point right when the stripers want to feed anyway, low light, moving water, bait with nowhere to go. That's the window I've been waiting on since the solstice.

Fluke first, because that's been the most honest bite of the last ten days. Mattituck Inlet on the outgoing has been holding fish stacked up where the channel narrows down by the jetty rocks — three-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom, working the seam where the sandy bottom drops into the deeper cut. Nothing spectacular, mostly 16 to 19 inches with the occasional fish pushing 21, but it's been steady enough that a couple hours on the right tide puts dinner in the cooler. The push out of the warmer bay water is real — those fish didn't leave home because they wanted to, they left because it got uncomfortable back there, and the cooler upwelled water sitting out front of the inlet is where they're settling. Fish it on the last two hours of the outgoing, right before dead low, and don't be afraid to slow your drift down to almost nothing.

Stripers have been a mixed bag, which is the honest way to put it. Some evenings at Horton Point you'll find them working the boulder field on a strong flood, other evenings you could soak an eel for an hour and never get a sniff. What I've noticed is it's all about that current — when the water's really moving, the bass tuck in behind the bigger rocks and ambush whatever gets swept past. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig right in that current break have been drawing the better fish, mid-20s to low 30-inch class, nothing enormous but solid keeper-range fish. Guys throwing swimming plugs — the bigger Danny-style baits — through the same water at last light have had shots at fish too, but you need to be working the exact seam, not just chucking and hoping. With the springs building toward that Saturday-Sunday-Monday peak, I expect this bite to sharpen up rather than fall off. More water moving through means more bait getting displaced, and displaced bait means feeding fish. If I only had one evening to fish this week, I'd take Saturday's sunset flood at Rocky Point and not think twice about it.

Bluefish have started showing in better numbers out toward the Plum Gut approach, which tracks with what I'd expect this time of year — that funnel of water moving between the Gut and the open Sound concentrates bait, and blues are opportunists first and always. Trolling small spoons or casting metal — Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — into the rip lines off Orient Point has been producing fish in the 3 to 6 pound range, choppy and aggressive, cutting off leaders if you're not running wire or heavy fluoro. It's not a blitz, but it's consistent enough that if you want a bent rod and don't care what species is on the other end, that's your play.

Porgy fishing off the drop at Orient Point has quietly been some of the better action on the beat — bottom rigs with sandworm or clam, fished in 20 to 30 feet where the bottom breaks toward the Gut, has been putting decent-sized scup in the bucket. It's not glamorous fishing but it's honest fishing, and on a slow bass evening it's a good fallback.

Now the disappointing part, and I'll be straight with you because that's the only way I know how to write this column: weakfish are still a no-show. Water's been plenty warm long enough now that if temperature alone triggered them, we'd have heard something by now. We haven't. I talked to enough people up and down this shore to say with some confidence that warm water is necessary but it isn't sufficient — there's something else weakfish need that just isn't here yet, or isn't here at all this year. I wouldn't burn a tide targeting them specifically. Fish for bass or fluke and if a weakfish shows up, treat it like a bonus, not a plan.

Looking ahead — the new moon springs peaking the 11th through 13th is the story of the week for this shore. IF the northeast wind holds and doesn't swing around and blow onshore hard, the sunset flood at Horton Point and Rocky Point should be the best striper window we've had since the full moon in June. I'd fish the last two hours of daylight into the start of dark, bunker chunks or a swimming plug worked tight to the rocks. Plan B, if the bass don't cooperate, is the Mattituck Inlet fluke drift on the outgoing — that bite has been dependable enough to lean on. Either way, bigger water's coming through this week, and bigger water on this shore usually means something's willing to eat.
