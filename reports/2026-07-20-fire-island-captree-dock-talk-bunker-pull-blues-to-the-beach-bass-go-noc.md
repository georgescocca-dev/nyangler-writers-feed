# Captree Dock Talk: Bunker Pull Blues to the Beach, Bass Go Nocturnal
*Fluke keep grinding on the bridge rips while stripers wait for dark and bluefish crash bunker pods right off the barrier beach.*

**CAPTREE, NY — July 20** — _by Mariana "Mari" Reyes, Zone Writer_

Had a guy leaning on my gunwale at the Captree dock Tuesday morning, coffee in one hand, asking why his buddy killed it on bass at 5am off Democrat Point and he got skunked at noon in the same spot. I told him what I've been telling everybody all week — you're fishing the wrong clock. Right now, this bay and this inlet run on two different schedules, and if you're not paying attention to which one you're fishing, you're going to burn a lot of gas for nothing.

Here's the deal. The ocean's been ugly — short, stacked-up swell, 2 to 3 feet at a nasty 3-second period, the kind of chop that makes the ocean side of Fire Island feel like a washing machine instead of a beach. NY Bight buoy's reading mid-70s, and that warm, wind-whipped water has pushed most of the good feeding activity off the open front and into the protected seams — the inlet channels, the bridge rips, the back bay flats where the water lays down and the current still does something useful. If you're planning a Democrat Point surf session this week, know you're fighting the ocean as much as you're fishing it.

Stripers have noticed the heat too. Water in the bay's holding solidly in the low-to-mid 70s, and that's put the bass into a low-light, mostly-after-dark pattern — same story from here to the Sound. Daytime bucktailing on the inlet seam has been a grind; the better move has been live-lining bunker after dark around structure — the Fire Island Inlet rocks, the pilings under the Robert Moses bridges, and the deep hole off the Coast Guard station. Guys running bunker on a fish-finder rig, fresh snagged and dropped right on the current break at the top of the outgoing, are still finding slot fish and the occasional overslot in that 32–36 inch range. Daytime, don't waste your good bait — save it for dusk and go park on structure.

Fluke, on the other hand, don't care what time it is, and that's been the most dependable bite in the whole zone. The channel edges around the Robert Moses bridges — both the Democrat Point side and out toward the Causeway pilings — have been holding fish all week in 25 to 35 feet, with the better drift coming on the last two hours of the outgoing and the first hour of the incoming when the current's still got some shape to it. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, have been the ticket, along with chartreuse bucktail-and-Gulp combos when the water's got a little color to it after a blow. Most of what's coming over the rail is in that 16 to 19 inch range with a keeper mixed in every few drifts — not a slam-bang bite, but steady enough that a patient boat puts fish in the box most days. Great South Bay flats off Nicoll Bay and out past the Sayville flats have been holding shorts in good numbers too, so if you're chasing a legal fish for the table, work the deeper edges, not the flats themselves.

Bluefish have been the loudest story on the beach this week, and we called this one — said the bunker-driven blitz pattern would keep spreading down the coast as long as the weather stayed calm, and that's exactly what's played out along the barrier beach. Bunker pods are running tight to the sand from Cherry Grove down past Kismet, and when the blues find them, it's fast and violent — poppers and metal getting hit the second they land. If you see the birds working and the water nervous, don't overthink the presentation — a 2-ounce diamond jig or a topwater popper thrown right into the boil will get bit in seconds. It's not an all-day thing, though; these blitzes are running in short, intense windows, usually first light and again right before dark, so don't plan your whole trip around chasing one pod for six hours.

Weakfish have been quiet but not dead. A few nice fish, some pushing 3 to 4 pounds, have come out of Patchogue and Sayville on warm, still evenings — the classic pattern for this fish, a soft-plastic worm imitation like a Bass Assassin or a bucktail-and-plastic combo worked slow along the deeper bay bottom right at last light. It's not a bite you can plan a trip around yet, but on the calmest, warmest nights it's been showing signs of rebuilding, and I'd keep half an eye on it heading into next week.

Looking ahead — we hit the first-quarter neap on the 21st, which should take some of the snap out of the current at the inlet and the bridge rips for the next several days. That usually means a wider window to fish moving water instead of a tight peak-tide sprint, which is good news for fluke guys who like a slower, more controlled drift. If the ocean lays down at all behind this pattern, I'd take a look at the outer beach for one more crack at those bunker-driven blitzes before the bait pushes further down the coast. And if you've only got one shot this week, I'd take it after dark on the inlet with a live bunker — that's where the bigger bass have been living, and that's not likely to change until this heat breaks.
