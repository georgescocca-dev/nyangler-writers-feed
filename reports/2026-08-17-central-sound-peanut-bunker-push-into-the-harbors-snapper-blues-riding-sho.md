# Peanut Bunker Push Into the Harbors — Snapper Blues Riding Shotgun Behind Them
*The bait's telling the story before the boats do: pogies stacking in the back bays, adult bluefish trailing them west to east, and porgies still the sure thing on the reefs.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — August 17** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spent Tuesday morning at the mouth of a harbor I won't name — not because it's some secret, but because half the guys reading this already fish it — just watching. Not casting. Watching. There's a difference, and this time of year it pays off more than the first hour of blind chucking ever does. What I saw was peanut bunker, thick as smoke, pinned against a bulkhead by the tide, and snapper blues working them from underneath in short, nervous rushes. Terns were up over the mouth doing the twitchy little dips that tell you the bait's getting pushed, not just schooling for fun. That's late-August behavior right on schedule, and it's the same pattern I flagged a couple weeks back — snapper blues thick in the back bays as a tell that the adult class would keep building. Here it is, showing up right where we said it would.

The water's holding warm and stable across the Central Sound — Kings Point reading just under 71, New London a bit cooler at 69.4 — and that west-to-east gradient matters more than people give it credit for. It means the bait's tighter and more concentrated on the western half of my beat right now, and the bigger bluefish pushing in behind the peanut schools are following that warmth east at their own pace. I'd expect that adult bluefish presence to keep spreading toward Port Jeff and the Norwalk Islands over the next week or two as the gradient levels out, which lines up with what we called for the zone the other day — the bluefish resurgence spreading east from the Western Sound's reef structure. It's happening, just don't expect it to hit every spot on the same day.

Striper fishing has been the toughest piece of the puzzle, and I'll be straight about it — this warm, stable water has compressed the bite into a tight window. First light and last light, that's it. I worked the Stratford Shoal rip before sunrise Thursday on the last of the ebb and had two fish in the mid-20s on a white 1-ounce bucktail dressed with a chunk of pork rind, worked slow along the bottom where the current breaks over the shoal. By 6:30 the sun was up, the bite was over, and I didn't see another fish the rest of the morning. Same story at Middle Ground Light — a couple of guys anchored up there Friday told me they had fish on topwater poppers for maybe forty minutes right at gray light, then nothing. If you're chasing bass right now, set your alarm and plan your trip around the tide change at dawn, not around convenience.

Porgies and scup remain the honest bite on this beat, and honestly, they've been the honest bite all summer. Charles Island and the rockpiles off the Norwalk Islands are still stacked with them — hi-lo rigs, size 6 or 4 hooks, sandworm or clam belly, fished on the bottom in 15 to 30 feet on the last two hours of the incoming tide. It's not glamorous fishing but it's reliable, and with the afternoon thunderstorms we've been getting rolling through — typical for this pattern, they blow up fast off the heat and clear out just as quick — those brief post-front windows have actually produced some of the better porgy counts of the week. Fish seem to turn on for thirty or forty minutes right after the storm passes and the barometer starts climbing again.

Fluke are still mostly a disappointment inside the Sound proper — plenty of action, not much size. I've been picking through a dozen shorts for every keeper drifting bucktails tipped with Gulp off the drop-offs near Port Jefferson Harbor and the deeper water off Middle Ground. If you want a real shot at a keeper, work the edges where the bottom breaks hard, 25 to 35 feet, and fish it slow on the outgoing when the current pulls bait off the flats into the channel. That's where the bigger fish have been sitting, waiting rather than hunting — classic warm-water fluke behavior.

Blackfish aren't in the story yet. I checked a few of the rockier stretches off the Norwalk Islands out of curiosity and didn't get so much as a nibble worth mentioning. That's expected — tautog interest on structure usually doesn't build until we're closer to Labor Day, and we're not there. Worth filing away for three weeks from now, not this week.

Weakfish have been a ghost — I haven't had a credible report of one on this stretch of the Sound all month, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

Looking ahead, the tides are rebuilding toward the August 28 full moon, and that extra push should sharpen up both the bass bite at dawn and the current-driven bait concentration everywhere from the Norwalk Islands to Stratford Shoal. If the bunker keep getting corralled into the harbor mouths the way they were this week, I'd bet on the adult bluefish class showing up in bigger numbers along the Connecticut shore by the time we get there. My plan for next week: first-light bass at the Shoal, then switch over to porgies on the rockpiles once the sun's up and the bass shut down. That's the rhythm this water's asking for right now — work the low light hard, then settle into the sure thing.
