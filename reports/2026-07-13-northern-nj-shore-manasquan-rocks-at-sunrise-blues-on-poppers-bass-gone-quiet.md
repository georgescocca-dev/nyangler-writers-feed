# Manasquan Rocks at Sunrise: Blues on Poppers, Bass Gone Quiet
*The bass have slid off into deep, cooler water for the summer — but the bluefish and fluke are keeping the jetty crowd honest.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 13** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Tommy Bianco was already out on the Manasquan Inlet north jetty when I pulled into the lot at 5:15 Tuesday, working a black Danny swimmer over the first rock pile like he owed the ocean money. He'd been at it since first light. Nothing. That's July on this stretch — you fish on habit more than hope, because the calendar says the bass should be somewhere and the water says they're not here.

Let's not dance around it. Striper fishing from Sea Bright down to Manasquan is slow right now, and if somebody tells you different they're either lying or fishing a spot they won't share, which amounts to the same thing. The buoy off the Bight is reading 72.9 degrees, and that's the story in three words — too warm, too shallow, too much sun on the water for resident bass to sit tight on structure all day. What's left of the schoolie population has pushed into deeper, cooler water off the reefs, and the bigger fish that wintered through are mostly gone north or holding on structure in 40-plus feet where we can't get a plug to them from the rocks. The guys still connecting on bass are doing it in that narrow window right at gray light, working bunker spawn or live eels off the Sea Bright reef and the rocks below Monmouth Beach, and even then it's a fish or two, not a blitz. I had one 26-incher on a white bucktail tipped with a chunk of shedder crab off the Sea Bright jetty last Thursday at 5:40 a.m., on the last of the incoming, and by 6:15 the bite was over like someone flipped a switch. That's the pattern. Get there before the sun clears the water, or don't bother.

What's actually paying the gas money right now is bluefish and fluke, and neither one is subtle about it. The bunker schools have been thick off Sea Bright and Long Branch, rolling right along the beach in that skinny water inside the second bar, and when the blues find them it's not a mystery — birds working, water nervous, and every guy on the beach suddenly sprinting for his rod. Bunker-colored poppers and 2-ounce Kastmasters have been the call, worked fast across the top, and these aren't snappers — we've had blues to 8 and 9 pounds crashing bait practically in the wash. The swell's been running about 2 to 3 feet on a long 10-second period, clean enough that you can wade out to your knees on the outer bar at Sea Bright and still get a good cast into moving water without getting worked over.

Fluke has been the more consistent play, and Manasquan and Shark River Inlets are both holding fish right now on the tide changes. Manasquan's been better on the last two hours of the outgoing, drifting the channel edge from the Coast Guard station down toward the jetty tips in 15 to 20 feet, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, bounced slow along bottom. We're not talking gorilla fluke — most are running 16 to 19 inches, with the occasional 20-plus mixed in — but it's steady enough that a couple hours on the right tide will put fish in the cooler. Shark River's mouth has been similar, a little smaller average fish but more consistent numbers, especially working the drop-off just outside the train trestle on the last of the ebb. Squid strips and spearing on a fluke rig behind the bucktail have been outproducing straight bait this week — the extra flash seems to matter when the water's had a little color to it after the swell.

Porgies are around too, and don't sleep on them — the Belmar and Spring Lake jetties have decent schools stacked on the rock piles in 8 to 12 feet, and a bloodworm or clam bait on a simple high-low rig will keep a rod busy while you wait on the tide to turn for something better. It's not glamorous fishing but it's honest fishing, and on a slow bass week that counts for something.

No sign of albies yet, and I wouldn't expect them — that's a late-August, September story on this stretch once the water starts to slide back down and the peanut bunker show up in the wash. Right now we're square in the middle of the summer lull that every jetty rat on this beach knows and nobody particularly enjoys. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd be on the Manasquan jetty an hour before sunrise working a swimmer through the first light window for bass, then pivot to the inlet channel with a bucktail once the sun's up and the fluke tide sets up right — probably Thursday or Friday morning on the outgoing, with the swell holding in that 2-to-3-foot range. Keep an eye on the bunker schools along the beach too; wherever they're thick, the blues won't be far behind, and that's about as close to a guarantee as this stretch of coast is handing out in mid-July.
