# Falkner Island Porgies Load Up While Bass Crash Bunker at the Breakwalls
*Warm water and Harvest Moon tides are stacking bait against the rock — porgies down deep, stripers and blues up top.*

**MILFORD, CT — September 21** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Porgies are piling onto the rockpiles off Falkner Island and the Charles Island bar this week, and the mates working that structure are seeing double-headers on high-low rigs before the sinker even settles. That's the story of the Central Sound right now — two fisheries running at once, one down on the bottom and one exploding on the surface, and both owe their timing to the same thing: a warm, bait-loaded Sound getting stirred hard by the Harvest Moon tides.

Kings Point is reading 70.9 degrees, and while New Haven Harbor doesn't have a clean number to hang on it, the pattern matches what the whole western half of the Sound is doing — water that should be cooling into fall is holding onto summer heat, and it's keeping everything from porgies to fluke around longer than they usually stick. We flagged this two weeks back — said this zone would see incremental improvement week over week as the water eased down — and that's exactly the shape of it. Nothing dramatic, just steady, and this week was better than last.

The bunker pattern is the headline event. Peanut bunker and adult menhaden schools are getting pushed around by the bigger Harvest Moon tides, and striped bass are riding them hard into New Haven Harbor's outer breakwalls, the mouth of the Housatonic at Milford Gulf, and along the beach at Hammonasset. The good sets are happening on the tide changes — last two hours of the ebb and first hour of the new flood — when the current stacks bait against structure instead of letting it spread out. Poppers and 1-ounce metal-lip swimmers worked over visible bunker balls are drawing the vicious strikes, and when the surface goes quiet, a white bucktail with a swim shad dragged just off bottom along the breakwall rocks is still finding fish that dropped under the school. Slot fish are common in these blitzes, with the occasional overslot mixed in tight to the rock where the current cuts hardest.

Bluefish are riding shotgun on every one of these bait balls, and they're getting bigger and meaner as the week goes on — that's the bunker doing its job, funneling bigger fish into range as the moon tides push more forage through the system. If you're throwing topwater at a breakwall blitz and missing hookups on stripers, there's a decent chance a chopper blue just intercepted your plug. Not a bad problem to have.

Down on the structure, the porgy bite has been the most consistent thing going. Falkner Island's rockpiles in 20 to 30 feet and the bar off Charles Island are both loaded, and sea worms or squid strips on a two-hook bottom rig are all it takes. These fish haven't started their fall push out of the Sound yet, and with water this warm, there's no rush for them to leave. Black sea bass are mixing into the same drops, especially out toward Stratford Shoal where the bottom drops into deeper cold pockets — 30 to 40 feet is the sweet spot, small strips of squid or a Gulp swimming mullet on a bucktail teaser working best. Fluke are still around too, which is a pleasant surprise for late September. Milford Gulf and the flats outside New Haven Harbor are giving up keepers on white Gulp Swimming Mullets and bucktails dragged slow on the last of the outgoing — the warm water is the whole reason these fish haven't slid off into deeper water yet, and that clock is ticking the moment a real cold front comes through.

Blackfish are the one piece not quite in play. The rocks off Charles Island and the breakwalls are already holding tog, you can see them stacking on the sonar, but Connecticut's fall season doesn't open until October 10 — so for now it's crab bait, patience, and marking spots for when the gate drops. Worth doing the scouting now while the porgies keep you busy on the same structure.

Looking ahead, the next few days should keep building on this pattern rather than break it. The bunker-driven blitz pattern is expected to intensify as more bait gets flushed south and east with the bigger tides, so the breakwall and river-mouth window should only get better through the weekend — worth being on the water for the tide change rather than the middle of the day. There's also a false albacore push working its way west from Fishers Island that should reach Falkner Island and the outer Sound within the week; if that shows up, small metal jigs like Deadly Dorados or half-ounce Kastmasters cast into surface-feeding albies will be worth having rigged and ready alongside the bunker gear. The one thing to watch is any real cold front — that's what finally shuts down this warm-water fluke and porgy run, and when it hits, the whole zone tightens up fast. Until then, fish the tide changes, work the structure, and don't be surprised if the rod bends before the bait even hits bottom.
