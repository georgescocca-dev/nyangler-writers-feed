# The Cut Off Cupsogue Is Pinching Down — And the Bait Is Stacking In the Throat
*Moriches Inlet's main channel has tightened against the west jetty, concentrating bunker, fluke, and blitzing blues into a narrower, more predictable lane.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — August 3** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

I've been watching the throat of Moriches Inlet narrow for about three weeks now, and this week it's obvious even from the beach at Cupsogue. The main channel that used to run fairly centered between the jetties has pinched against the west rocks — probably 20, 25 yards of lateral shift since the last real blow pushed sand off the ebb shoal. What that does mechanically is simple: same volume of water, tighter cross-section, faster current. On a strong outgoing you can stand on the west jetty now and watch the seam boil where it used to just swirl. That seam is where everything is happening.

Bunker have been pouring through that cut on the last two hours of the ebb, especially around first light, and the bluefish are riding shotgun on them right into the surf line at Cupsogue and down toward Pikes Beach. We flagged this pattern last week — said the bunker push would keep the inlet and beachfront blitzing on low light while fluke stayed steady inside on the channel edges — and that's exactly how it's playing out. Blues from two to six pounds are crashing bunker schools hard enough to throw spray visible from the parking lot at Cupsogue around 5:30 to 6:15 AM. Bucktails and metal — Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters, anything that matches the 4-5 inch bunker profile — thrown into the froth get bit almost every cast when it's happening. It doesn't last long, 20 minutes to half an hour before the blitz slides west with the tide, so you have to be positioned before it starts, not chasing it.

Inside the bay, the fluke bite is the more dependable story, which tracks with what deeper, cooler channel structure should be doing with surface temps sitting in the low-to-mid 70s. The sod banks along the Pattersquash Island channel and the drop-off edges around Senix Creek are holding keeper fluke in 8 to 14 feet, with the better fish coming right where the channel bottom breaks from hard sand into that softer mud edge. Standard drift with a 3/4-ounce white bucktail tipped with Gulp Swimming Mullet, or a spearing/squid strip combo, worked slow along that seam on the last of the incoming into the top of the tide. It's not a blitz — it's a steady pick, one or two keepers mixed with a handful of shorts most drifts, which is honest summer fluking, not a highlight reel. The East Moriches flats off Senix and around Tuthill Point are producing the same way, slightly shallower, 6 to 9 feet, better on the first two hours of the incoming before the sun gets high.

Striped bass are where the thermal story really shows up. Surface water in the bay and off the beach is warm enough that daytime bass activity is close to shut down — that low-70s surface layer pushes them deep and nocturnal, same pattern showing up from here through the Sound. If you want bass right now, you're fishing the inlet itself after dark, working the scour hole that's deepened on the inside edge of that shifted channel — I'm reading it at 18 to 20 feet now where it used to be closer to 14. Bunker chunks or a whole eel drifted through that hole on the last two hours of the ebb, right where the current dumps out of the narrowed throat, has been the most consistent way to connect. Slot fish mostly, with the occasional overslot mixed in on the darkest part of the tide around the new moon, which is building now and should add real push to the current by the weekend.

Weakfish have shown up sporadically on the same channel edges as the fluke — nothing you can plan a trip around, but a few keeper-class weakfish have come off Senix Creek on light jigs fished right on bottom during slack water transitions. Worth a cast or two if you're already out there for fluke, not worth a dedicated trip yet.

Porgies are stacked on the inlet rock piles and the jetty structure itself, which makes sense given how current has concentrated bait right along that new pinch point. Sandworms or clam on a simple hi-lo rig dropped tight to the rocks on either jetty, incoming tide, is about as close to guaranteed action as this zone offers right now — good for a bucket of dinner fish even on a slow bass or fluke day.

Looking ahead, the building tides into the new moon should sharpen this whole pattern rather than break it. Stronger current means a harder-defined seam at the inlet throat, which should concentrate both the bunker-driven bluefish blitzes and the bass that are working that scour hole after dark. If the wind stays out of the south-southwest and doesn't stack up ocean swell into the cut, I'd fish the last two hours of the ebb at first light for blues, then slide into the bay on the incoming for fluke. If a cold front does slide through in the next few days and knocks surface temps down even two or three degrees, don't be surprised if bass start showing some daytime willingness again right on that same current seam — that's the one thing that could change this pattern fast, and it's worth watching the sky for.
