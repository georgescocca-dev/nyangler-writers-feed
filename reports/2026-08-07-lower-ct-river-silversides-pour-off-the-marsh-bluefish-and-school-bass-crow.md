# Silversides Pour Off the Marsh — Bluefish and School Bass Crowd the River Mouth
*August's dog-day warmth has pushed the baitfish out of Great Island's guts, and everything with teeth is waiting at the door.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — August 7** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

By the first week of August the marsh grass at Great Island has gone from that bright new-green of June to the deep, almost blue-green color it holds through Labor Day, and that's always told me more than any thermometer. When the spartina thickens up like that, the silversides and peanut bunker that have been hiding in the guts and pannes all summer start pushing out on every dropping tide, and the predators know it before we do. I've watched this pattern come and go for fifty summers on this river, and it never fails — warm water pushes bait toward the mouth, and the fish follow right behind it. We said as much a few days back, that the warm river flow would keep shoving bait and predators down toward Saybrook Point through this stable stretch of weather, and that's exactly what's unfolding right now.

The river itself is running warm and a little sleepy on the surface — nothing like the ripping cold flow you'd see in April with the shad pushing through. Out in the Sound the gradient is steep, Kings Point sitting around 75 and New London pushing past 77, and the river mouth is splitting the difference, warm on top with a cooler push of fresher water underneath on the last of the ebb. That thermal layering is exactly where I want to be fishing — Saybrook Point breakwater, the flats off Great Island, and the deeper cuts around the DEP Marina where that cooler bottom water holds through the heat of the day.

Snapper blues are thick right now in the shallows off Old Lyme and up into the coves around Great Island — small, aggressive, and about the most fun a kid with a spinning rod can have on this river. Half-ounce gold Kastmasters and tiny Deadly Dicks worked fast on the surface are doing the damage, best on the first two hours of the incoming when the warmer marsh water starts sliding back over the flats. Mixed in with them, and this is the part worth paying attention to, are bigger adult blues — three and four pounds, sometimes better — that have been riding the peanut bunker schools right up to the mouth. I had a report of fish busting bunker hard off the Point on a calm evening tide, the kind of surface commotion you can see from the breakwater rocks. Bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig, or a bucktail with a strip of pork rind trailing it, worked along the current seam where the river water meets the Sound — that's been the ticket.

Striped bass are still here, but I'll be straight with you, they're not stacked up the way they were in May. What we've got now are holdover school fish and a scattering of better ones working the tide changes, particularly the last hour of the ebb when bait gets flushed hard out of the marsh creeks into the main river channel off Great Island. White bucktails, half-ounce to three-quarter, tipped with a strip of squid, bounced along that channel edge in eight to fifteen feet, has been the most consistent producer. Don't waste your time fishing the slack — these fish want moving water pushing bait past them, and on this river that means fishing the tide, not the clock. Essex has been quieter than I'd like this week, but the deeper holes below the town dock have given up a few better bass after dark, fish that seem to be holding on that cooler bottom layer through the heat of the day and only committing to feed once the sun's off the water.

White perch fishing has actually been the most dependable thing going, if you're willing to slow down for it. The shaded deep holes above Essex and along the Lyme side channels are holding good numbers, and small grass shrimp or a piece of bloodworm on a simple two-hook bottom rig, fished right on the bottom in eight to twelve feet, will keep you busy. It's not glamorous fishing, but on a river that's otherwise running warm and a little quiet in the daylight hours, it's the kind of steady action that keeps you sharp for when the tide turns and the blues start showing again.

Looking ahead, the tide is starting to build back out of the neap phase we've been sitting in, and that extra push should sharpen up the bite everywhere current matters — the breakwater, the river mouth flats, the deeper bends around Essex. If the bunker schools stay put and this stable, calm weather holds through the week, I'd expect the blitz activity at the mouth to keep building rather than fade, especially on evening tides when the light goes flat and the bait gets nervous. If a front does push through and stirs up the river, I'd shift my time to the perch holes and wait it out — that pattern doesn't care much about wind. Either way, this is a week to fish the moving water and let the tide tell you where to be, same as it's always been on this river.
