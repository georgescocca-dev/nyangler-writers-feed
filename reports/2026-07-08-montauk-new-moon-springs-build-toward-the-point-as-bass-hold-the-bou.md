# New moon springs build toward the Point as bass hold the boulders and bluefin show south
*Bass are eating on structure at first and last light, tuna moved in close, and the fluke have abandoned the bays for cleaner ocean water.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 8** — _by Rich Martin, Zone Writer_

Last week the moon was fading toward new, which meant the tides were still building, not yet at their biggest push, and that gave us a stretch of manageable current at the reefs — the kind where you can actually hold a jig or a plug in the strike zone instead of getting swept off the structure in six seconds flat. Water out at the Point has been warm, warmer than I'd like for peak striper metabolism, but the bait doesn't care and neither do the bass when there's structure to ambush from. We had a solid week of fish up to 50 pounds coming off bucktails, topwater, and fly gear worked over the boulder fields — not every trip, not every tide, but consistent enough that guys who put in the hours at dawn and dusk were rewarded.

This week the moon runs new on the 11th, and that's the story you need to plan around. New moon means spring tides — bigger swings, faster water, more current pushing through the rip off the Lighthouse and across North Bar than we've seen in weeks. The peak alignment hits Friday through Monday, the 10th through the 13th, and that's when I want to be standing on the rocks at first light with the tide just starting to move. Bigger tides mean more bait getting pushed and pulled through the structure, which means more reason for a big bass to leave her rock and commit. It also means faster water, so leave the light bucktails home — you want enough lead to hold bottom in that current, not get swept into the slick.

The bass fishing has been legitimate, not fantasy-league, but legitimate. Best window has been the last hour of the incoming through the first hour of the outgoing, right at the Point itself — the rip that forms off the Lighthouse when the tides collide, and the boulder field just west of it toward the Elbow. Guys throwing topwater pencils and Danny plugs at first light have been getting bites from fish in the 20 to 35 pound class, with the occasional 40-plus mixed in when the current's really moving bait. After dark, live eels drifted through the same water have out-produced everything else for the true cows — that 50-pounder everyone's talking about came on an eel, not a plug, and that's almost always how the biggest fish in this system decide to eat. Shagwong Reef has been holding fish too, especially on the last two hours of the outgoing when the current sweeps bait off the reef edge into deeper water — bucktails in the 1 to 2 ounce range, white or chartreuse, tipped with a strip of pork rind or a Fin-S, worked slow along bottom in 20 to 35 feet.

Turtle Cove has been a mixed bag — some mornings stacked with bait and bass working through it, other mornings dead calm with nothing showing. That's the nature of this fishery in July: the bass are here, but they're not glued to one spot the way they are in October. You've got to check three or four places before you find where they're actually feeding that tide.

Fluke have made their seasonal move, and it's right on schedule. The bays have gotten too warm to hold quality fish, so they've pushed out toward the inlets and onto cleaner ocean structure. I'm hearing good things from the drift outside Montauk Harbor and along the edges toward Fort Pond Bay on the outgoing, where that flushing water stays cooler and clean. Standard drift rig — 3/4-ounce bucktail with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet trailer, or a spearing-and-squid strip combo on a high-low rig — fished in 25 to 40 feet has been putting keepers in the box, with the occasional four and five pounder mixed into a lot of shorts. It's not a doormat parade, but it's honest fishing if you put in the drifts.

Porgies remain a reliable backup around Star Island and the rock piles off the harbor mouth — small hooks, clam bait, standard high-low rigs, nothing fancy, and they'll keep bending rods when the bass go quiet midday.

The bigger story developing is offshore. Bluefin have pushed in tight to the south side, close enough that small boats running 15 to 20 miles out of the harbor are connecting on schoolie-class fish, and that's the kind of thing that doesn't happen every July. If that pattern holds through this week's tide change, I'd expect more boats making that run instead of grinding the inshore rips. There's also a real bonito and albie watch underway right now — the window's been open since the Fourth and it's closing fast, so if you want a shot at the first false albacore of the season, this is the stretch to be running the rocks off the Point with small metal and epoxy jigs, watching for that nervous water and diving birds.

Bottom line for the week ahead: fish the building tide around the new moon for bass, work the Point structure at the tide change, keep eels rigged for after dark, and don't be surprised if the fluke boat outproduces the surf rats this week. The water's warm, the bait's here, and the Point is doing what it's always done — you just have to show up on the right piece of the tide.
