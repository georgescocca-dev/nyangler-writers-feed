# Manasquan Rocks at Dawn: Blues Run the Jetties, Bass Wait for Dark
*The bluefish showed up right on schedule and haven't left — but if you want a real striper, you're fishing this by moonlight, not sunrise.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — August 3** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

Five-fifteen Tuesday morning, north jetty at Manasquan Inlet, and there were already four guys standing on the rocks I usually have to myself. Word travels fast when the blues are running — one of them had a bluefish still flapping on the rocks before I'd even climbed over the first boulder. That's the story of the last two weeks on this stretch, and it hasn't slowed down.

The Miss Belmar has been stacking limits of bluefish since the end of July, and I've watched the same pattern play out from the rocks — smaller fish first, then bigger ones mixing in day by day. That's not the same tired resident school getting worked over morning after morning. That's fresh fish pushing down from the north, stacking on top of what's already here. We flagged this exact move last week — said watch for bigger fish signaling a new wave, and here it is, cocktail blues giving way to three- and four-pound choppers on the same drift.

For shore guys, the pattern is simple and it's been reliable, which after twenty-five years out here I don't take for granted. First light, before the sun gets high enough to push the bait down, the blues are up on top at the Manasquan north and south jetties, at the Shark River Inlet rocks, and along the Spring Lake jetties. White and chrome metal — Ava 27s and 17s — worked with a fast retrieve gets bit the second it hits the water when there's a blitz going. Once the sun's up and the surface action dies, switch to poppers, a Gibbs or a Yo-Zuri stickbait, and work them slower along the edges where the current sets up against the rocks on the incoming tide. That current seam is where the bait gets pinned, and where the blues sit waiting for it.

Fluke have been the honest bread-and-butter fish all week, and I mean honest — not a blitz, not a giveaway, but steady if you put in the drift time. The Manasquan Inlet channel on the outgoing tide, 20 to 25 feet, has been giving up keepers mixed with a lot of shorts on 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 4-inch chartreuse Gulp swimming mullets, dragged slow along bottom. Shark River's a little different animal — that one fishes better on the incoming, working the sod banks and channel edges in 15 to 20 feet with the same bucktail-and-Gulp combo, sometimes swapped to smoke or pink when the water's got a little color to it after a blow. Sea bass are riding along on the same drifts wherever there's structure — rock piles off the Shark River reef and the edges around the inlet rocks themselves — and a squid strip on the bucktail hook takes care of both species without changing your rig.

Now the part nobody wants to hear but everybody already knows: the striper bite on the rocks at dawn has been mostly dead. Water's sitting in the low-to-mid 70s on the surface, and bass don't love that kind of warm water in daylight — they've slid deep and gone nocturnal, same as they do every summer when it gets like this. I've stood on the Sea Bright rocks at first light three mornings this week and had exactly one fish to show for it, a schoolie that clearly didn't get the memo. If you want a real striper right now, you're not fishing sunrise — you're fishing two hours either side of midnight. Eels drifted off the jetty tips at Manasquan Inlet on the outgoing, working the drop-off where 8 feet falls to 12, have produced better than anything I've thrown in daylight. Same goes for swimming a big Danny plug or a Gibbs pencil popper along the Sea Bright rocks after dark — slow, methodical, working the wash where the white water meets the rock face. It's not fast fishing. It's one good fish for a lot of casts, which is about what you'd expect when the whole population's decided daylight isn't worth the trouble.

Porgies have been a nice consolation prize for anybody who wants steady action without the late-night commitment — bloodworms or clam on a high-low rig off the Shark River rocks or the base of the Manasquan jetties has been filling coolers with decent-sized scup, especially on the bottom half of the incoming when the current slacks enough to keep bait down without getting swept.

Looking ahead, the tides are building toward the new moon, and that extra push of current usually does one of two things around here — either it stirs the bass out of their nighttime funk a little early, or it does nothing until the water actually cools off. I'd bet on the latter unless we get a real break in the heat. The bluefish, though, I don't see any reason to slow down as long as that bait stays thick along the beach — if the size keeps climbing the way it has, there's probably another wave stacking up somewhere north of us right now. Worth getting up early for. Just don't expect the stripers to cooperate until the sun's been down a while.
