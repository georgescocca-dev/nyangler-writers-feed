# Shoals Ledges Give Up Cod to 8 Pounds While the Piscataqua Keeps Its Bass Honest
*Thirteen miles of coastline sounds thin until you've fished the drops off Appledore and the seam at Henderson's Point on the right tide.*

**ISLES OF SHOALS, NH — July 27** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People see thirteen miles of coastline on a map and figure there's nothing here worth trailering a boat for. I've been running my 20-foot Lund out to the Shoals before sunrise for going on fifteen years, and I can tell you those thirteen miles fish like a hundred and thirty if you know where the bottom breaks. This week proved it again — cod, haddock, mackerel, stripers, and bluefish, all inside a morning's run from Rye Harbor.

Start with the Shoals, because that's where the week's best fish came from. White Island and the ledges south of Appledore have been holding cod in the 60 to 90 foot range, and I mean holding — not just passing through. We had a stretch on Saturday's slack tide where three of us put five keeper cod in the boat, biggest going right around eight pounds, on skimmer clams fished tight to bottom with just enough weight to feel the structure. Haddock have been mixed in but smaller, mostly shorts, with a few keepers coming off the rockier stretch between Star and Duck Island on diamond jigs worked with a short hop. The pattern's been the same all month — you want the last hour of the ebb into the first hour of slack, when the current lays down enough to hold bottom without a boat anchor's worth of lead. Fish the moving water hard and you're pulling gear instead of catching.

Mackerel have been thick around the Shoals ledges too, schooling tight enough that you can jig a few dozen in fifteen minutes on a string of small crappie jigs or a mackerel tree, then use the fresh ones for bait or just take a cooler home for the smoker. That mackerel show is also feeding the bigger picture — bluefish have followed the bait right up onto the ledges and into the mouth of the Piscataqua, and they've been willing daytime, which isn't always the case once July gets hot. Blues to four and five pounds have been busting on top around Odiorne Point and off Frost Point, chewing through anything metal — Kastmasters, Deadly Dicks, anything with flash worked fast on top.

The striper story is a little more nuanced, and it's worth being straight about it. Down south the bass have gone nocturnal with surface water sitting in the low-to-mid 70s, and that pattern's creeping into our southern stretch too — Hampton and Seabrook have had their slow stretches midday. But the Gulf of Maine water up here stays cooler than the Sound, and that's kept some daytime activity going around the river mouths and ledges where southern New England guys are stuck waiting for dark. The Piscataqua's been the most consistent piece of that puzzle. Henderson's Point and the seam below the General Sullivan Bridge have both been giving up stripers to 30 inches on the last two hours of the incoming, fish holding in the current break waiting for bait to get swept through. Bucktails in the half-ounce to one-ounce range, white or chartreuse, worked slow along the bottom edge of the seam, have outfished everything else I've thrown. A few guys working live mackerel under the Memorial Bridge on the same tide stage have done even better after dark, which tracks with what's happening everywhere else on the coast right now — the bigger fish are playing it cautious once the sun's up.

Hampton Harbor has been a mixed bag. The inlet's producing schoolie bass and the occasional slot fish pushing in on the tide, especially the last of the incoming when bait gets funneled through the channel by Great Boars Head. Peanut bunker on a fishfinder rig, or a soft plastic swimbait worked slow along the channel edge, has been the play. But it's been spotty — some days the harbor's stacked with bait and fish, other days it's dead water for two hours before anything shows. If you're fishing Hampton right now, don't burn your whole trip on one spot. Move with the bait.

We called this stretch of stable weather last week, and it's held — no major thermal shift, no blown-out surf, no sudden cold-water push off the Shoals to scatter the groundfish. That stability is exactly why the cod bite has stayed consistent instead of turning on and off with every weather system, and why the bluefish have kept riding that mackerel bait right through the harbor mouths instead of pushing offshore.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full in early August, which means current is going to sharpen up fast over the next week. That's good news for the Shoals — stronger current concentrates bait tighter against the ledges and usually tightens up the cod bite around the slack windows even more. It's a mixed bag for the harbor bass, though — stronger tides mean shorter, more violent bite windows at Henderson's Point and the Hampton inlet, so timing matters more than it has the last few weeks. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take the early slack at White Island for cod, then run back in to hit the Piscataqua seam on the tail of the incoming before the current gets ahead of the presentation. That's the play I'm making, anyway — box first, coffee after.
