# August 14: Washington's Early Push Rolls Into Its Second Month
*The canyon that starts early is still holding its bigeye and mahi pattern while everybody else is just catching up.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON — August 14** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

I've said it for fifteen years and I'll say it again now that we're two weeks past the new moon in mid-August: Washington doesn't wait for the rest of the fleet. This canyon rides the Gulf Stream's inside edge earlier than Hudson, earlier than Wilmington, and by the time the calendar flips to August we're not starting a bite — we're managing one that's already a month old. That's the difference between fishing a canyon and knowing a canyon. Right now we're deep into the mature stretch of the season, past the frantic June scramble to find the first push of warm water, and into the steady grind where the fish know where the bait is and so do we.

The pattern this week has been consistent with what a mid-August Washington should look like. Bigeye have settled onto the deeper structure along the eastern wall, working the drop where the bottom falls from the 600-fathom curve down toward 900. Boats dropping squid and butterfish on the overnight drift are getting quality bites in that window between midnight and first light — not a stacked bite, but a repeatable one, two to four fish a trip if you put in the time and don't burn out your window chasing showier action elsewhere. Yellowfin have been more scattered, working the temperature breaks higher up in the water column, and the troll bite on green machines and small Ilanders behind the spreader bars has been decent but not explosive. You want to be working the seams where the cleaner blue water pushes against the green — that's where the bait's getting pinned and that's where the fish are sitting on it.

Mahi are doing exactly what mahi do this time of year — they're under anything that floats. Lobster pot warp, loose debris, even a stray patch of sargassum will hold a pod. I had a report of a good gaffer-class fish and a pile of schoolies come off a single piece of junk drifting on the edge, and that's the story right now: small debris fields scattered along the canyon, each one worth a few minutes of your time on the way out or the way back. Don't skip them to save fuel — that's free fish if you're paying attention.

Wahoo have been in the mix on the high-speed troll, particularly for boats running wire line and Braid rigged lures at 12 to 15 knots along the drop-off. It's not a dedicated wahoo bite yet — nobody's limiting out — but enough fish are showing up incidentally on the tuna spread that it's worth throwing one dedicated wahoo bomb deep in the pattern if you're already trolling that structure.

White marlin have been around the temperature breaks too, though the bite's been more of a tease than a program. A few pitch-bait opportunities on skipping ballyhoo for boats running dink baits behind the dredge, but I wouldn't plan a trip around marlin specifically right now — treat it as a bonus while you're really out there for the tuna.

Here's the thing about mid-August at Washington that a lot of boats forget: the crowd thins out. Everybody ran hard in July when the word got out that we were producing early, and now attention's shifted east toward the warm pool that's been sitting off Hudson and Wilmington. That's fine by me. Less traffic means less pressure on the same lumps and eddies, and the fish that are here haven't been pounded by six boats a day dropping the same squid rig on the same spot. If that warm-water pocket stays put east of the closer canyons over the next several days, I'd expect Washington to keep its share of the bigeye and yellowfin traffic rather than lose it to boats making the shorter run elsewhere. If it slides west, that's when things get interesting — and crowded.

Looking ahead, I don't see a reason for this pattern to fall apart in the next week. The overnight bigeye bite along the eastern wall should hold as long as the thermal structure stays where it's been, and the debris-line mahi fishing should keep producing right through the end of the month — that bite typically doesn't fade at Washington until we're deep into September. Wahoo will likely stay a bycatch story rather than a dedicated target unless we get a real influx of bait pushing tighter to the drop. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd run straight for the eastern wall, set up the overnight drift on squid and butterfish, and troll the seam on the way home — that's the two-for-one that's been working, and there's no reason to think it stops now unless the water moves on us.
