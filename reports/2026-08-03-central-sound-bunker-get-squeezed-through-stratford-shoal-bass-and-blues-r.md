# Bunker Get Squeezed Through Stratford Shoal — Bass and Blues Riding the Pinch
*As the Sound narrows and the tide eases toward neap, the bait has nowhere to hide — and neither do the fish underneath it.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — August 3** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spent Tuesday morning drifting off the Shoal doing nothing but watching birds, and it told me more than any temp gauge could. Terns were working tight little knots over peanut bunker balled up right where the Sound necks down between the Long Island side and the Connecticut shoreline — that classic Stratford Shoal squeeze where the whole water column gets funneled through a skinnier lane. The bait wasn't spread out cruising, it was stacked and nervous, diving for the bottom every time a shadow passed under it. That's not weather behavior. That's predator behavior.

Kings Point is reading 72.9 degrees, New London's sitting at 69.6, and that gradient matters more than people give it credit for. The warmer water on the western end is holding bait comfortably, but as you push east toward the Shoal and beyond, that bunker starts bunching up against the cooler push instead of just drifting through it. That's exactly the kind of choke point that turns a wandering bait school into a fixed target — and fixed targets get found fast by bass and blues that don't want to work any harder than they have to in August heat.

The striper bite has settled into the pattern you'd expect for this time of year — fish holding deep on structure through the bright hours, then sliding up to feed hard at first light, last light, and after dark. Middle Ground Light and the deeper edges around the Shoal have been holding fish during the day on bucktails worked slow along bottom, but the real quality has come after the sun drops. Guys running eels at the bridges over on the Norwalk side — the causeway spans and the cuts around the Islands — have been doing well on the last two hours of the outgoing into the start of the incoming, snake-style presentations right along the pilings where the current squeezes past the structure. We flagged this pattern last week, saying that as tides softened toward the coming neap, bass would compress onto the strongest current hours and the deepest structure — that's precisely what's playing out now, and the eel bite at the bridges has been the clearest proof of it.

Fluke fishing has been the most dependable thing going right now, and it's living up to the seasonal peak everyone talks about in late July into early August. Boats drifting the channel edges off Port Jeff Harbor and working the deeper flats around Middle Ground Light have been putting doormat-class fish in the box — nothing giant every drop, but a steady grind of fish in the 3 to 6 pound range mixed with enough shorts to keep you busy. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a squid strip, dragged slow across the sandy-mud bottom on the tail end of the outgoing tide, has been the go-to. The bite isn't uniform everywhere — I had one drift off Charles Island last week that produced nothing but shorts for two hours before we found the right depth change and started connecting on keepers — so it pays to keep moving until the sounder shows that subtle break where sand gives way to structure.

Bluefish are riding shotgun with the bunker wherever it concentrates, and that's been most consistent right in that Shoal corridor and along the edges of the bait pods pushing past the Norwalk Islands. When the blues show up under a bunker school, it's not subtle — the water goes white and the birds go crazy — but those windows have been short-lived this week, ten or fifteen minutes of chaos before the whole show moves fifty yards and you're chasing it again.

Porgies remain the most reliable fish in the zone right now. Every reef from Bridgeport out to New Haven has been giving up steady counts on the incoming tide, small pieces of clam or squid on a simple high-low rig doing the job without much finesse required. We called this building trend a week ago and it's held — if you want a guaranteed bend in the rod for the kids or for dinner, this is still the play.

Blackfish are still off the table with the season closed, but I'd be lying if I said I wasn't already circling a few rock piles off Norwalk for October. Weakfish have shown up here and there in the marsh creeks around Oyster Bay and the back sides of the Islands, but sporadic enough that I wouldn't build a trip around them yet.

As for the bonito — nothing confirmed in my water yet, but I'm watching. The thermal gradient east of here needs to clean up and the bait needs to keep pushing in tighter pods before that first push shows on this side of the Sound. Looking ahead, that neap tide settling in around the 5th and 6th should keep softening the current through the Shoal and the Gut-adjacent structure, which means lighter jig heads and slower retrieves are the right call for the next several days. If the bait stays squeezed the way it is right now, the bass and blues underneath it aren't going anywhere — they're just going to keep getting easier to find.
