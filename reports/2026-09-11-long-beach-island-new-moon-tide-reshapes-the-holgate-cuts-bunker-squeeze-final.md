# New moon tide reshapes the Holgate cuts — bunker squeeze finally pulls stripers to the sand
*Barnegat Inlet is holding the first real blitz of the fall while LBI's surf fluke stay stubbornly short.*

**SHIP BOTTOM, NJ — September 11** — _by Tommy Nguyen, Zone Writer_

Walked the Holgate cuts Tuesday morning on the tail of the ebb and the sandbar geometry had shifted again — the September 10 new moon pushed enough water to carve a fresh trough about sixty yards south of the old inside bar, right where the beach bends toward the refuge. That's the kind of structure change that matters this time of year, because it's exactly where bunker pods have started getting pinned against the beach on the last two tide cycles. The moon's pull is the whole story right now. It's generating the strongest push we've seen since Labor Day, and it's squeezing bait tight against the beaches, inlets, and river mouths from Barnegat Light down through Little Egg. That's the mechanism behind everything that's happening — and starting to happen — on this stretch of coast.

We flagged this last week — said bass would start showing real interest in the tightening bunker schools as the moon built, rather than just picking at stragglers, and that's exactly what's playing out. Barnegat Inlet has been the epicenter. Boats working the rips off the north jetty and the surf crowd casting from the rocks at the base of the lighthouse have both connected on bass committing hard to bunker pods getting funneled through the inlet on the last two hours of the ebb. Nothing sustained yet — this is the leading edge, not the full-blown blitz — but the commitment level is real. Bunker spooking on the surface, boils showing, and fish taking the bait instead of just shadowing it. That's a different animal than the pecking-order activity we had through most of August.

The presentation that's working is simple: match the bunker with a bunker spoon or a big paddle-tail swimbait — six to eight inches — worked just under the surface where the bait is nervous. Surfcasters throwing metal-lip swimmers or a heavy Hopkins into the wash at the base of the jetty are getting hooked up when the bait gets pushed tight against the rocks on the dropping tide. If you're working bait, a whole bunker on a fish-finder rig, dropped just outside where the boils are showing, has been the better call than trying to horse a live one through the melee.

South of the inlet, Ship Bottom and Surf City are showing the same pattern in miniature — smaller pods, shorter windows, but the same mechanics. High tide plus dusk has been the sweet spot; that's when the bait gets pushed shallow enough for the bass to work it without burning too much energy chasing.

Fluke is the tougher conversation this week, and it's worth being straight about it. Little Egg Reef and the surf zone from Holgate up through Barnegat Light are producing plenty of bites but almost all undersized fish. That's not a fluke in the productivity — pun intended — it's the classic late-season thermal split. The keeper-class fish have already staged off toward deeper, cooler water ahead of their fall exit, and what's left working the sand and the back-bay channels are juveniles that haven't made that move yet. We called this fade a week ago too, and it's holding exactly to form. If you're set on fluke, the Mullica channel edges and the deeper holes off Graveling Point are still worth a drift with a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a half-ounce bucktail, but temper expectations — you're sorting through a lot of shorts for every keeper.

Bluefish are the other half of that same tapering-fluke story, and they're picking up exactly where the fluke bite is dropping off. Snapper blues are thick in the back bays right now — Great Bay, the marsh creeks off Tuckerton, the flats behind Beach Haven — small but aggressive, hitting tiny Kastmasters and Gotcha plugs off the docks and bulkheads. That's usually the tell that adult blues aren't far behind as the water keeps cooling, and this week's action at the inlet backs that up. Bluefish in the 4-to-8-pound class have been mixed in with the striper activity off the north jetty, hitting the same bunker spoons and swimmers.

Black drum and weakfish have been quiet, which tracks for this point in September — both species are more of an early-season story on this stretch, and neither has shown up in any numbers worth chasing right now.

Looking ahead, the next 48 to 72 hours should be the window to watch. The moon's still close enough to new that the tidal push hasn't eased off, and if the bunker keep tightening the way they have been, Barnegat Inlet and the stretch of beach immediately south of it should be the first spot on the island to go from scattered boils to a sustained blitz. If you can only get out once this week, fish the last two hours of the ebb at the inlet, or push to Holgate and work that new cut on the dropping tide — the bait's been using it as a pinch point, and where the bait gets squeezed, the bass show up to collect. Keep a bucktail rigged as backup in case the blitz doesn't materialize and you need to work the edges instead of the middle of the chaos.
