# Bay pushes into the high 70s — porgies mob Shelter Island, bass slide into the night shift
*Fluke stay steady in the channels, weakfish are trickling back on calm evenings, and the stripers have gone strictly after-dark.*

**GREENPORT, NY — July 18** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

By the third week of July the Peconic has fully caught up to itself. This bay warms faster than the Sound every spring, and by now it's paid the bill for that head start — surface temps are sitting in the mid-to-high 70s from Noyack clear out to Orient, and that's pushed the whole system into its classic summer rhythm. The porgies don't care one bit. The fluke are holding to their lanes like they always do. But the striped bass have punched the clock and gone to night shift, and if you're still fishing them at noon you're fishing the wrong hours.

Start with the good news, because there's plenty of it. Porgy fishing around Shelter Island right now is about as good as this fishery gets anywhere on the East Coast. Cedar Island, Ram Island, and the mussel beds off Coecles Harbor are stacked with scup running 12 to 15 inches, some pushing 16, and they're eating anything you drop — but if you want quality over quantity, small pieces of clam or squid on a hi-lo rig with size 6 or 4 hooks, fished right on the bottom in 15 to 25 feet, is still the way to go. Fish the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide — that's when they really turn on around the harbor mouths and the current isn't ripping your rig sideways. I had a half day out there Tuesday morning and we could have filled a five-gallon bucket in an hour if we'd wanted to keep grinding. Bring light tackle. These fish deserve it.

Fluke are doing exactly what we said they'd do — steady, dependable, unglamorous, and that's fine by me. The channels between Robins Island and the Southold shore, the deeper cuts off Nassau Point, and the drop-offs into Noyack Bay are all holding keeper-class fish in the 18 to 22 inch range mixed with a lot of shorts you'll have to sort through. White or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the drift, are still outproducing bait rigs for me. Best window is the last two hours of the outgoing when the current is still moving enough to keep your bait working but not so hard you're bouncing bottom like a jackhammer. We flagged last week that this bite would carry through summer, and it has — no signs of it slowing, just the usual grind of sorting through short fish to find your dinner.

Weakfish are the interesting story right now. They'd gone quiet for a stretch, but on these calm, warm evenings they're starting to show again in small numbers off the deeper edges — Threemile Harbor's outer flats, the mouth of Sag Harbor Cove, and the drop just outside Greenport Harbor. Nothing to build a trip around yet, but a few fish in the 20 to 24 inch class have come off soft plastics and small swimming plugs fished at dusk into full dark, which lines up with exactly what we thought would happen once the evenings settled down and stayed warm. If you're patient and you like fishing that last hour of light into darkness, it's worth a rod.

Now the bass. They haven't left — they've just adjusted, same as they do every July when the water climbs into the mid-70s. Daytime, they're tucked deep against current breaks and structure where it's a few degrees cooler, and they're barely feeding until the sun drops. Plum Gut is still the best bet in the whole system for this pattern because the current there never really stops moving and it keeps water churned and slightly cooler than the flat bay around it. Fish it on the last two hours of the ebb into the turn, right along the western edge where the rip folds back on itself. As we head toward the first quarter moon on the 21st, that current is going to ease up some, and that's actually good news — it means slower presentations will start working better than the frantic stuff you needed during full push. A 1.5-ounce bucktail cast across the seam and allowed to sink clean before a slow bottom retrieve has been the most consistent producer, especially after dark. Orient Point and the North Race are holding similar action for anyone willing to make the run and fish the same tide stages at night.

Bluefish are mixed in wherever the bass are working the rips, and they'll save a slow bass night — small to mid-size blues chasing bait through the Gut and off Orient, happy to jump on diamond jigs or poppers if you're covering water in the dark.

Looking ahead, expect this pattern to hold as long as the bay stays warm and calm. The porgy and fluke bites should keep rolling with minimal change — they're both tied more to structure and tide than temperature swings at this point. The bass will keep favoring darkness until we get a real cool-down or a stretch of wind that mixes the bay, which doesn't look imminent. If I had one evening to fish this week, I'd be at Plum Gut on the last of the ebb into the turn, working that western seam slow and deep, with a light spinning rod rigged for porgies as a backup plan on the way home.
