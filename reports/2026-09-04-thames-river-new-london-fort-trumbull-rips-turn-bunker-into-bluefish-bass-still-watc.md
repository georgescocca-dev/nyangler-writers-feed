# Fort Trumbull Rips Turn Bunker Into Bluefish, Bass Still Watching From the Seams
*The ebb off Fort Trumbull is stacking bait against the current line, and the blues have committed — the stripers haven't, yet.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — September 4** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

The ebb off Fort Trumbull has a different sound to it this week. Where the river current pinches against the fort wall and kicks out toward the Race, there's a hard seam forming on the back half of the tide — you can see it from the seawall, a visible crease where the darker river water meets the harbor green. That seam has been loaded with peanut bunker and silversides for about ten days now, and it's the best single read on what's actually happening in this system heading into the second week of September.

Water at the mouth is sitting around 69.4 degrees, a few clicks cooler than what's showing out west toward Kings Point near 71. That gradient matters. It's the same gap that's been holding bunker schools tight in Raritan Bay and along the Long Island south shore without tipping them into full blitz mode — the water's warm enough that bass don't need to commit hard yet, but cool enough on this end of the Sound that the bait is starting to stage instead of scatter. The moon is building toward full on the 6th and 7th, and that's the mechanism that usually breaks this stalemate. Stronger tidal flow on the building moon tightens loose bait balls into something a predator can actually pin against structure. We flagged the bonito push toward Fishers Island Sound last week as something worth watching given the proximity, and the spillover has shown up — a handful of fish have been seen working bait on the surface out toward the Race and into the eastern approaches, which tells you the same warm-water regime that's been feeding Block Island and Point Judith all summer hasn't broken yet.

Bluefish are the story right now, no question. The mouth of the Thames, from the sub base pilings down through Fort Trumbull, has had steady blitz activity on the last two hours of the ebb and the first hour of the flood, timed almost exactly to when bait gets squeezed through that current seam. Blues from 3 to 6 pounds are hammering peanut bunker imitations — 1-ounce Deadly Dick or a chrome Kastmaster worked with a fast, erratic retrieve gets bit as soon as it lands near the surface commotion. Poppers work too when the fish are up and slashing, which has been most mornings this week around first light. Gold Star Bridge has produced the same pattern on the pilings themselves — blues stacking on the downtide side of the concrete on the ebb, feeding on bait getting swept through. Bring wire or heavy fluoro leader, because these fish are cutting off gear that isn't built for it.

Striped bass are around, but honest talk: they're staged, not committed. That matches what's showing up basin-wide — the fish are sitting on bait rather than actively working it over. In the Thames, that means bass are holding deep on the current breaks below Fort Trumbull and along the deeper pilings under Gold Star Bridge, usually 15 to 25 feet down on the last of the ebb, picking off stragglers rather than blitzing the surface. Live-lining a peanut bunker on a 4/0 circle hook with just enough weight to hold near bottom has been the most consistent way to connect, fished tight to the pilings where the current slows in the eddy pocket. Bucktails in the 1- to 1.5-ounce range, white or olive over white, worked slow along the bottom through the same seams have also picked up fish in the mid-20-inch class, mostly on the last two hours of the ebb when the current is still moving enough to hold the bait but has eased enough to fish it properly. Nobody's finding numbers yet. It's a quality-over-quantity bite, and it's going to stay that way until the bunker schools tighten enough that the bass stop watching and start feeding.

Porgies are still worth a stop if bass and blues aren't cooperating — the pilings around the harbor and the rocky stretch off Pleasure Beach are holding decent scup on squid strips and clam, fished on standard high-low rigs in 15 to 20 feet. Nothing dramatic, but a reliable box-filler while the bigger story plays out.

Looking ahead, the full moon on the 6th-7th is the hinge point. If the tidal push it generates does what it usually does this time of year, expect the bait currently staging at Fort Trumbull and along the bridge pilings to get squeezed into tighter, more vulnerable schools — and that's when the stripers usually stop sitting on the sidelines. The window for the first real fall blitz in this river is realistically seven to ten days out, in line with what's setting up basin-wide in Raritan Bay and the south shore. Until then, the smart play is working the ebb at Fort Trumbull for blues, dropping bunker or bucktails deep on the bridge pilings for the bigger, quieter bass, and keeping an eye toward the Race for any more bonito showing up on the surface — that push west isn't finished yet.
