# Thames goes slack toward the neap — bass tuck into the pilings, blues hold at the river mouth
*As the tide range narrows this week, the bite window gets shorter and the fish get pickier about where they sit.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 3** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

You can feel the current losing its grip before you ever look at a tide table. I was under the Gold Star Bridge Thursday evening on the last of the ebb, and the seam that usually rips hard enough to hold your jig at an angle was barely nudging my line. That's the neap talking — we're sliding into the softer tides around August 5-6, and anybody who's fished this river long enough knows what that means. The bass stop roaming and they go find the one piece of structure that still moves water reliably, and they sit on it.

Right now that's the bridge pilings and the deep pocket off Fort Trumbull where the river narrows and the current gets squeezed even when the rest of the harbor goes quiet. We flagged this pattern last week — said that as the tides softened, bass would compress into the strongest current hours and the deepest, coolest structure, and that's exactly what's playing out. Daytime fishing has gotten tougher. Surface temps in the harbor are pushing toward 70, a touch cooler than what they're seeing down toward Kings Point, and that gradient matters — our end of the Sound stays a few degrees behind the western basin all summer, which is part of why this river holds fish deep into the heat that shut down other spots. But even here, midday sun on slack water puts the bass down on the bottom, hugging the pilings and the rock at Fort Trumbull, and they're not moving much until the light changes.

The move has been eels after dark, fished slow and heavy right against the pilings on whatever current there is — a 3/4-ounce egg sinker ahead of a swivel, six-foot fluorocarbon leader, letting the eel settle and just twitching it back through the seam. I had two fish over 30 inches that way Wednesday night working the downstream side of the bridge on the tail of the outgoing, and lost a third that felt bigger when it wrapped me around a piling. That's the game right now — fewer opportunities, but the fish that are there are quality, and they're not leaving structure to chase you around.

Bluefish have been the more reliable player this week, and it's a straight bait story. Bunker are holding thick right at the river mouth off Ocean Beach and pushing up as far as the Pleasure Beach line on a good push of tide, and wherever that bait balls up, the blues show up within the day. I watched three separate blitzes off Ocean Beach on Tuesday morning — nothing coastwide-blitz size, but enough fish crashing bait on top that a metal-lipped swimmer or a 2-ounce Kastmaster thrown right into the boil got hit on the first few casts every time. That bunker-driven pattern is the same thing playing out from Raritan Bay on down — bluefish go where the bait is, full stop, and right now the bait is sitting at our doorstep instead of pushing further up the river the way it does in a stronger tide. If you're on the beach at first light with bait visible on the surface, don't wait — those windows have been closing fast once the sun gets up and the bunker sound.

Porgies have quietly been the most dependable fish in the harbor all week, and I mean that as a compliment — nobody's out here talking about scup, but if you want bent rods for the kids or a guaranteed dinner, anchoring up on the mud-and-shell patches inside New London Harbor, especially the flats off the Fort Trumbull side in 15 to 20 feet, has been steady on high-low rigs with squid strips or clam. Small hooks, light sinkers, drop it to bottom and wait — that bite hasn't cared one bit about the softening tide, because those fish aren't current-dependent the way the stripers are.

Blackfish are mostly an afterthought this time of year — the water's too warm and they're tucked tight to structure not feeding heavy, and most of us are saving that game for the fall reopening anyway. I wouldn't spend a tide targeting them right now; better to put that time into the bridge or the beach.

Looking at the week ahead, the neap keeps tightening through the weekend, which means shorter, more defined bite windows rather than all-day grazing. If I've only got one evening, I'm fishing the last two hours of the outgoing at the Gold Star Bridge with eels, right on the pilings, and I'm there before the light's gone because that transition period — dusk into full dark — has been when the bigger fish commit. For bluefish, keep an eye on the river mouth and Ocean Beach at first light; if the bunker stay put through this stretch of stable weather, that topwater window should keep producing, but if the bait redistributes with the softer current, the blues will follow it and you may need to run down toward Pleasure Beach to find them again. Either way, this isn't a week to expect fish to come find you. Go to the structure, go to the bait, and fish the tide change like it's the only thirty minutes you're going to get — because right now, it often is.
