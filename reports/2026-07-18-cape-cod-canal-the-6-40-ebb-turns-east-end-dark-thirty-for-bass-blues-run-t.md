# The 6:40 Ebb Turns East End Dark-Thirty for Bass, Blues Run the Daylight Shift
*Warm water pushed the stripers to a strict night schedule through the ditch — the blues and fluke are covering for them by day.*

**SAGAMORE, MA — July 18** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

Six-forty this evening, the east end drops into max ebb, and that's the number I've been circling all week. Current's running maybe four and a half knots off the Sandwich side on this cycle, not the five-plus we saw around the last new moon, but plenty to fold the rip line back on itself where Cape Cod Bay dumps into the ditch. We're sliding toward the July 21 first quarter, so the swing is easing off the top — smaller tidal range, softer current, longer slack. That matters more than people think. A canal that's ripping at six knots blows bait past the bass before they can commit. Ease it back to four and change, and fish get a real window to sit on the seam and feed instead of just flashing through it.

Here's the pattern I've been telling guys at the bait shop all month, and it's held: daytime, this canal is warm — I'm talking mid-70s on the surface at both ends now, same story you're seeing pushed up from the Sound and the Jersey beaches. Bass don't love that. They're not gone, they've gone nocturnal. That's a thermal thing, not a disappearing act, and it's exactly what I flagged last week — said the night bite would carry the Canal through the heat, and that's precisely what's happening. From about an hour before sunset through first light, especially straddling the tide changes, the east end rip and the Mashnee-Hog Island seam on the west end are still throwing keeper bass to guys who'll stand there in the dark and work a current.

Plugging after dark is still the most honest way to connect. Big paddle-tail swimmers — the 2.5-ounce Gibbs Danny or a soft-bodied Slug-Go rigged on a jighead — worked slow and broadside to the current seam, is putting bass in the 28 to 36-inch range on the board. West end guys working the Mashnee flats and the drop-off toward Hog Island on the last two hours of the west-to-east flood have had the better numbers of overslot fish, several pushing 20 pounds, taken on eels drifted dead-stick along bottom right where the seam folds against the rocks. That's classic July pattern — those current breaks off Mashnee stack bait, and stacked bait after dark with cooler bottom water is where the bigger fish are holding.

Bluefish are filling the daylight gap the bass left behind, and they're not shy about it. Both ends have blues chopping through bait pods on the stronger stages of the tide — I had a report of choppers to 8 pounds hammering topwater poppers off the Scusset Beach rocks on the 1pm max ebb Tuesday, blood in the water, guys reeling in half a mackerel. If you want daytime action and don't mind retying wire leaders every twenty minutes, work the last hour of the flood and first hour of the ebb at either end with a Hopkins jig or a bucktail with a straight tail — anything that flashes and doesn't need finesse, because these blues aren't picky.

Fluke are the quiet dependable story right now, and I mean that as a compliment. They're not sexy, but they're there — working the current-swept bottom off the west end near Wings Neck and drifting the deeper edge toward Phinneys Harbor on the tide changes, guys are picking at keepers in the 18 to 22-inch range on white Gulp paired with a half-ounce bucktail, dragged dead slow right on bottom during the slower slack-adjacent minutes. That's the beauty of fluke in this system — they don't care that the bass have gone to bed. They sit tight to structure and current edges regardless of surface temperature, and with the softer tide swing this week there's more usable bottom time to work them properly instead of getting your gear ripped off the bottom by max current.

Worth noting: mackerel are still running through the east end on the stronger ebb stages, which is exactly the kind of forage that keeps this whole system honest. As long as that bait keeps funneling through the ditch, the bass — even on their nocturnal schedule — have a reason to stack up on the rip rather than scatter.

Looking ahead, if this softer, first-quarter tide pattern holds through the weekend, I'd expect the night bite to stay the most reliable ticket at both ends — plan your trip around the hour before and after the tide change, dark hours only, and don't skimp on eels or big swimmers. Daytime, I'd split my time between bluefish blitzes wherever the bait's thickest and a slow fluke drift off Wings Neck on the softer current. If we get a stretch of onshore wind that knocks the surface temps down even two or three degrees, don't be surprised if the bass start showing a little earlier in the evening — that's the tell I'll be watching for the rest of July.
