# Great Point rip: doormats stacking the Shoals, still no albie in the boat
*Nantucket Sound is quietly running the best fluke bite in New England while every rip chaser east of Montauk waits on the first bonito.*

**NANTUCKET, MA — August 14** — _by Charlie St. Pierre, Zone Writer_

I had a swirl behind the boat Tuesday morning off Great Point that stopped my coffee halfway to my mouth. Tight, fast, gone in a blink — the kind of boil that makes your hand go for the light spinning rod before your brain catches up. I fired a cast into the wash anyway. Bluefish, maybe four pounds, snapped off a peanut bunker imitation like it owed him money. Not what I wanted. But it's the kind of thing that's been happening all week out there — little flashes and false alarms that keep you leaning over the rail a beat too long, because everybody on this island knows the water's right for the real thing to show, and nobody's seen it yet.

Let's start with what IS happening, because it's been a genuinely good week if you know where to point the bow. Nantucket Sound and the Shoals are, right now, producing the best fluke ratio I've seen in years — not just around here, but compared to what I'm hearing out of the rest of New England. We're finding keepers and better drifting the sand-to-gravel transitions on the north side of the Shoals in 25 to 40 feet, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid when the current's really moving. The back half of the ebb has been the money tide — enough push to keep the bait pinned and the bucktail ticking bottom without turning it into a boat anchor. We're seeing three, four keepers to every short, which for fluke fishing anywhere in New England this summer is a number worth writing down. A few fish have pushed past 4 pounds off the drop-offs south of Tuckernuck, and I heard secondhand of a doormat closer to 6 taken drifting bait — squid strip over a spearing — in that same stretch.

The bigger story region-wide is striped bass, and we're seeing our version of it here too. Peanut bunker have poured into Nantucket Sound and the bass have gone right after them, tight to structure around Horseshoe Shoal and along the current edges off Monomoy. This is a little unusual for mid-August — normally the bass have slid deeper and gone quiet on us by now, sulking through the warmest stretch of summer. Instead we've got visible blitzes, birds working low and fast over nervous water, bass punching bunker to the surface in short, violent windows. If you see the birds pile up, don't putt over — cut the engine well outside the school and let the boat drift in. Topwater plugs and 2-ounce metal have both worked once you're in range, but honestly a peanut bunker fly-lined on a fishfinder rig outfishes everything else when the fish are keyed that tight on real bait. Bluefish are riding shotgun in the same schools, which means wire or a heavy fluoro leader if you don't want to donate lures all afternoon.

We flagged a couple weeks back that building current into the new moon would sharpen things up at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's played out — the Sound's channels have more push behind them now, and the bite at Horseshoe Shoal and the rips off Monomoy has firmed up right on schedule. Scup fishing has held up nicely too on the same structure, small hooks and squid strips over the rockier patches off Chatham keeping rods bent for anyone who wants a break from watching for bigger things.

And bigger things is where everybody's head really is. No confirmed albies or bonito yet anywhere from Montauk through Buzzards Bay, and that includes us. But the water's doing everything it's supposed to do to bring them in — it's warm, it's bait-thick, and we're past the new moon push from the 12th that usually kicks the migration corridor into gear. A hammerhead showed up in the surf down in Rhode Island this week, which tells you exactly how much warm, forage-heavy water is sitting out there right now. That's not albie confirmation, but it's the kind of secondary sign that makes a guy like me start rigging epoxy jigs a little earlier than I probably need to.

If I had one morning to burn this week chasing the first fish of the season, I'd be at Great Point on the last two hours of the ebb, working the rip where the current stacks bait against the shoal, watching for that tight, nervous surface disturbance that's different from a bluefish blowup — faster, more erratic, birds diving instead of hovering. I'd have a Deadly Dick or a small Albie Snax tied on and ready before I even left the dock. Sankaty's outer edge is worth a look too on the same tide stage, especially with any bait pushed up along the bluffs.

Best case, that first bonito or albie shows within the next few days and this report changes tone fast. If the pattern slides the way it sometimes does, we might be another week out yet. Either way the fluke bite on the Shoals is real and worth your time right now regardless of what shows up chasing bait behind it — I wouldn't skip a good doormat morning waiting on a fish that hasn't punched in yet.
