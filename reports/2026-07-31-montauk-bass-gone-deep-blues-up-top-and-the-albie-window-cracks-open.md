# Bass Gone Deep, Blues Up Top, and the Albie Window Cracks Open
*The Point is doing its late-July shuffle — quality stripers holed up on structure, bluefish running the surface, and the first credible albie window closing in fast.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 31** — _by Rich Martin, Zone Writer_

I've stood on that boulder field below the Lighthouse enough Julys to know what this water looks like when it's holding its breath. Surface temp at the Point is sitting around 69 degrees, which sounds cool until you remember what the rest of the coast is doing — mid-70s to near 80 from Jersey up through the Cape. That gap is the whole story right now. Montauk stays the last cold-water refuge on this stretch of coast, and that's exactly why the quality fish are still here instead of pushed north into the Gulf of Maine like everywhere else.

But cold-water refuge doesn't mean easy fishing. The bass have gone deep and they've gone smart. I'm finding them stacked on the humps off Shagwong Reef and down in the deeper guts of the boulder field on the ocean side, but they're not up feeding in the middle of the day like they will come October. Right now it's a tide-change and after-dark program. The building tide off the July 29 full moon has been running hard through the rip, and that's when the bigger fish slide up onto the structure to work bunker schools that have been holding just off the drop. I had a 38-pounder come off the North Bar at 5 AM last Tuesday on a fresh bunker chunk, fished on a fish-finder rig with 60-pound fluoro leader, right as the tide turned from ebb to flood. That window — the hour around the turn, both day and night — is where the real fish are living this week. Daylight hours in between have been mostly quiet for anything over slot size, though you'll pick at schoolies working bait pods scattered through Turtle Cove on the last of the outgoing.

Bluefish are the ones benefiting from all this warm water everybody else is complaining about. They don't care about the thermal squeeze the way bass do, and they're all over the rip lines and bait balls from the Point out to Shagwong in good numbers through the middle of the day. Diamond jigs, poppers, anything chrome thrown into a working boil will get bit right now — jumbo blues in the 8 to 12 pound class, teeth like a bandsaw, exactly the kind of daytime action that's been missing from the bass bite. If you want steady rod-bending without waiting for the tide change, this is your play.

Fluke fishing has stayed dependable, which is more than I can say for some Julys. Drifting the edges off Frisco Ridge and out along the deeper flats south of the Point, we're seeing keepers mixed with plenty of shorts — nothing blitz-level, just steady structure fishing on 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets tipped on 3/4-ounce bucktails, working the drift slow so the jig ticks bottom without dragging through the mussel bottom too hard. Offshore, the sea bass and porgy fishing on the humps and wrecks has been about as good as it gets all season — heavy bags, good average size, and it's given a lot of boats a reason to run wide when the inshore bite gets stingy.

Now, the thing everybody's actually asking me about at the dock: the albies. We flagged this setup two weeks running — said the water temp sitting right in that 68-to-72 degree band, combined with tides building back out of neap toward the August 12 new moon, was the classic recipe for the season's first false albacore and bonito. That water temperature is still exactly where it needs to be. The current strength is building. What I don't have yet is a confirmed fish in anybody's hands — no albie has crossed my gaff or anyone else's that I trust as of this week. But every piece of the puzzle Skinner and the boys down the beach are seeing — squid pushing into the Rhode Island and Nantucket Sound water, the temperature break setting up along the south side — points to this being the window. If I had one morning to burn looking for the first scout school, I'd be running the temp break off the south side beaches at first light with a Deadly Dick or a small Got-Cha plug rigged and ready, working from the Lighthouse east toward Shagwong, watching for diving terns and nervous water rather than committing blind.

Looking at the week ahead — tides are easing back off the full-moon peak toward neap, which historically slows the bass bite even further during daylight and pushes it harder into full dark. I'd fish bunker chunks and eels on the turn, work the rip during the bluefish window for guaranteed action, and keep one eye on the horizon for that first small, fast-moving school that doesn't look like anything else out there. That's usually how it starts — not a blitz, just three or four fish busting bait for thirty seconds before they vanish. When that happens, everything changes fast, and I intend to be on the water when it does.
