# Barnegat's bunker run finds the inlet — bass and blues stacked on the peanuts, fluke thinning out at Double Creek
*The Harvest Moon tides are doing exactly what big September tides are supposed to do — pushing bait, and stripers, hard toward the inlet.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — September 21** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

Forty years back, when I was mating out of Point Pleasant as a kid, the old captains had a saying about the Harvest Moon: "the moon pulls the bunker, and the bunker pulls everything else." I've watched that hold true on Barnegat Bay more Septembers than I can count, and this week it's holding true again. The peanut bunker have finally committed to the run south, funneling down through Double Creek Channel and out past Barnegat Light in numbers that got the whole fleet's attention by midweek.

The big spring tides that come with this moon are the engine behind it. More water moving means more bait getting flushed off the flats and out through the inlet, and predators know it. Striped bass have been shadowing those bunker schools tight to structure — the rock piles along the north side of the inlet, the deeper edges of Double Creek where it necks down before spilling into the ocean side, and the bulkheads and bridge pilings up toward Oyster Creek where warm water still holds. Bluefish have piled in right behind them, and by Thursday there were blitzes visible from the Route 72 causeway bridge on the last two hours of the outgoing, gulls working bait balls no bigger than a kitchen table but loud enough to hear from the road.

We flagged this pattern last week — said the fluke bite would keep fading while bait-driven bluefish action picked up, and that's exactly the shape of things now. The bluefish have gone from scattered snappers to genuine cocktail-to-medium blues mixing into the bunker schools, and that intensity is only going to build as more bait gets pushed south through the system over the next several days.

Fluke are still around, but the bite has thinned out noticeably from where it sat two weeks ago. Double Creek Channel, which carried the bay's best keeper fluke action most of the summer, is producing more shorts than keepers now on the drift, mostly on white Gulp Swimming Mullets tipped on 3/4-ounce bucktails worked slow along the channel edges in 10 to 14 feet. The keepers that are still showing — a few to 5 pounds reported out of the deeper bends near the ICW markers — are coming on the last of the incoming, right before the tide turns and the current slackens enough for the fish to sit up off bottom. Anybody chasing summer flounder this week should treat it as a bonus fish, not the main event, and be ready to move if the drift goes two passes without a bite.

Striped bass are the story right now, and the better fish are keying on live bunker fished on fishfinder rigs — an inch or two of leader, a single 4/0 to 6/0 circle hook, snap-weighted just enough to hold bottom in the current around the inlet rocks and the channel edges off Barnegat Light. Some of the mates working out of the inlet marinas are also doing well snap-jigging bunker-imitation swimbaits — 6-inch paddletails in bunker or white — through the edges of the blitzes rather than casting straight into the boil, since the biggest fish tend to sit just off the边 edge of the surface chaos waiting on cripples. Slot fish in the high 20s to low 30-inch range have been the bulk of it, with a few better fish mixed in on the dawn tide changes.

Blowfish are still around the docks and bulkheads on Oyster Creek and up toward Toms River, biting small on squid strips fished on tiny hooks under a split shot — good action for anybody who wants an easy evening and a fryer full of tails, though numbers are thinning as the water starts its slow slide toward fall temperatures. Kingfish have been intermittent on the ocean side beaches around Island Beach State Park, mostly on bloodworm rigs in the wash on the last of the ebb, but that bite has never been the reliable one on this stretch and this week is no exception.

Wind has cooperated more than not — a few blustery afternoons out of the northwest kept the smaller skiffs tucked up in the back bay, but mornings have mostly laid down enough to work the inlet safely on the tide changes. Water color has stayed reasonably clean given how much current has been moving; the bay hasn't turned to chocolate milk the way it sometimes does when these big tides really rip.

Looking ahead, the next several days should keep building on this pattern rather than breaking it. The Harvest Moon tides don't fall off a cliff — they ease down gradually, and as long as that happens, expect the bunker schools to keep getting squeezed toward the inlet and the striper and bluefish activity to keep concentrating there on the tide changes. If a real cold front finally pushes through late in the week, watch for the fluke bite to tighten up further and for more of the striper class fish to start showing outside the inlet on the open beach, working bunker schools along Island Beach. Until then, the smart money is fishing peanut bunker on the last two hours of whichever tide lines up with first or last light, working the inlet structure and Double Creek's deeper bends, and keeping a rod rigged with a bucktail ready for whenever the gulls start working.
