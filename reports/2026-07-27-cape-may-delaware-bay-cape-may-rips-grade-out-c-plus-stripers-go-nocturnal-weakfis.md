# Cape May Rips Grade Out C-Plus: Stripers Go Nocturnal, Weakfish Finally Show Some Teeth
*The ocean side is a daytime chore with a nighttime payoff, while Delaware Bay's back channels are quietly turning into the better bet this week.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 27** — _by Capt. Kate Brennan, Zone Writer_

Grading the Rips this week, I'd call it a C-plus. Fishable, not easy, and you're paying for every fish with a strong back and a willingness to fish the clock instead of the calendar. Daytime water on the ocean side is sitting in that low-to-mid 70s range that pushes stripers off the top and down onto structure, and once that happens in late July, they don't come back up until the sun does. I ran the Rips proper Tuesday morning on the tail of the outgoing — 18 to 35 feet, working bucktails tight to bottom over the humps — and picked at fish, nothing you'd brag about at the dock. The real work has shifted to last light and after dark. Eels drifted on three-way rigs along the current seams off Cape May Point, and bunker chunks fished on the bottom in 25 to 40 feet through the new dark hours, are where the keeper-class fish are coming from. That's not me sugarcoating it — that's just where the fish are living right now, and it lines up with what the whole coast is doing this time of year. If you want stripers in the Rips in July, you fish the night tide, full stop.

Fluke has softened up in the same way. The open sand inside Hereford Inlet that was holding fish in June has gone quiet as that shallow water heats up faster than the fish want to tolerate. What's still working is the harder structure — the mouth of Hereford, the deeper bends along the inlet channel, and out toward the reef sites where you've got current moving and cooler water underneath it. Four- and five-inch white and chartreuse Gulp on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow through 25 to 45 feet on the last of the incoming, is still putting keeper fluke in the box, just not in the numbers we had a month back. If you're used to drifting the flats, adjust — go find the edges.

Now for the story I actually want to tell you about. We flagged this last week — said weakfish activity in Delaware Bay would keep climbing as the water held steady — and that's exactly what's happening. The back channels from Fortescue up toward Brandywine Shoal have pushed into the mid-70s, and instead of killing the bite like it does with fluke, it's concentrated the weakfish into the deeper channel bends where the tide keeps oxygen moving. First light and dusk are the windows — I had two solid mornings this week working soft plastics, four-inch paddletails in pearl and chartreuse on light jigheads, bounced slow along the channel drop-offs in 15 to 22 feet on the front half of the incoming. Weakfish to 20 inches, mixed with a few shorts, and enough consistency that I'm calling this a real pattern, not a one-day fluke. Bucktails tipped with a strip of squid worked the same water just as well on the dusk bite. If this keeps building through the first week of August the way it's trending, Fortescue's going to be the better half of my two-bay game for a while.

Black drum, if you're wondering — done. That run wrapped up back in May like it always does, and anybody still soaking bunker chunks off Fortescue looking for drum in July is fishing a calendar that already closed. I get the question every week this time of year and the answer's always the same: come back to me in April.

Kingfish and croaker are steady, unglamorous, and exactly what they should be for late July — bloodworms and Fishbites fished on the bottom off the Wildwood bulkheads and the ocean beaches on the incoming tide keep putting fish in coolers for the families who don't want to run offshore. It's not a headline bite but it's a reliable one, and reliable counts for something in a summer where the bigger fish are making you work nights and edges for everything.

Bluefish are trickling through on bunker pods along the beachfront and into Hereford, giving surfcasters intermittent windows rather than sustained blitzes. When the bunker balls get pinned against the jetty rocks on a moving tide, you'll see the blow-ups, but they're short and scattered rather than the kind of sustained chop we sometimes get in August. Worth having a metal lure rigged and ready, not worth planning your whole morning around.

Moon's building toward full in the first days of August, which should sharpen the current through the Rips and Hereford both — more push on the tide usually means a tighter bite window but a better one when it hits. My plan for the coming week is simple: keep the daytime hours on fluke around the harder structure, shift to the night tide for stripers in the Rips, and lean into the weakfish pattern in the bay while it's building instead of waiting for someone else to confirm it first. If the bay water holds where it's at and doesn't spike further, I'd expect weakfish to keep climbing into next week. If a cold push or a big blow rearranges things, that's a different conversation — but right now, that's where I'd put my time.
