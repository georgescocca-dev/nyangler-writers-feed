# Great Point Rip: Bluefish Boiling on Top, Bass Working the Seam Underneath
*The birds gave it away before the rod did — and Nantucket Sound's bonito window is closing in fast.*

**NANTUCKET, MA — August 7** — _by Charlie St. Pierre, Zone Writer_

Watched a wall of terns fold up and drop on the outside edge of the Great Point rip on the tail of the ebb this week, and that's usually all the invitation you need. When the birds stack that tight over that piece of water, it's not bait skittering — it's bait getting eaten from underneath, and sure enough the surface behind them went white with bluefish choppers working a pod of sand eels against the current seam. That's the giveaway with this rip: the blitz on top is bluefish, showing themselves, splashy and obvious. The stripers are doing their business quieter, sitting deeper on the drop-off where the moving water piles the bait against the sandbar, and you find them by working a bucktail or a swimming plug down through the wash instead of chucking topwater into the chaos everyone else is casting at.

That's been the read across the Sound the last several days — bluefish are the loud fish right now, bass are the patient fish, and the two are stacked on the same real estate more often than not. Great Point on the last two hours of the ebb into the start of the flood has been the most consistent version of that pattern, with white water forming off the rip and holding bait against it. A 3/4-ounce bucktail with a white or chartreuse curl-tail trailer, worked along bottom on the up-current side, has been picking up stripers in the mid-20s to low-30s while poppers and Danny plugs stay busy with blues on top. Nothing enormous, but steady, and that's worth something when a lot of the Sound has gone quiet in the afternoon heat.

Monomoy has been fishing the way it usually does when the wind sits in the southwest — which it has, on and off, through this stretch of stable high pressure. That's the same setup we flagged a few days back, and it's held: steady weather, steady bunker and sand eel schools, and violent surface feeds wherever those bait pods bunch up along current lines. The rips on the south side of Monomoy, especially around the elbow near the Powder Hole, have been throwing up short, sharp windows of surface activity on the last of the incoming, with bluefish crashing bait pushed against the shoal edge. Bass are there too but they're not always up top — swimming a Sebile or a soft-plastic paddletail through the deeper trough on the drift, letting it sink before the retrieve, has been the better way to find the bigger fish that are holding under the visible show.

Fluke have been steady but unspectacular on the Horseshoe Shoal drift and around the edges of the channel off Monomoy, mostly fish in the 16 to 19-inch range with the occasional keeper mixed in on a white Gulp swimming mullet tipped with squid strip, worked slow over the sandy bottom in 15 to 25 feet. It's not a bite that's going to fill a cooler fast, but it's consistent enough to fill an afternoon between rip runs, and the water there has stayed clean even when wind has kicked up chop elsewhere on the Sound.

Now for the fish everyone keeps asking about. No confirmed bonito over Nantucket Shoals yet — the water's been climbing steadily and the broader corridor from Buzzards Bay through the Vineyard and out toward Block Island has been closing in on the temperature range that usually flips the switch, which is exactly what was flagged as imminent earlier this week. That call still stands. It hasn't happened here yet, but the ingredients are lining up: warm surface water pushing into the high 60s and low 70s in the shallower stretches, sand eels and bay anchovies thick enough to hold bluefish and bass, and stable high-pressure weather that isn't scattering the bait schools around. That's the exact recipe that precedes the first bonito showing over the Shoals, and it's worth rigging a few small metal jigs — half-ounce Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — and keeping them within reach on any trip out toward Great Point or the eastern edge of the Shoals over the next week or two. Albies are further behind that curve; historically they trail the bonito push by a couple weeks in this water, so don't expect Great Point's classic albie blitz just yet, but the pieces are moving in the right direction.

Chatham inlet has quietly been the most reliable striper spot after dark, with eels worked slow along the inlet's current edge on the dropping tide producing better-than-average fish, some pushing into the upper 30s. That bite doesn't need a blitz or birds to work — it just needs current, darkness, and patience.

Looking ahead, if the high pressure holds through the coming week the way it has, expect the same pattern to persist: bluefish loud on top, bass patient underneath, fluke steady on the flats, and everyone on the Shoals checking their temperature gauge for that first sign of bonito. If a cold front rolls through and stirs the water, that timeline could slip. But with conditions holding the course they're on, I'd keep the metal rigged and the eyes on the birds — because when the bonito show, they'll show fast, and the rip that's holding blues today could be holding something faster tomorrow.
