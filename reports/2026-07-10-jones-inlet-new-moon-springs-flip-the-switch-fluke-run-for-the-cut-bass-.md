# New moon springs flip the switch: fluke run for the cut, bass bite the sunset flood
*Jones Inlet's outgoing water is stacked with fluke pushed off the hot bay, and the first real PM striper window since June's full moon just reopened.*

**JONES INLET, NY — July 10** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay did what overheated bay water always does in July — it pushed the good fish out. Great South Bay and the flats behind Jones Beach have been running close to 79 degrees, which is bathwater for fluke, and they've been sliding off those skinny flats and into the channels, the inlet mouth, and the ocean side where the water's actually breathing. We had a stretch of light southwest wind most of last week that kept the bay glassy and warm, and honestly it made for pretty water and slow fishing if you stayed shallow. The guys who moved out to Sloop Channel and the inlet cut did fine. The guys who stayed on the flats got a tan and not much else.

This week the moon's the story. We're building into new moon springs — the tide swings are getting bigger, the current's ripping harder through the inlet, and that's exactly what flushes bait and repositions fish. More important for surf guys and inlet regulars: the PM flood is now lining up with sunset. That's the first evening high-tide window we've had since the June full moon, and it runs strongest Friday through Monday, July 10-13, with the tightest alignment right around the 11th and 12th. When the flood tide peaks as the light's dropping, bass move up into the inlet and along the Jones Beach bars to feed in that low-light window before full dark. That's not a maybe — that's a pattern I've fished for fifteen years out here, and the tide charts back it up this week better than they have in a month.

Fluke first, because that's been the more dependable bite. With the bay pushing 78-79 and the ocean side running cooler — call it low 70s where the upwelled water is mixing in through the inlet — the fish are stacking on that thermal break. Sloop Channel edges, the deeper bends off the Meadowbrook flats, and straight through the inlet cut itself on the outgoing tide have all been holding fish. Best window is two hours either side of the turn to outgoing, when the current's moving enough to make bucktails work but hasn't turned into a freight train. I've been dragging 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 5-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets right along bottom through the deeper cuts, slow enough to feel the bottom change. Fish have run mixed — plenty of shorts, but a decent number of 19-22 inch keepers mixed in, with one 26-incher reported off the Jones Beach inlet rocks on a squid-and-spearing combo fished on a high-low rig. It's not a wide-open bite. You're covering water and culling through shorts to find your keepers, but that's summer fluking on this coast and it's better than what the bay flats gave up last week.

Stripers have been the more interesting development. Resident fish never really left after the spring run, but they've been quiet through the heat, holding deep and feeding mostly at night. With this sunset-flood alignment kicking in, I expect that to change through the weekend. The play is the inlet mouth itself — working the rocks on the Jones Beach side and drifting bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig into the current seam where the outgoing meets incoming structure. If you'd rather cast, live-lining bunker where you can find it, or working a bucktail with a curly-tail trailer through the wash at last light, has been productive for the guys putting in the hours. Don't expect blitz conditions — this is a July resident bite, not the fall bunker run — but a 15 to 20 pound fish in that inlet current on the right stage of tide is a real possibility this weekend, and worth losing some sleep over.

Bluefish have been mixed in with the fluke drift and occasionally show in short-lived blitzes off the inlet on bait pods pushed out by the tide. Nothing sustained, but if you see birds working the rips off Short Beach, throw metal — Deadly Dicks or Hopkins in the 2-3 ounce range have been getting hit fast when the blues show.

Weakfish is where I have to be straight with you. The water's been warm enough, and warm enough long enough, that on paper this should be a weakfish summer in South Oyster Bay and around the Freeport creeks. The reality on the water hasn't caught up to the thermometer. A few fish have turned up — one 26-inch, 7.3-pound weakfish came out of the bay on a light bucktail worked slow along a channel edge — but it's still one-off stuff, not a pattern you can plan a trip around yet. If you want to chase them, fish the dusk incoming around the Wantagh Bridge pilings and the deeper holes off Freeport with bucktails tipped with bloodworm or chartreuse soft plastics, but go in expecting a long shot, not a sure thing.

Porgies remain the honest bread-and-butter right now — the rockpiles around the inlet and the reef structure off Jones Beach are stacked with them, and a simple hi-lo rig with clam will keep a rod bent while you wait on bigger things. Blackfish season's closed through mid-August, so leave the tog gear home for now.

Looking ahead: if that sunset-flood window performs the way the tide charts suggest, Saturday and Sunday evening at the inlet mouth is where I'm putting my time. Backup plan is the Sloop Channel fluke drift on the outgoing if the bass don't cooperate — that bite's been consistent enough to trust. Keep an eye east too; bonito and Spanish mackerel have started showing at the eastern inlets on the incoming tide, and if that warm plume keeps sliding west the way it's been forecast, Jones could see its first fish of the year before the month's out.
