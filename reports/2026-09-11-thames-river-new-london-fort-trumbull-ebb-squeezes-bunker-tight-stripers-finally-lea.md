# Fort Trumbull Ebb Squeezes Bunker Tight — Stripers Finally Lean Into the Push
*The new-moon flow through the Thames did exactly what it should this week, and the river's first real fall commitment showed up right on schedule.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — September 11** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

Stand on the seawall at Fort Trumbull on a hard ebb right now and you can watch the current do the work for you. The seam off the fort's point is running fast and clean, bending around the old battery rock and dragging bait straight into the throat of the harbor mouth. That seam has been the tell all week — bunker pods getting pinned against the rocks on the last three hours of the drop, and stripers the kind of commitment that's been missing since August. We flagged this last week — said the new-moon tide would tighten the bait and push the bass from picking at stragglers into real feeding — and that's exactly what's playing out in the river system this week.

Water at New London is holding around 69 degrees, which is still warm enough to keep fish comfortable but cool enough that the bunker schools are bunching up defensively instead of spreading loose across the flats. That's the whole story right now. When bait tightens, predators commit, and the September 10 new moon gave this stretch of the Sound the strongest tidal push it's had all season. The flow through the Gold Star Bridge pilings has been noticeably stronger on both stages, and that increased current is exactly what's herding peanut bunker and adult bunker alike into the pinch points — the Fort Trumbull rip, the mouth of the Thames, and the deeper cuts along Pleasure Beach.

The bite itself has been honest work, not a blitz yet, but real. Stripers from 24 to 34 inches have been coming off the pilings at Gold Star on the second half of the ebb, when the current really starts ripping through the structure and disorients the bait. Bucktails in the 1- to 1.5-ounce range, white or chartreuse, worked with a slow lift-and-drop right off the base of the pilings, have been the most consistent producer. A few boats working the deeper water off Fort Trumbull on the last two hours of the ebb have done better with swimming plugs — Bombers and SP Minnows in bunker patterns — worked with a slow retrieve through the seam where the fast water meets the slower back-eddy. That transition line is holding fish because it's where exhausted bait gets funneled and picked off.

Bluefish have been mixed in with the bass, mostly smaller harbor blues in the 2- to 4-pound range, but there's real reason to expect that to change fast. Snapper blues are thick in the back coves right now, up around the flats behind Ocean Beach and in the shallow water off Pleasure Beach, and that's historically the leading indicator for adult blues showing up in bigger numbers as the water keeps cooling. Montauk's derby fish this week — an 18-plus-pound bluefish — tells you the adult class is already staged and moving through eastern Long Island Sound. It's not unreasonable to expect some of that biomass to work its way into the Thames corridor over the next tide cycle or two, especially if the bunker keep tightening the way they have.

Hickory shad haven't shown in numbers yet, but that's not unusual for this point in September — that run tends to build through late month and into October as water continues to cool and the shad stage for their own migration. Worth watching the surface activity above the Gold Star Bridge on calm mornings; when the shad do show, they tend to give themselves away with nervous water and diving gulls before anyone marks them on a fishfinder.

Porgies remain the dependable secondary option while the fall pattern sorts itself out. The rockpiles off Pleasure Beach and the deeper structure inside the harbor entrance have been giving up steady numbers on sandworms and clam, fished on the bottom during the last of the ebb and the first hour of the flood when the current eases enough to hold bottom without a ton of lead. Nothing spectacular, but it's a reliable box-filler for anyone who wants action while waiting for the bigger fish to commit.

Looking at the next few days, the tidal push is still strong coming off this new moon, and that argues for staying with the pattern that's working — fish the last half of the ebb at Fort Trumbull and Gold Star, target the current seams where fast water meets slack, and don't be afraid to work the pilings tight on the drop. If the wind stays out of the north and holds light, expect the bite to hold steady or build slightly as more bunker get pushed into the river system over the coming tide cycles. If a southerly kicks up and dirties the harbor entrance, the better bet shifts inside to the upper harbor and the deeper holes off the Coast Guard Academy waterfront, where the water stays clearer longer.

The next real test is whether this turns into the first legitimate blitz activity of the fall run. The bait is tightening, the moon phase is right, and the water temperature is sitting in that sweet window where stripers stop sampling and start hunting in earnest. Worth keeping gear rigged and ready over the next several days — when this breaks open on the Thames, it tends to happen fast, and the fish don't stay stacked at the bridge or the fort for long once the tide starts easing off its new-moon high.
