# Why Won't the Bass Touch Anything After Sunrise? Absecon's Nighttime Answer
*Fluke keep the sod banks honest while stripers go strictly nocturnal in the warm water — here's the class lesson for the week.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — August 3** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I got the same question three times this week, from three different guys standing at three different rail bridges — Absecon, the Route 30 causeway, and one fella texting me from Longport. All three wanted to know the same thing: why are the stripers only hitting for that first gray half hour at dawn, and then nothing?

Here's the lesson. Surface temps along this stretch have settled into the low-to-mid 70s, and that's simply above the comfort range for daytime striper activity. These fish aren't gone — they're just smart. They've pulled off the flats and pushed into deeper, cooler refuge during the day and shifted to a mostly nocturnal feeding pattern. You want a shot at them right now, you fish the edges of the light — that first hour of gray at Brigantine inlet, or the last hour before full dark working the deeper holes off the Route 30 bridge pilings on a dropping tide. Bucktails in white or chartreuse, 3/4 to 1 ounce, worked slow along bottom. Daytime, forget it — you're better off putting that rod down and picking up the other one.

Because the other rod, the fluke rod, is where the real schoolwork is happening. The sod banks in Absecon and up through Great Egg Inlet continue to be the most dependable classroom on this beat. I had three trips this week working the drift along the edges of the Great Egg channel, 8 to 14 feet of water, moving bait — 4-inch chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets teamed with a 3/4-ounce bucktail — and it's a steady bite. Not a blitz, not a bend-the-rod-every-drift kind of week, but steady. Keepers mixed in with plenty of shorts, exactly the pattern I told you to expect. We flagged this last week — said the fluke bite would stay steady rather than blitz-level, with keepers mixed among the shorts on structure drifts — and that's precisely what's showing up on the sod banks day after day. The water's warm and stable, which keeps this bite dependable, but it also means you're culling through a lot of 15-inch fish to find your dinner. Best window has been the last two hours of the outgoing, when the bait gets funneled off those grass edges and the fluke stack up tight to the drop.

Inside the back bays proper — the channels behind Ocean City and up around Sea Isle — I'm hearing about a few weakfish mixed in with the fluke catches, mostly at first light on bull minnow fished on a simple fishfinder rig, bounced slow along the deeper holes near the ICW channel edges. Nothing to build a trip around, but if you're already out there working fluke structure, don't be shocked if a weakfish grabs your bait instead. Keep a net handy and handle them gently — they don't forgive rough treatment.

On the beachfront, the kingfish have been solid citizens all summer and this week is no different — small strips of bloodworm or Fishbites on a high-low rig, fished in the wash right off Ocean City and Stone Harbor, 2 to 4 feet of water, first couple hours after sunup. Reliable, unglamorous, good for a bucket and a kid who wants to feel something pull.

Now, the bluefish. There's a wave of them working down the Jersey coast right now, building off a strong push that started further north and has been gaining size as it comes south — I'd expect that pattern to keep extending into this stretch over the coming days. I haven't seen the blitz hit Absecon or Townsends yet in any consistent way, but the ingredients are there: warm surface water, bait pushed tight to the beach, and a hungry migratory class of blues working their way down. Keep an eye on the birds working tight over the surf line at dawn between Brigantine and Ocean City — that's usually the tell before the blues show in force. Metal — a 1-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — will out-produce plastic once they're in close and slashing.

On the triggerfish front, quiet for now — that's more of a late-summer, structure-and-warm-water pattern that tends to develop around Townsends and the reef edges once the water holds steady into late August. Worth checking again in a couple weeks, not this one.

Looking ahead: we're building toward the new moon, and tidal strength is going to pick up noticeably over the next several days. Stronger current on the sod banks should tighten up the fluke bite and concentrate bait along the drop-offs — that's good news for the drift program. If a cooling front pushes through behind that building tide, and there's some chance of that this week, it could reset the striper pattern back toward something closer to normal daytime feeding. I wouldn't plan a trip around that possibility yet, but if the wind swings out of the northwest and temps drop even a couple degrees, I'd be back on the bridge at first light instead of last light. Until then, class stays in session on the sod banks — that's where I'd spend my time.
