# Trolled the Wilmington Edge All Week — Yellowfin Steady, White Marlin Scattered but Real
*The warm-water setup that's held for three weeks isn't breaking down yet, and boats running ballyhoo spreads are still finding fish on the color change.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — August 27** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one trip left in the budget this week, troll before you chunk. That's the call coming off the docks in Cape May and Indian River right now, and it's the right one. The water sitting over Wilmington Canyon has held its warm, sterile-blue character for three straight weeks — no real breakdown, no cold intrusion pushing through to mess with it — and that kind of stability rewards covering ground with a spread before committing to an anchor-up chunk bite. Find the temp break first, mark bait on the meter, then decide whether the day turns into a drift-and-chunk operation.

The pattern holding this canyon together is the same one that's been in place since early August: a warm eddy has set up shop off the canyon's edge, and the blue water riding on top of it is about as clean as it gets this time of year. That kind of water isn't always loaded with life — it can look gorgeous and fish empty — but this stretch has been holding yellowfin consistently, with bluefin mixed in on the better days and white marlin working the temp lines on the troll. Boats aren't finding wall-to-wall numbers, but the ones running the edge with a full spread are putting fish in the box on most trips, which for late August is a fair trade for fuel burned.

High pressure has been steady, winds out of the southwest in the 8 to 12 knot range, which is about as comfortable as this stretch of water gets in summer. That's kept sea state manageable for the run out and let boats work the drop methodically instead of getting bounced off the troll early. If that pattern holds through the next couple days — and there's no strong signal it's about to change — expect the canyon to keep fishing close to how it has been: not a blitz, but a dependable multi-species day for boats willing to put in the time on the troll.

Tactically, the spreads that have been producing are straightforward. A dredge teaser run short off the transom, ballyhoo — naked and skirted both — worked in a standard pattern with a couple of daisy chains for teasers, and a spreader bar or two run long for bigger fish. Yellowfin have been coming on smaller skirted ballyhoo in green machine and pink/white combinations, fished 30 to 60 feet back where the water still shows some color transition rather than dead flat blue. White marlin have been more selective — they're showing on the naked ballyhoo pulled behind the dredge, and hookup ratios have been decent but not great, which tracks with a scattered rather than concentrated marlin push this month. Bluefin have come mixed in with the yellowfin on the better mornings, usually on the same spread, no special adjustment needed other than making sure drags are backed off enough to handle a bigger fish if one decides to eat.

Once boats find bait stacked and marked well on structure along the canyon wall, the switch to chunking has been worth it. Butterfish chunks fished on a slow-sink rig, no weight or just enough to get it under the slick, have drawn yellowfin up in the chum line on the calmer days. The key has been patience — let the slick build for twenty, thirty minutes before expecting results, and keep the chunks small and steady rather than dumping the bucket. Boats that rushed the chunk without a real bait presence on the meter mostly wasted drift time; the ones that waited for marks to show before setting up did better.

Mahi have been a bonus rather than a target this stretch, showing under whatever scattered weed lines boats have crossed running out to the drop. Nothing consistent enough to plan a day around, but worth a couple of casts with a small skirted bait or a bucktail if you cross a good mat of grass with the usual dolphin signs — peanut mahi flashing under the edges. Wahoo have been quieter than they were earlier in the summer; a couple of incidental fish on the high-speed lures run off the outriggers, but nobody's targeting them specifically right now and the numbers don't justify dedicating a rod to it yet.

Looking at the next few days, the setup holding this bite together shows no sign of breaking. Stable high pressure and light southwest wind should keep conditions fishable and the warm-water regime intact through the near-term window. That means the same game plan should keep working — troll the edge early, watch for the color change and bait marks, and be ready to switch to the chunk if the meter lights up. Where this gets more interesting is the moon building toward full over the next several days, which typically sharpens current and can tighten up bite windows right at the edges and breaks. If that plays out the way it usually does, expect shorter but more intense windows rather than an all-day grind — worth adjusting the day around first light and the last couple hours before the tide change rather than fishing the whole clock at the same intensity. Fuel math still favors this trip for boats willing to work the troll patiently before committing to anchor.
