# Where Did the Fluke Go? Ask the Bunker Schools at Absecon
*The fluke program is winding down in the sod banks just as the first real bass interest shows up on bait pushing through the inlets.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — September 7** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

Every September I get the same question from readers, usually phrased some version of: "The fluke bite dried up on me last week — what happened?" It's a fair question, and the answer is a lesson in how these back bays actually work. Fluke don't vanish. They stage. Right now they're pulling off the sod banks in Absecon and Great Egg and easing toward the deeper inlet channels ahead of their fall run offshore. That's not a slump, that's a calendar event, and it happens on a schedule you can set a watch by.

The bigger story this week is what's replacing that fluke attention in angler conversation at the ramps — bunker. Peanut bunker schools have been tightening up along the beachfront from Brigantine down through Ocean City, and under the full moon that just passed on the 6th and 7th, that bait got squeezed even harder. We flagged this exact setup last week — said the building moon toward full would tighten these schools and that the first legitimate blitz behavior of the fall was likely within the next week to ten days. That's precisely what's forming up now at Absecon Inlet and along the Great Egg jetty complex, where guys are starting to see nervous water and the occasional swirl that isn't a bluefish.

Water conditions are cooperating. Surf temps are sitting in that low-to-mid 70s range that's comfortable for both the tail end of the summer species and the first push of migratory bass working down from the north. That's a workable window, not a guarantee — the bass showing interest right now are mostly still picking at the edges of these bait schools rather than committing to a full-on blitz. Give it a few more nights of cooling air temps and a tightening moon cycle, and that picture should sharpen.

For fluke, the pattern hasn't fully closed the book yet. The sod banks from Absecon back through the Brigantine meadows are still giving up keeper fish on the last two hours of the outgoing, when bait gets funneled off the banks into the deeper guts. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the channel edges in 8 to 12 feet, are still the most consistent producer. Live bait guys soaking peanut bunker or spot on a simple fishfinder rig off the bottom in the same depth are picking off a few better-grade fish mixed in with the shorts. Expect that bite to keep tapering through the rest of September as water temps ease and fish start their push toward the inlets and out to deeper structure.

Bluefish have been the more dependable player all week. They're working the same bunker schools that have the stripers interested, and they're not being shy about it. Ocean City and Sea Isle City surf anglers throwing metal — Kastmasters and Hopkins spoons in the 1- to 2-ounce range — have had steady action in the first hour of light and again at dusk, when the bait pushes tight to the beach. If you want bluefish specifically, follow the diving birds; they'll put you on the school faster than any chart.

Weakfish have been a quieter mention this week, mostly a incidental catch for guys working the deeper holes in Townsends Inlet and around the Stone Harbor flats on the last of the ebb. Nothing to build a whole trip around right now, but worth a bucktail-and-Gulp combo bounced along bottom if you're already in that stretch working fluke.

Kingfish have shown up in decent numbers in the surf from Ocean City down to Stone Harbor, which is normal for this time of year as the sand fleas and small crabs get active in the wash. Bloodworms or Fishbites on a high-low rig in the first trough will take them steady, and they make a nice bonus fish while you're waiting on bigger action.

Looking at the next few days, the tide and moon phase both argue for patience paying off at the inlets. If the bunker stay stacked and the nights keep cooling the way they have been, the smart move is working Absecon and Great Egg on the last two hours of the outgoing, right where bait funnels out of the back bay into the main channel — that's where predators set up to ambush rather than chase. Cast bucktails or swim shads across the current seam and let it sink before working it back. If the blitz activity that's been building finally breaks loose, it's going to happen right there first, before it ever reaches the open beach. Best case, that full moon push holds the bait tight through the weekend and the first real striper blitz of the fall shows itself at one of these inlet mouths. If the bait scatters instead, shift back to the sod banks and squeeze the last of the fluke season out of the outgoing tide while you can.
