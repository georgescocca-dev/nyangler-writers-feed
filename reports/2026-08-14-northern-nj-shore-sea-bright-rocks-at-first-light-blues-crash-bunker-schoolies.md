# Sea Bright Rocks at First Light: Blues Crash Bunker, Schoolies Ride the Edges
*Bunker pods pinned tight to the beach are turning the jetty country into a blitz alarm — the albies are still a no-show, but everything else about this week says they're close.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — August 14** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

5:20 a.m., low tip of the Sea Bright rocks, and there were already four guys spread out over structure I usually have to myself this time of year. Nobody said much. You don't need to when the water in front of you is boiling with peanut bunker trying to climb out of the ocean. Bluefish underneath them doing what bluefish do — not subtle about it. One guy was throwing a bunker spoon on 40-pound braid, getting bit on almost every retrieve. Another had a wooden darter, worked slow through the froth, and came up with a bluefish that had a school bass practically riding shotgun behind it. That's the story right now up and down this stretch — the blues are the headline, but the stripers are working the margins of the same meal, and neither one is being shy about it.

This is the pattern we flagged last week — bunker pods pushed tight to the sand, and wherever they show, the tide change lights the fuse. It's playing out almost exactly like that at Sea Bright, at the north end of Monmouth Beach, and off the jetties below Long Branch. The bite windows are short and violent, usually the last hour of the outgoing and the first half hour of the new incoming, and then it shuts off like someone flipped a switch until the bait regroups. If you're fishing it, don't waste time on the slack. Get there for the tide change and be ready to move down the beach if the pod slides.

The swell's been an annoyance more than a dealbreaker. Ocean's been running close to four feet at five seconds out front, which makes the outside face of the Sea Bright rocks a good way to lose your footing and your rod in the same motion. I've been working the calmer pockets — the lee side of the jetties, the washes right at the base where the white water settles — rather than climbing all the way out to the tip. Bass have been sitting in that same wash, picking off stunned bunker, and you can cover it just fine with a Super Strike swimmer or a 3-ounce Ava jig without risking your neck on wet rock.

Down at Manasquan Inlet, the story shifts a little. Less blitz chaos, more steady grinding — which for August is honestly a fair trade. The jetty on the north side has been giving up bluefish to 4 pounds on bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig in about 10 to 12 feet at the mouth, best on the last two hours of the incoming when the current's still got some push but hasn't gone slack. Fluke fishing inside the inlet has been steady rather than spectacular — 3-to-6 pound Gulp Swimming Mullets in white and pink shad, bounced along the channel edge in 15 to 20 feet on the drift from the Point down toward the Coast Guard station. Nothing to write home about size-wise most days, but there's real doormat potential in this corridor right now — the same trophy fluke pattern that's been showing up down toward Raritan Bay has fish in the high single digits turning up on bucktails tipped with a strip of squid worked slow along bottom structure. Worth a few extra drifts before you give up on a spot.

Shark River Inlet has been the porgy story this week — bloodworms and clam on a bottom rig off the Belmar side jetty and the Route 35 bridge rocks, decent numbers of 10-to-12-inch fish stacked up in 8 to 15 feet on the last of the outgoing. Snapper blues are thick in the back of that inlet too, which is a good early sign — those fish grow up fast, and by mid-September the adult class behind them tends to show in force.

Blackfish are still weeks off — water's too warm and the season's not close — but I mention it because guys keep asking. Not yet. Patience.

As for false albacore — nothing. We said the new moon on the 12th would be the trigger to watch, and the tide's building the way it should through this current cycle, but nobody I've talked to on the rocks or at the bait shop has laid eyes on a single fin. The ingredients are all sitting there — warm water, bait stacked tight, sharper current — and that combination has a way of producing albies out of nowhere once it does. I'd keep a bag of Deadly Dicks and small metal in the truck for the next couple weeks regardless. Spring Lake jetties and the water off Belmar are typically where this stretch sees its first fish if they show at all, usually working the tide lines just off the rocks in the early morning glass.

Looking ahead, I don't expect much to change through the weekend — bunker's still holding tight to the beach, blues are still going to crash it on the tide changes, and the bass will keep riding the edges of that chaos rather than driving it themselves. That's an early-season pattern more than a late-August one, honestly, and it's got me thinking this could be the leading edge of fish starting to stage for the fall run a little ahead of schedule. If the swell backs off even a foot, I'd get out to the tip of the Sea Bright rocks on the dawn outgoing and work a bunker spoon through the froth before the sun gets too high. That's where I'll be.
