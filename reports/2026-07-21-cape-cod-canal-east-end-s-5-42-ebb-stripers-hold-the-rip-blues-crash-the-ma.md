# East End's 5:42 Ebb: Stripers Hold the Rip, Blues Crash the Mackerel
*A softening neap current is stretching the bite window at both ends of the Canal, and the night shift is carrying the load.*

**BOURNE, MA — July 21** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

5:42 AM, East End, current running maybe 3.8 knots on the ebb — down a full knot and a half from what I clocked on the last new moon two weeks back. That's your first-quarter neap talking, and if you've fished this ditch as long as I have you know exactly what that number means: softer push, longer window, fish spread out a little more instead of getting pinned against the rip in a tight 90-minute burst. That's the story of this week on the Canal, and it's a decent one if you're willing to work around it instead of fighting it.

The East End rip off the Herring Run and down toward the Cribbon revetment has been the most consistent thing going, but only on the two bookend tides — the last hour of the dawn ebb and the first hour of the dusk flood. Stripers are stacked on the current seam where the outflow meets the bay water, and they're eating mackerel-pattern swimmers hard. I've had guys doing well on 7-inch Bomber Long A's in mackerel and on the Gibbs Danny-style poppers worked with a slow subsurface wobble right through the seam. Bluefish are mixed right in on the same water — some real cocktail-to-3-pound blues busting bait on top mid-tide, which tells you there's still mackerel and silversides getting pushed through the ditch on that current. If you see the birds working tight over the rip instead of scattered, that's your green light — get a bucktail or a Deadly Dick down through it on the drop before the blues shred it, then work topwater once the sun's fully up.

West End's been the sneaky better bite for guys willing to fish it after dark, and this is exactly what we flagged last week — that night fishing was going to carry the Canal through the heat of summer, and that's precisely what's happening. Water temp in the ditch is holding cooler than the bay flats on either side, and the bass know it. They're sliding up onto the current seams off the Herring Run jetty and down toward the Days Beach side on the tail of the flood, laying in the eddies where the current curls behind the rocks. Eels — 10 to 12 inch, fished on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom without killing the swim — have been the most consistent producer from about an hour before to an hour after the top of the tide. Guys throwing bucktails, the white-and-chartreuse ¾-ounce with a curl-tail trailer, worked slow along bottom on the last two hours of the ebb, have been picking off keeper-class fish too, mostly in that 28 to 34 inch range with the occasional better one mixed in.

Fluke have been the honest bite for anyone who wants a daytime option while the stripers sulk through the bright hours. Mashnee flats and the channel edge off Hog Island have both been giving up decent numbers on the last two hours of the incoming, drifting Gulp Swimming Mullets in white on ½-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid when the current's really moving. Nothing giant — most fish 16 to 19 inches with a few pushing 20 — but it's steady enough to fill an afternoon when the sun's got the stripers locked up tight to structure and unwilling to eat.

Phinneys Harbor and the water off Wings Neck are still a bonito watch more than a bonito bite — nobody's iced one yet that I've heard of — but Buzzards Bay water is creeping into that range where they typically show, and I wouldn't be shocked to hear of the first scout fish in the next two to three weeks if this settled weather holds. Worth running a few casts of a small Deadly Dick or an epoxy jig through any nervous water or diving terns you see out there, just in case one shows early. No albies confirmed yet either, but that's the same clock — mid-to-late summer temperature break, and we're getting close.

Here's what I'd do this week: fish the bookend tides at the East End for the mackerel-and-blues combo, and save your best effort for a night session at the West End on the flood if you can manage the hours — that's where the better-class stripers have been living while the current's soft. The neap is going to keep easing through the next few days before the tide starts building back toward the full moon, which means the aggressive, short-window bite at both ends will stretch out a bit longer than usual — good news if your schedule doesn't line up perfectly with peak current, bad news if you're used to a hard 45-minute window and nothing else. Keep an eye on the surface for nervous bait and diving birds, because with mackerel still running through the ditch, that's where the next blitz is going to show. And don't sleep on fluke as your daytime fallback — with stripers mostly a low-light game right now, that channel edge bite off Hog Island is the difference between a slow trip and a good one.
