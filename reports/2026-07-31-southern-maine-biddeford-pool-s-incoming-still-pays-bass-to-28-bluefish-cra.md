# Biddeford Pool's Incoming Still Pays: Bass to 28, Bluefish Crashing Higgins
*Cooler Gulf of Maine water keeps stripers shallow and structure-bound while mackerel schools stack Pine Point and Wells Harbor.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — July 31** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Tuesday, first light, I'm standing on the ledge at the Pool watching that incoming current stack up against the rocks the way it's done for me a thousand mornings, and a 28-inch bass comes up under a black Danny plug like she's got a train to catch. That's the hour. Right there. Water pushing in past the bar, bait getting pinned against structure, and stripers stacking up to feed before the tide tops out and everything goes quiet. I've said it before and I'll say it till they bury me — the first hour of the incoming at Biddeford Pool is the best hour of striper fishing this state has to offer, and this week it proved it again.

Here's the thing about Southern Maine right now, and it's the reason we're still catching fish in daylight while guys south of the Cape are cursing warm water and fishing at midnight: our water hasn't cooked off the way theirs has. Down in the Sound and off Jersey they're pushing 70-plus degrees and shoving bass onto deep wrecks, fishing bunker chunks after dark just to get a bite. Up here the Gulf of Maine is still running cool enough that stripers are comfortable working structure in daylight — ledges, river mouths, current seams — on a normal, unhurried summer program. We flagged this pattern holding as long as the weather stayed stable and mild, and it has. That's exactly what's kept the Pool, the Saco River mouth, and the rocks off Cape Elizabeth fishing the way they should this time of year.

The bass themselves have been a mixed bag — plenty of schoolies in the 18 to 24-inch range mixed with better fish pushing 28 to 32 inches, and I heard of one over 34 taken on a live mackerel off Wood Island on the last of the outgoing. Nothing epic, nothing you'd call a slaughter, but steady enough that if you put in the time on the right stage of tide you're going home with something to show for it. Pencil poppers and Danny-style swimmers have been the ticket at first and last light around the Pool and the mouth of the Saco. Once the sun's up and the bite goes quiet, switch to bucktails — half-ounce to one-ounce white or chartreuse — worked slow along the bottom in the deeper cuts off Wood Island Harbor. That's where the bigger fish have been holding through the heat of the day, sulking on structure and waiting for the tide to turn again.

Bluefish are the other story this week, and it's a good one. Higgins Beach has had a couple of honest blitzes — peanut bunker and small mackerel getting pushed right up onto the sand on the last two hours of the incoming, blues busting through them like they're mad about something. I watched one Saturday evening that had guys reeling up half-bitten pogies for twenty straight minutes. Metal — Kastmasters, Deadly Dicks, anything silver you can chuck far and retrieve fast — has been the play. Don't overthink it. When they're up and crashing, match the retrieve speed to the panic in the bait and you'll get bit. Pine Point has seen the same bait pushing through on calmer mornings, though the blitzes there have been shorter and more scattered — more of a here-and-gone than a sustained run.

Prouts Neck has been a story of wind direction, same as it always is. A southwest breeze pushes bait and current right into that stretch of rock, and when it's blowing 10 to 15 out of the southwest, the outside ledges have been worth a soak with topwater at dawn. When the wind swings onshore or dies flat, the bite there dies with it — I fished it Thursday in a dead calm and managed one fish in three hours. Not every day's a winner out there, and I won't pretend otherwise.

Mackerel have been thick off Wells Harbor and Ogunquit, schools rolling through on the tide changes, and if you want bait for eels or live-lining, that's your spot — small Swedish spoons or a mackerel tree will fill a bucket in twenty minutes most mornings. Flounder fishing off Wells has been steady but unspectacular — bloodworms and clam bellies on the bottom in the channel edges near the harbor, mostly keeper-sized fish, nothing to write home about but a nice change of pace on a slow tide.

We're coming off the full moon tides and easing toward neap over the next several days, which usually softens the current push a little — expect the bite windows to shrink slightly and get more particular about exact tide stage. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd be back at the Pool on the first hour of an early incoming, throwing a pencil popper into the white water off the ledge and betting on that same push of current that's fed me for years. As we build back toward the August 12 new moon, look for that tide energy to return and the bait to reconcentrate — that's usually when Prouts Neck and the river mouths turn from decent to genuinely good.
