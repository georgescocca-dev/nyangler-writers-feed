# Mackerel Push Through the Shoals, Bass Tuck Into the Piscataqua on the Ebb
*Thirteen miles doesn't sound like much until you fish it right — and this week the Shoals and the river both delivered for those who know the tide.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — September 14** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People hear thirteen miles of coastline and figure New Hampshire is an afterthought between Massachusetts and Maine. They haven't fished the Shoals on a clean slack tide, and they haven't watched stripers stack up where the Piscataqua dumps into Portsmouth Harbor on a hard ebb. This state punches well above its weight if you know where the structure is and when the tide wants to be fished — and this week proved it again.

Water's cooling the way it's supposed to in mid-September, and that's the whole story right now. Mackerel schools have been working through the Isles of Shoals in loose, nervous pods, showing up on the sounder in 40 to 60 feet around the structure and then vanishing for a tide before showing back up. Where the mackerel go, the bluefish and bass follow, and that's exactly the pattern setting up right now — bait pushing tight to structure, predators shadowing it instead of just picking off stragglers. We flagged this last week — said stripers would start showing real interest as water temps kept dropping and bunker schools tightened up — and that's exactly what the fleet is seeing now, from the Harbor out to the river.

The striper bite has been best in two places: the mouth of Hampton Harbor on the last two hours of the outgoing, and up in the Piscataqua where the current squeezes past Portsmouth Harbor and the old Navy Yard pilings. Both spots are doing the same thing — funneling bait through a narrow gap and letting the bass sit in the seam and wait. Live eels drifted on a fish-finder rig have been the most consistent producer in the river, fished right on the bottom where the current breaks around the pilings. In the surf at Great Boars Head, guys throwing bunker chunks on a slow incoming tide have picked at slot fish in the last hour before dark, though it's been more of a pick than a blitz — a fish every twenty minutes rather than a wall of them. That's honest: the bite is building, not exploding yet. The bigger commitment from migratory bass usually shows up here another week or two behind the southern New England run, once the water eases further and more bait gets flushed out of the back bays.

Bluefish have thinned out compared to August, which is normal for this time of year, but there are still pockets of them working the mackerel schools right off the Shoals. Anyone trolling small tube-and-worm rigs or casting metal — a 1-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — into visible surface activity around White Island and the outer ledges has had shots at bluefish in the 3 to 6 pound class mixed with the occasional better striper. It's not a blitz fishery right now, more a matter of running to visible birds and bait and getting a few casts in before it moves.

The better story for those willing to make the run to the Shoals is groundfish. Cod have started showing on the classic humps and ledges in 80 to 120 feet, and this is the time of year when that bite starts to build seriously as water cools and bait stacks up on structure. Jigging diamond jigs and 6-to-10 ounce bucktails tipped with a strip of squid on the slack around the tide change has been the most efficient way to connect — cod don't like fighting hard current, so the twenty-minute window on either side of the turn is when the rod tips have been loading up. Fish in the 4 to 8 pound range have been the norm, with a scattering of bigger fish mixed in for boats willing to work the deeper pockets off the back side of the islands. Haddock have been more hit-or-miss — a few boats picking at them in the same depth range, but nothing consistent enough yet to call it a pattern. That usually tightens up as we move deeper into fall.

Flounder fishing has stayed steady but unspectacular in the Hampton estuary and up in Rye Harbor, where blackbacks are holding on mud bottom in the deeper holes. Standard flounder rigs with squid strips or clam bellies fished on the bottom during slower current have kept a few coolers respectable, though this is more a bonus fishery than a target trip right now.

Looking at the next few days: the moon is building, which means more current push through the river and around the Shoals — good news for anyone working current seams for bass or working the slack windows at the Shoals for cod, since bigger tidal swings tend to concentrate bait harder in the funnels. If the wind stays out of the northwest and lays down overnight, early morning runs to the Shoals should stay fishable and the mackerel-and-bluefish pattern should hold. If it swings onshore and kicks up a chop, the smarter move is to stay inside — work the Piscataqua on the ebb and Hampton Harbor on the last of the outgoing, where the structure blocks most of the wind-driven mess anyway. Either way, the next week should keep building on what's already started: cooling water, tightening bait, and stripers finally starting to show real conviction instead of just picking at scraps. That's the pattern to lean into before the better fall run truly arrives.
