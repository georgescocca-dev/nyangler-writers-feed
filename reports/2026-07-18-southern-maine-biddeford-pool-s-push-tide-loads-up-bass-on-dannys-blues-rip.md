# Biddeford Pool's Push Tide Loads Up — Bass on Dannys, Blues Ripping Higgins
*The incoming at the Pool is still the best hour in Maine surf fishing, and this week it proved it twice.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — July 18** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Tuesday night, second hour of the incoming at Biddeford Pool, I put a black Danny plug across the current seam off the east side and had it swept about four feet before something hammered it like it owed money. Thirty-four inches, fat as a football, gill plates flared under the headlamp. That's the Pool doing what the Pool does when the tide starts stacking bait against the rocks — and right now it's doing it better than anywhere between Kittery and Cape Elizabeth.

Here's why. South of the Cape, water's pushed into the low-to-mid 70s and the stripers down there have gone nocturnal — thermal retreat, not disappearance, but it's made them ghosts by daylight. We don't have that problem up here. Gulf of Maine influence keeps our water cooler and better oxygenated, and the bass know it. They're pushing hard into the York River and the Kennebunk River mouths on the tide, chasing mackerel and peanut menhaden right through daylight hours, which for a surf guy means you don't have to burn your whole night to get bit. We called this one last week — said the striper bite up here would carry through August behind that cooler Gulf water, and three trips out this week backed it up. Not lights-out, but consistent: two to four fish a session for anyone willing to walk the rocks and read the current.

Biddeford Pool remains ground zero. The pattern hasn't changed in twenty years and it hasn't changed this week either — bait gets pinned against the ledges on the incoming, current wraps around the point, and bass stack up in that seam like they're waiting for a bus. I'm still throwing the black Danny early and late, switching to a bone Gibbs pencil popper when there's any surface commotion — and there's been some, small blitzes of school bass chasing mackerel right up against the rocks around the top two hours of the flood. Wear studded boots. That bottom doesn't forgive.

Higgins Beach has been the bluefish address all week, and it's the one spot where I'd tell you to show up mid-morning instead of dawn. Blues have been stacking under bunker pods pushed tight to the sandbar, and when the tide's moving and the wind's calm, they'll blow up right in the wash. Same story we flagged with the bluefish — they're going to keep following that bait up the coast as long as this settled weather holds, and Higgins has been the proof. Tins are the move: 1-2 oz Deceivers or Kastmasters in chrome, worked fast through the froth. Bring a wire leader or you'll donate half your box. Bluefish teeth don't care how nice your leader knot is.

Pine Point and Prouts Neck have been more of a grind. Southwest wind the last few days has put a little push on the surf at Prouts, and that's actually helped — stirred up the sand fleas and greenheads that flounder feed on in the guts between the rocks. If you're bottom fishing there, bloodworms or sea worms on a simple hi-lo rig in eight to fifteen feet, right where the sandy pockets meet ledge, has been worth the trip for a mixed bag of flounder with the occasional keeper bass mixed in on the same rig fished tight to structure. Wells Harbor's been quieter — some school bass working the channel on the last of the ebb, nothing to write home about, but a decent bet for someone who wants an easy evening with light tackle and a bucktail.

Cod's still mostly a boat game this time of year — the guys running out to the deeper water off Cape Elizabeth and the ledges past Wood Island are picking at them in 60-90 feet on diamond jigs and clams, but it's slow, technical fishing, not a surf story. If you've got a boat and patience, it's there. If you're standing on rock throwing plugs, don't bother chasing it.

Moon's swinging toward first quarter on the 21st, which means the big spring push we had is easing off into more moderate tidal current. That's actually good news for surf guys — those wild, ripping tides make wading the Pool's ledges a white-knuckle game, and the softer stages coming should let you fish the incoming a little longer without getting run off the rocks. I'd plan around that second hour of the flood at Biddeford Pool as your best window through the weekend, and if the wind stays out of the southwest and doesn't kick up too much chop, Prouts Neck should keep producing that mixed bottom bite.

Look, it's not a blitz every night and I'm not going to sell it as one. Some sessions this week I threw for an hour before anything touched the plug. But the pattern's real — cooler water, bait pushed into the river mouths and against the rocks, bass feeding by day instead of hiding by night. That's a gift this time of year, and Southern Maine's cashing it in while the rest of the coast waits for dark.
