# Sea Girt Reef in 68 feet: sea bass to 4 pounds, bluefin working the Mud Hole thermocline
*The bottom bite is steady money right now — the tuna bite is real but you have to find the temperature seam, and it's moving.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 31** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

Sea Girt Reef, 68 feet of water, four and a half miles off the inlet. That's the number I gave three different boats this week and all three came back with sea bass in the box — some pushing four pounds, most a solid fourteen to sixteen inches, which tells me the population out there is mature and it's not thinning out the way it usually does by late July. I've fished that reef for going on fifteen years and the pattern holds: pile of clam shells and tire structure on the south end, current running enough to keep bait tight to bottom, and the fish stack up in the breaks between the high spots. Squid strips on a two-hook bottom rig, or a Gulp Grub in white on a 4-ounce jig if the tide's really moving — either one works when you're right on top of the structure. Axel Carlson and Shrewsbury Rocks are producing the same way, slightly shallower water, slightly smaller average fish, but you won't go home skunked.

That's the dependable half of the week. The other half — the tuna — is where you earn it.

We flagged this last week: surface temps offshore running into the upper 70s with a well-defined thermocline holding bait in that 60 to 90 foot band, and that's exactly what's setting up the bite on the Mud Hole lumps right now. I had a report Tuesday of bluefin working bait balls on the northeast corner of the Hole, birds diving, fish coming up on the troll in that same depth window — spreads with green machines and cedar plugs out at 100 to 120 feet on the wire, small green-and-white or naked ballyhoo up top. Not a wide-open bite. More like windows — an hour of activity when the bait gets pushed up against the thermocline edge, then it goes quiet until the current shifts again. That's typical for this time of year on the Hole. You want to be on the water early, working the edges where the warm surface layer meets that cooler subsurface push, and you want your eyes on the birds more than your eyes on the sounder.

Farther out, yellowfin are working the canyon-adjacent structure from Hudson south through the Washington and Wilmington drops, riding a warm-water filament that's pushed further north than you'd expect for the end of July. That's outside true midrange range for most of the Manasquan/Belmar fleet unless you're running a boat that can cover sixty-plus miles comfortably, but for the guys who make that run, the reports have been solid — yellowfin in the 30 to 50 pound class on trolled ballyhoo and small spreader bars, and some jigging success once they're marked holding under the birds. If that filament holds its position — and there's no sign yet that it's sliding — I'd expect that yellowfin bite to stay consistent into next week. The risk is always the same out there: these warm tongues move, and a boat that found fish Tuesday can find nothing Thursday if the edge shifted twenty miles overnight.

Mahi are just starting to show around the pot buoys and any floating structure inshore of the Klondike — weed lines, lobster gear, the occasional piece of debris. It's early. I wouldn't call it a pattern yet, more like a handful of scattered fish, small ones mixed with a few keepers in the 4 to 6 pound range, taken on small spreader bars and split-tail mullet skirts pulled slow past the pots. August is when that fishery typically turns on around here as the warm water builds and holds, so if you're planning a trip specifically for mahi, I'd give it another week or two before betting the whole day on it. Right now it's a bonus fish while you're running out to the tuna grounds, not a destination.

Bonito and false albacore haven't shown in this zone yet, and I'm not going to pretend they have. The thermal setup up in Montauk and Block Island has crossed the threshold where those fish typically arrive, and if that pattern pushes south the way it usually does, we could see early scouts working the surf line and inlet edges off Sea Girt or Manasquan within the next couple weeks. Worth keeping a light spinning rod rigged with small metal on the boat just in case you run into a nervous bait pod on the way out — but I wouldn't plan a trip around it yet.

Sea conditions have been workable but not flat — running 3 to 4 feet at a short period out on the reef line, which is fine for bottom fishing but makes the ride out to the Mud Hole and beyond a bit of a washing machine in an afternoon sea breeze. If you're running out there, I'd leave early, fish the tuna window in the calmer morning hours, and be ready to duck inshore to the reef structure if it kicks up.

Looking ahead: if this warm-water pattern holds through the week the way it has the last several days, I expect the sea bass bite to stay steady on Sea Girt, Axel Carlson, and the Barnegat Ridge wrecks, and the bluefin window on the Mud Hole to keep opening in short bursts tied to bait movement rather than any all-day blitz. The yellowfin bite south should hold as long as that filament stays put — that's the piece I'd watch closest, because when it slides, the whole game moves with it. Mahi should build through August as usual. Bonito, keep an ear out — this is the window where the first real report could break.
