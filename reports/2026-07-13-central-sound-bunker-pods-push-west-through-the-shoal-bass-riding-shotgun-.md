# Bunker Pods Push West Through the Shoal — Bass Riding Shotgun, Blues Right Behind
*The bait told the story before the boats did: peanut bunker piling into the Norwalk Islands and Stratford Shoal, and the predators followed the smell.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 13** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

I spend more time watching bait than I do watching rods these days, and this week the bunker did all the talking. Monday morning off Charles Island I found a pod the size of a tennis court, balled tight and nervous, dimpling the surface like rain that wasn't falling. That kind of behavior means something's under them, and sure enough — a bass came up and cracked the edge of that pod hard enough to send bunker skipping across the calm. That's the tell I look for every July: not the birds yet, not the boats, just the bait acting scared before anybody else notices.

The reason it's happening here and not further east is written right in the water temps. Kings Point is sitting at 71.2 degrees, New London's still down at 66.4. That's a five-degree wall stretched across sixty miles of Sound, and bait doesn't like walls — it piles up against them. Central Sound, from Norwalk down through Stratford Shoal and across to Port Jeff, is sitting right in that warm pocket where bunker and peanut bunker are comfortable and slow-moving, which makes them targets. Every predator in the middle Sound knows it too.

Stratford Shoal has been the most consistent read all week. The rip on the west side, where the current stacks up over that 15 to 25 foot shelf, has been holding bass on the last two hours of the outgoing and first hour of the incoming — the current-edge window when the bunker get pushed off the shoal and funneled into the deeper cut. Guys live-lining whole bunker on a fish-finder rig with 3 ounces of lead have been connecting with slot fish and the occasional overslot pushing 34, 35 inches. If you don't want to mess with live bait, a bunker-colored umbrella rig trolled slow along that same edge, or a white 2-ounce bucktail with a chunk of pork rind bounced along bottom on the drift, has been drawing strikes too. Nothing about it has been easy money — you need to be sitting on that rip at the right stage of tide, and if you show up two hours late you're fishing empty water while the fleet twenty minutes away is hooked up.

Bluefish have been the more reliable player this week, and they've been downright violent on those same bunker schools. Middle Ground Light and the water just off the Norwalk Islands have both had blitzes — sometimes short, five-minute windows where the whole surface goes white, then it shuts as fast as it started. Metal is the move: half-ounce to one-ounce Kastmasters and Deadly Dicks, cast into the froth and ripped back fast. No finesse required. If you see the gulls stack up and dive-bomb, you've got maybe ten minutes before the school pushes under or moves off, so don't waste time tying knots — have a rod rigged and ready.

Fluke have been more of a grind. The flats off Port Jeff Harbor and the mouth of the Housatonic have given up keepers, but it's been a pick, not a bite — three or four fish for a full afternoon of drifting 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails in 20 to 30 feet. The warm surface layer at Kings Point and points west seems to have pushed some of the better fluke activity deeper than usual for mid-July, so if you're marking bait on the sounder but not getting bit, don't be afraid to go down to a heavier head and slow the drift right down.

Weakfish have shown up here and there — nothing to build a trip around, but a few nice ones in the 18 to 20 inch class have come off the grass edges around the Norwalk Islands on chartreuse Gulp shrimp fished under a slow retrieve at dusk. That's a species I keep half an eye on every summer because when they show, they show in short windows, usually low light, usually over soft bottom near current. Worth a cast or two if you're passing through anyway.

Blackfish are off the table with the season closed through mid-August, and porgies have been steady but unspectacular on bloodworms and clam over the rockier stretches near Charles Island and the mouth of Oyster Bay — good action for a slow afternoon, not the headline.

Looking ahead, I'd keep my eye on that thermal gradient more than the calendar. If the warm water at Kings Point keeps holding and the bunker keep stacking against that cooler eastern wall, Stratford Shoal and the Norwalk corridor should keep producing through the coming week, especially on the tide changes when the current pushes bait off the structure. If a strong blow out of the southwest comes through and mixes that gradient down, I'd expect the bite to scatter and the bass to slide deeper — worth checking the rip before you commit to a whole day out there. Bonito haven't shown yet, but this is exactly the kind of bait concentration that usually tips them off early. I'll be watching the peanut bunker close for that first sign.
