# September 11 and Washington's Eddy Still Runs Like July
*The early-season canyon that fired ahead of everyone else is now the one holding the pattern together into fall.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON, NY — September 11** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Anyone who's fished this canyon long enough knows the calendar lies. Washington doesn't run on the same clock as its neighbors. It warms first in June because of where it sits relative to the Gulf Stream, it produces first, and most years it's already grinding through a mid-season lull by the time the other canyons are just getting hot. September usually means the opposite of peak — a canyon that's spent, water that's cooled off the edge, boats drifting east or south to find the better bite. Not this year. Not yet.

What's held here through the back half of summer is the same stable eddy that's carried the whole stretch from the Hudson down through Wilmington. That filament of warm water hasn't broken down, and Washington sits right in the middle of it. We flagged this last week — said the bite here would track closely with Wilmington and Hudson because the same warm water mass was governing all three — and that's exactly how it's played out. The edge is still blue, still holding bait, and the marlin and mahi activity that's been the story up and down that stretch hasn't skipped Washington. Yellowfin have stayed steady along the thermal break too, which for a canyon that usually starts fading by Labor Day is a good problem to have.

That said, steady isn't stacked, and anyone heading out this week should go in with realistic expectations. The fleet working the edge is describing a moderate bite — not a blitz, not a bust. Yellowfin are coming on the troll in the 50-70 range on green machines and cedar plugs pulled off the shorter riggers, with the occasional bigeye showing up on the chunk after dark over deeper structure. White marlin have been mixed in with the mahi around any floating debris or grass lines that collect along the current seams, which is where the dredge-and-naked-ballyhoo combo has been drawing strikes. Wahoo have made a few appearances too, mostly on high-speed lures worked just off the edge where the water color breaks clean, though that's been more opportunistic than a pattern anyone can plan a whole trip around.

The why here is simple bathymetry and thermodynamics. Washington's walls and the deeper contour breaks act like a funnel for whatever bait gets pushed by the eddy, and as long as that warm filament holds its shape and doesn't slide off or get sheared apart by a frontal passage, the edge stays productive. It's the same mechanism that's kept the whole Hudson-to-Wilmington run alive deeper into the season than usual. Boats working the canyon have been finding the better color and the better temperature break by running the edge itself rather than committing to one drop and sitting on it — a September habit that pays off because the break line has been mobile, not fixed, day to day.

Sea state has been workable, nothing extreme, which matters this time of year when the pattern is this thin a thread. A stretch of clean weather is what's let boats stay on the number long enough to find fish rather than getting bounced off the grounds by wind before the pattern reveals itself. That's the tell for how fragile this late-summer stability actually is — it doesn't take much, a strong frontal push or a shift in the upper current, to shear that filament and send the warm water sliding off somewhere else. When that happens, Washington reverts to form and starts cooling ahead of the pack the same way it warmed ahead of the pack back in June.

For the next few days, the smart play is to treat this like found money rather than a sure thing. If the edge holds its position and the water stays blue where it's been sitting, the mixed yellowfin-mahi-white marlin bite should continue, maybe even pick up a notch if a fresh push of bait gets funneled onto the wall by the eddy's rotation. Best case, that stability carries another week and Washington keeps punching above its September weight, same as it's been doing since June. If the filament starts to break down — and this is the real risk heading into fall — expect the bite to go quiet fast, and boats will need to widen the search rather than sit on last week's numbers.

Anyone planning a run out here this week should watch the color and the temperature break daily rather than trusting last week's report to still be true by the time the lines go out. Troll the edge early, work any grass or debris lines hard for the mahi and white marlin, and keep a chunk bait ready after dark if the yellowfin show reluctant to eat trolled spreads. It's not the wide-open canyon action of June, but for the second week of September, this is about as good as Washington usually gets — and right now, it's holding.
