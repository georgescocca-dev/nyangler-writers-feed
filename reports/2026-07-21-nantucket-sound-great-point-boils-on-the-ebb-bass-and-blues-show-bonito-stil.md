# Great Point Boils on the Ebb — Bass and Blues Show, Bonito Still a Rumor
*The rip at Great Point is loaded with bass and blues on the low-light tides, but two weeks of watching for the first bonito has come up empty — for now.*

**NANTUCKET, MA — July 21** — _by Charlie St. Pierre, Zone Writer_

I was standing on the beach at Great Point Thursday morning, coffee going cold in my hand, watching a knot of terns work a rip line about 200 yards off the lighthouse. My first thought — every year around this date it's my first thought — was bonito. That tight, nervous diving pattern, the way the birds hover and drop instead of wheeling wide. I grabbed the binoculars and it wasn't bonito at all. It was bluefish, chopping through a pod of sand eels hard enough to turn the surface white, with a few bigger swirls underneath that I'd bet money were bass working the leftovers. Still a good sight. Just not the sight I've been waiting on.

That's where we sit in Nantucket Sound right now — a genuinely productive bass-and-blues bite riding on top of a bonito watch that's stretching into its third week without a confirmed fish. The water's warmed into a range that should be comfortable for them — Montauk's been reading right around 69°F, and the Sound tends to track close behind that number this time of year — so the ingredients are there. I just haven't put my hands on one, and I haven't heard from anybody on this island who has either. When it happens it usually happens fast, so keep a rod rigged with something small and shiny — a half-ounce Deadly Dick or a Yo-Zuri epoxy jig — every time you're anywhere near a rip.

While we wait, the striper fishing has settled into the pattern you'd expect with the water this warm — low light or nothing. Dawn and dusk at Great Point are producing the most consistent action, with fish stacked on the north side of the rip where the ebb dumps bait around the point. Bunker chunks fished on the bottom in 15 to 20 feet have been drawing keeper-class bass after dark, and at first light the same water gives up fish to a Danny swimmer or a Gibbs pencil worked slow across the seam where the clean water meets the churn. Monomoy's doing the same trick on its own schedule — the rips off the north end and down toward the Powder Hole are holding bass that feed hard on the last two hours of the ebb and go quiet fast once the sun climbs. If you're fishing Monomoy or Chatham inlet in daylight, you're mostly fishing for bluefish, and there are plenty of them — solid choppers in the 3 to 6 pound class hammering topwater plugs and bucktails wherever bait gets pushed against structure.

The real bright spot this week, if I'm honest, is fluke. Nantucket Shoals and Horseshoe Shoal have both been throwing quality fish, and it's been steady enough that a few boats out of the island have made it their whole day rather than a stop on the way to somewhere else. Structure and channel edges in 25 to 35 feet are the play — drifting white or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid when the current's really moving. The bite's been best on the last two hours of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing, right when the water's got some push but hasn't gone slack. We're coming off today's first-quarter neap, so the current through the Shoals and around Horseshoe won't be screaming this week — that's actually good news for the fluke game because it widens the window where the drift speed stays in that sweet, manageable zone instead of blowing you through the good bottom in ten minutes.

That softer neap current is worth factoring into your bass and blue plans too. Monomoy and Chatham's rips depend on a hard-running tide to really turn on, and with the moon in its quieter phase this week, expect the bite windows to stretch out rather than concentrate into one short, violent hour. That can mean less frantic top-water blitzing but more time on the water when fish are willing — a fair trade if you're working around a day job and can only get out for a few hours.

Looking ahead, I'd keep doing what I've been doing — working the low-light hours at Great Point and Monomoy for bass, running metal through any bluefish activity you find, and hitting the Shoals or Horseshoe on the better half of the tide for fluke. On the bonito front, I'd start checking the temperature breaks off Sankaty and the outer edge of the Shoals every time you're out there, because if the regional warming trend holds, this is exactly the kind of week they show up without much warning. When that first one gets caught, you'll hear about it inside a day — this island doesn't keep that kind of secret for long.
