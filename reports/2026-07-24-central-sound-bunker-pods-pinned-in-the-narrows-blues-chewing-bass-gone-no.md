# Bunker Pods Pinned in the Narrows — Blues Chewing, Bass Gone Nocturnal
*The bait tells the story this week: bunker balled up tight from the Norwalk Islands to Stratford Shoal, and everything with teeth is underneath them.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 24** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

First thing I do every morning is stand on the bulkhead and watch what the bait's doing before I even look at a rod. This week the bunker told me everything. From the Norwalk Islands across to Charles Island, and pushing east toward Stratford Shoal, I'm seeing bunker schools balled up so tight they look like oil slicks from a distance — that nervous, flickering shimmer on the surface that means something's underneath pushing them. Terns are working them hard, diving and screaming, and every so often the whole pod erupts as blues come up through the middle of it.

That's the visible story on the Central Sound right now, and it's a good one. Bluefish have taken this bait and made it their own from the Norwalk Islands down through Stamford, Greenwich, and across to the Oyster Bay side. We flagged this pattern building over a week ago, said it would keep spreading as long as the bunker stayed pinned and the weather held calm — and here it is, right on schedule, still expanding. I had a report from a buddy running out of Norwalk who hit a blitz Tuesday morning off Sheffield Island that lasted a solid forty minutes, cocktail blues to 4 pounds crashing bunker on the surface, poppers and metal — Ava 27s, white bucktails with a strip of pork rind — getting bit on nearly every cast. That's the kind of action you drop everything for when you see the birds working.

The bunker themselves are the key variable here. Surface water in the mid-70s (Kings Point reading 70.9°F, and it's likely a touch warmer than that in the shallow coves by afternoon) has these bait schools pinned tight to structure, not roaming open water. That's exactly the kind of setup that concentrates predators instead of scattering them thin across the Sound. As long as we don't get a hard blow out of the southwest to break the bait apart, I'd expect the blitz activity to keep rolling along the Connecticut shoreline and spreading toward New Haven.

Striped bass are a different conversation entirely right now, and I want to be straight with you about it — daytime bass fishing in the Central Sound has gone quiet. Water's warm enough that the fish have pushed down onto the thermocline and gone nocturnal, which is the textbook mid-summer move. If you're bass hunting between 10 AM and 6 PM over Stratford Shoal or Middle Ground Light, you're better off vertical jigging deep — I'm marking fish in 45 to 60 feet on the shoal itself, working diamond jigs and 4-ounce bucktails tipped with a chunk of fresh bunker down to them. It's slow, deliberate fishing, not exactly exciting, but it's producing keeper-class fish in the mid-20s to low 30s for guys willing to work the sonar and drop right on the marks.

The better bass program right now is after dark, and that's not a secret to anyone who's fished this water a few summers. Norwalk Islands rock piles, the back side of Charles Island, the drop-offs around Middle Ground — all holding fish that come up shallow to feed once the sun's off the water. Live eels drifted on the current edges, or bucktails cast tight to the rocks and swum slow, have been the ticket. The neap tide we're coming off has softened the current through the deeper channels, which hasn't helped — less push means less concentrated feeding, and the bite's been more scattered than aggressive. That should change as we build back toward the next stronger tide cycle over the coming week, and when the current picks back up around the Norwalk Islands and Stratford Shoal, I expect the night bite to sharpen up considerably.

Fluke are in that classic summer pattern where quantity and quality have split apart. Shorts are everywhere — drift the flats off Oyster Bay or Port Jefferson Harbor and you'll bend a rod every few minutes, but most of them go back. The keepers are sitting deeper and cooler, and the better fish this week came from 35 to 50 feet along the channel edges off Port Jefferson and the deeper humps north of the Norwalk Islands, on the stronger half of the tide. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along bottom, with a squid strip added for scent, has been the winning combo. Don't waste time on the shallow flats past mid-morning once the sun's cooking that water — push out to depth.

Porgies remain the most dependable meal-ticket fish on this beat right now. The reef structure off Eaton's Neck, around Middle Ground, and the rock piles near Charles Island are stacked with them, and a bag of clams with small hooks on a high-low rig will fill a cooler without much drama. Blackfish season's closed this stretch, so that's off the table until fall, but I'm already hearing chatter about early scouting trips once the season reopens.

Looking ahead, I'm watching two things closely. First, whether that bunker-bluefish blitz keeps its grip on the Connecticut shoreline or starts thinning out as bait disperses — if the calm weather holds, I'd bet on it spreading further toward New Haven before it breaks. Second, and more interesting to me, is the calendar. Bonito and albies typically start showing in the Sound's narrower stretches — right around Stratford Shoal and the Race — in the first half of August, and with this warm water pattern established early, I wouldn't be shocked to see the first scout report a little ahead of schedule this year. I'll be watching the bait for it, same as always.
