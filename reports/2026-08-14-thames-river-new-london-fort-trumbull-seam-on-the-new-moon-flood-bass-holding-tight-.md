# Fort Trumbull Seam on the New-Moon Flood: Bass Holding Tight, Blues Chasing Peanuts
*Post-new-moon current has sharpened the rip at the mouth, and both stripers and bluefish are stacking on it.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 14** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

I stood on the seawall at Fort Trumbull Tuesday evening and watched the rip line do something it hadn't done in about three weeks — hold its shape. Through most of late July that seam off the point was mushy, current pushing weak and diffuse, the kind of water that makes you squint and wonder if the tide's even moving. This week it's a clean, defined edge, current stacking against the old fort's granite base and peeling off into a visible crease you could set a watch to. That's the new moon doing its work. We flagged this last week — said the building tidal flow into the August 12 new moon would sharpen the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's showing at the mouth right now.

Water's sitting around 69.4 degrees down at the harbor mouth, which is on the cooler side for mid-August in this river, and I think that's part of why the fish are staying put instead of pushing further up toward the Bridge or holding shallow. Cooler water plus a stronger current seam equals fish that don't have to work as hard to eat — they just sit in the seam and let the bait come to them. Striped bass have been doing exactly that off Fort Trumbull on the last two hours of the ebb, holding in 15 to 20 feet right where the current breaks off the point. I've had the best luck there with bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, just enough weight to hold bottom in that push, and I'm not skimping on chunk size — half a bunker on a 6/0 circle hook has been outproducing smaller cuts. A few guys working eels after dark off the same stretch have connected too, though that bite's been more about timing than volume — you want to be there right as the tide turns, not an hour into it.

The bigger story this week, and one that matches what's happening from the Sound on down through Narragansett, is bait. Peanut bunker have flooded into the lower river and they're thick off Ocean Beach and Pleasure Beach, rolling in tight pods right along the wash. Snapper blues have been all over them in the shallows — you can walk the beach at low light and see the surface nervous with them. That's produced some fun light-tackle action on small Deadly Dicks and Kastmasters for anyone who wants an easy afternoon with the kids, but it's also pulled adult bluefish up into the harbor to feed on the same bait, and those have been the better story. I had a solid half hour off the Pleasure Beach jetty Thursday morning working a white bucktail through a bunker school and connecting on blues in the 4 to 6 pound class, blitzing right on top for a stretch before it shut off as quick as it started. That's the pattern right now — short, violent windows tied directly to where the bait gets pushed by current, not sustained action you can count on lasting.

Porgies remain the steady, dependable piece of the puzzle. The riprap around the Gold Star Bridge pilings has been holding good numbers in 20 to 25 feet, and a simple high-low rig with sandworms or clam has been enough to keep a rod bent through most of a tide change. Nothing flashy, no size to write home about, but if you want fish in the cooler without chasing a blitz, that's the spot. Blackfish season's open here through the end of the month, and I've heard of a few decent tog coming off the rock piles near Fort Trumbull and the Pleasure Beach jetty on green crab, though it's early yet for that bite to really turn on — that's more of a September and October story once the water starts to slide the other direction.

What I haven't seen — and I want to be straight about this — is any sign of the bigger blitz activity that's apparently lighting up water from Narragansett Bay up through Buzzards Bay and into Cape Cod Bay right now. That's a bunker-driven push tied to early staging ahead of the fall run, and it's a real phenomenon worth watching, but the Thames hasn't gotten that memo yet. What we've got here is a tighter, more localized version of the same story — smaller bait pods, shorter feeding windows, fish that reward you for being in the right seam at the right tide stage rather than fish that are just everywhere.

Looking ahead, I'd keep working that Fort Trumbull rip on the ebb as long as this current pattern holds — the seam's only going to get more defined as we build toward the first quarter moon later this week, and that usually means a sharper, more predictable bite window rather than a bigger one. If the peanut bunker keep pushing into the harbor mouth the way they have been, I'd expect the bluefish blitzes off Pleasure Beach and Ocean Beach to keep popping in short bursts through the week, especially on the last of the outgoing when bait gets flushed out past the beach. I wouldn't plan a whole trip around chasing that blitz, though — treat it as a bonus if you're already out there working bass or porgies, and keep an eye on the gulls. They'll tell you where the bait's getting pushed before you ever see it yourself.
