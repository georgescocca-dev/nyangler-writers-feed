# Bay water hits 69 as weakfish crash the party with keeper fluke and sea robins
*Jamaica Bay's trifecta firing on bucktails and clams as stripers start their summer shuffle.*

**JAMAICA BAY, NY — June 24** — _by Denise "Dee" Vasquez, Zone Writer_

The bay is alive right now, and I mean really alive. Water temps hit 69 degrees this week, and that magic number has flipped the switch on everything from the Marine Parkway Bridge to the back channels near Cross Bay. We're seeing the classic Jamaica Bay summer transition — stripers moving out to cooler ocean water while the warm-water crew moves in to fill the void.

The big story is weakfish. Real weakfish, not the rumors we've been chasing for years. I'm talking keeper-sized fish to 19 inches mixed in with shorts, hitting bucktails and fresh clams on the incoming tide. The best action has been around the bridge pilings and the deeper holes near the inlet, where that 69-degree bay water meets the slightly cooler ocean flow. These fish are staging up just like they used to, and it's bringing back memories of the old days when weakies were a regular part of the Jamaica Bay mix.

Fluke fishing has turned the corner too. The warming water has them active and feeding, with keepers showing up on white Gulp Swimming Mullets dragged slow on 3/4-ounce bucktails. The key is working the edges where the bay channels drop into deeper water — 12 to 18 feet seems to be the sweet spot. I'm seeing fish to 25 inches, with most keepers running 19 to 22 inches. The outgoing tide has been most productive, especially that first hour when the current really starts to rip.

What's really got me excited is the sea robin explosion. I know, I know — most people curse these guys, but when you're seeing monster sea robins like we are now, it means the bottom is loaded with crabs and worms. That's bait, and where there's bait, bigger fish follow. Plus, these sea robins are eating machines on light tackle, and the big ones make decent table fare if you know how to clean them.

The stripers haven't disappeared completely, but they're definitely thinning out as the water warms. The fish that are still around are mostly smaller slot fish in the 28 to 32-inch range, and they're getting pickier about when they feed. Early morning and late evening are your best shots, especially around the bridge structure where there's still some current and shade.

Bait-wise, fresh clams are absolutely crushing it right now. The weakfish and fluke both love them, and even the sea robins can't resist. I'm running simple fishfinder rigs with 2/0 hooks, keeping the sinker just heavy enough to hold bottom in the current. For the fluke, I'm sticking with that bucktail-and-Gulp combo that's been money all season — white or chartreuse Gulp on a white or pink bucktail head.

The bunker situation is interesting. We're not seeing the massive schools we usually get this time of year, which has some guys worried. But there's still plenty of bait around — spearing, sand eels, and small crabs are thick in the shallows. The lack of big bunker schools might actually be helping the fluke bite, forcing them to focus on smaller, easier targets.

Looking ahead, this warming trend should continue to push more summer species into the bay. I'm expecting porgies to show up in numbers any day now, and the first blackfish of the season shouldn't be far behind as they move into their summer haunts around the bridge pilings and rocky structure.

The new moon this weekend means spring tides and strong currents — perfect for flushing fresh bait through the system and getting fish moving. I'll be focusing on the inlet area where that tidal flow is strongest, especially on the outgoing when everything gets pushed toward the ocean. That's when the big fluke like to set up and ambush whatever comes their way.
