# Monhegan Ledges Hold Pollock on Top, Mackerel Thick Off Eastern Egg Rock
*Groundfish still working the deep channels of Penobscot Bay while stripers stick to structure through the warm stretch — a steady week, nothing wild.*

**BOOTHBAY HARBOR, ME — July 31** — _by Rusty Pelletier, Zone Writer_

Forty years ago my father took me out past Monhegan on a morning just like this one — flat calm, gulls working close to the rocks, and he told me the mackerel would tell you everything you needed to know about the rest of the season. If they're thick and staying thick into August, the pollock and the bass will follow them right up to the surface. I thought of him this week hauling gear off Allen Island, because the mackerel are exactly where he'd have expected them — schooled up tight off Eastern Egg Rock, puffins diving through them at first light, and the pollock stacked right underneath doing the same thing we've watched them do every summer since Reagan was in office.

We flagged this last week — said the traditional midsummer Maine program would hold through the week — and that's about the size of it. Nothing dramatic, nothing collapsing, just the Gulf of Maine doing what cool water does when the rest of the coast south of Cape Cod is running warm and pushing everything deep. Our water hasn't budged much past the mid-60s in the open bay, which means the bass and the groundfish are still willing to work a daytime tide instead of hiding until dark like they're doing from Boothbay south to the Sound. That's the one advantage we've got right now, and it's worth using while it lasts.

Monhegan's ledges have been the most consistent piece of water all week. Fishing the ebb off the southwest side in 40 to 60 feet, jigging diamond jigs and 6-inch soft-plastic shads for pollock that are running 3 to 6 pounds, with a few better fish pushing 8 mixed in deeper on the drop. Same spot's been holding stripers up on the shallower rock piles — schoolies mostly, 24 to 30 inches, taking topwater poppers at first light before the sun gets high and pushes them down onto the structure. By 8 or 9 in the morning you're better off switching to a bucktail and working it slow along the bottom edge where the ledge drops into deeper water.

Eastern Egg Rock itself has been a mackerel show more than anything, and that's fine — those schools are pulling everything else in behind them. Bass have been working the edges of the bait, and we've had a few bluefish mixed in too, which isn't typical for us this far north but isn't unheard of either when there's this much forage stacked up. Small metal jigs, Kastmasters and Deadly Dicks, worked fast through the school will take mackerel by the bucket, and if you slow down and let a swimming plug sink through the middle of the bait you'll draw the bass and blues up out of it.

Penobscot Bay's been the quieter half of the report, but quiet doesn't mean empty. The deeper channels between Vinalhaven and Isle au Haut are still holding cod and haddock — not the numbers we had twenty years ago, nowhere close, but lobstermen are pulling the occasional cod as gear bycatch in 80 to 100 feet off the western side of the Fox Islands Thorofare, and a few charter boats working jigs and bait rigs down in that same water have put together decent mixed bags of cod and haddock, most fish in the 3 to 8 pound range. If you're headed out there, fish the last two hours of the ebb when the current's still moving enough to keep bait pinned against the bottom structure, then hold through the slack before the flood picks back up.

Stonington's been a mackerel and pollock town this week too — the same pattern repeating itself up and down the coast wherever there's a ledge and moving water. The bite off the outer islands there has been best on the last of the incoming, fish stacked tight to structure in 30 to 50 feet, and a few stripers showing up on the flood tide working bait pushed up against the rocks near the harbor entrance.

Tides have been strong coming off the full moon, easing now toward the middle of the cycle, and that's actually helped — enough push to keep bait moving without blowing the whole bay out. Wind's been light out of the southwest most afternoons, which keeps the surface calm enough for topwater work early and jigging later without fighting a chop.

Looking ahead, I don't see much reason for this to change through the first week of August. The water south of us stays warm and keeps pushing quality bass and bait north along the coast, which should keep feeding our pattern rather than disrupting it. If a frontal system pushes through midweek like they tend to this time of year, expect a day or two of dirty water and slower fishing right behind it, then a quick return to normal once it clears. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd take the early ebb at Monhegan before the boats crowd the ledge, work the topwater bite for an hour, then switch to jigs and stay on the pollock through the tide change. That's about as sure a bet as this coast offers in late July.
