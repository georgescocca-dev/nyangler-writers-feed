# Captree dock talk: the blitz is real, but the bass went nocturnal on us
*Bunker showers along Fire Island are pulling blues to the surface all day while the bass wait for dark — here's how to fish both.*

**CAPTREE, NY — July 27** — _by Mariana "Mari" Reyes, Zone Writer_

Ran into Sal from the state boat ramp yesterday morning, coffee in hand, watching gulls work a mile of water off Democrat Point like they'd lost their minds. He looks at me and says, "Mari, I ain't seen bait like this in years — where the hell are the bass?" And that's been the question all week. The bunker are here, thick as I've seen them push along the barrier beach since maybe 2019, and the blues are all over them. But the bass? They've gone dark on us. Literally.

Water's sitting in the low-to-mid 70s inside the bay and along the beach — buoy readings offshore have the surface up around 71, and it's warmer than that tucked into the flats on a hot afternoon. That's above the comfort line for stripers in daylight. They're not gone, they've just gone nocturnal, holding deep on the current seams and structure through the day and only really turning on to feed after the sun drops. We called this exact pattern last week and it's held — the warm, stable stretch of weather kept the bunker pinned to the beach and kept that blitz alive, and it's still running strong seven days later.

So here's how I've been splitting my days. Mornings and midday, I'm running the beachfront from Democrat Point east toward Kismet chasing the blitzes. You don't need much finesse for this — a bunker spoon or a wide-wobble metal like a Deadly Dick trolled just outside the surf line, or if you can get close enough, a topwater popper cast right into the boil. Blues have been running 3 to 6 pounds, some bigger mixed in, and when the bait gets pinned against the beach on that morning tide change, it's about as visual a bite as we get all summer — gulls diving, bunker skying, blues slashing through the middle of it. I watched a guy Tuesday morning fill a cooler in twenty minutes off a Boston Whaler without moving more than two hundred yards.

But if you want bass, you've got to change your whole day around. I've been putting the boat away at dusk and heading back out for the last two hours of light into full dark, working the inlet and the bridges. Robert Moses causeway, both spans, on the last of the incoming into the top of the tide has been the money window — eels drifted along the pilings, or bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in that current. Slot fish in the 28-to-34-inch range have been coming out of there consistently after dark, with a few better fish mixed in for guys willing to stay out past midnight. Democrat Point itself, the deep water right off the tip where the inlet current rips past the jetty, has also been giving up bass on bunker chunks fished on the bottom during that same night bite — that seam where the outgoing dumps warm bay water into the cooler ocean current is exactly the kind of edge these fish want right now.

Fluke have actually been the more dependable daytime player while the bass sleep it off. The open flats inside the bay have slowed down as that shallow water heats up past what a fluke wants to sit in, but the inlet-adjacent structure is still holding fish — the deeper bends around the Robert Moses bridge pilings, 25 to 35 feet, and the channel edges off Oak Beach have kept producing on the drift. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid, dragged slow along bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing has been my go-to. Nothing enormous — most fish are running 16 to 19 inches with a keeper mixed in here and there — but it's steady, and on a week like this, steady daytime action while you wait for the bass to wake up is worth taking.

Weakfish have shown up in ones and twos on the same fluke drifts, mostly incidental, but if you're marking soft marks on the sounder near bottom in that same inlet current, it's worth dropping a bucktail down just to check. Porgies are still there for the taking around the Robert Moses rock piles and the Fire Island Inlet breakwater if you want a change of pace — small hooks, sandworms or clams, anchor up on the last of the ebb.

Looking ahead, I don't see much reason for this pattern to break unless the weather does. If the heat holds and the wind stays out of the south and southwest like it's been, expect the bunker blitzes to keep marching along the beach and the bass to keep punching the clock after dark. What I'd watch for is any cold front or a stretch of onshore wind that mixes the water column — that's usually what pushes bass back into daylight feeding, and it can happen fast this time of year. Until then, if I've only got one trip in me this week, I'm fishing the last light into dark at Robert Moses on the incoming, eel in hand, and doing my fluke drifting early before the sun gets too high.
