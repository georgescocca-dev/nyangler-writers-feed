# Double Creek Channel Fluke Holding the Drop-Offs While Snapper Blues Take Over the Docks
*Warm, stratified bay water is spreading the fluke thin, but the edges are still paying off — and the snapper run is telling us exactly what comes next.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — August 27** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

There's a certain kind of late-August week on Barnegat Bay that old-timers like me have seen more times than we can count — the surface goes glassy and warm, the bay stratifies top to bottom, and every species with a lick of sense starts hunting for the cooler layer down deep or the moving water at the edges. I've watched this pattern play out from Toms River clear down to the Light for forty years now, and this week fit the mold about as well as any I can remember. The bay's sitting in the mid-to-upper 70s on top, the flats have gone thin and quiet in the heat of the day, and the fish that are still biting have pushed themselves onto structure — channel edges, drop-offs, the inlet itself — anywhere there's current keeping the water turning over and the oxygen up.

We flagged this exact pattern last week — said fluke fishing would stay modest until the nights cool enough to knock down the thermal layering, and that's precisely what's held. The flats themselves have been soft and scattered, fish spread out over acres of warm bottom instead of stacked where you can find them twice. But the channel edges are a different story. Double Creek Channel has been the most consistent producer in the system, particularly working the drop-offs on the last two hours of the incoming tide, when bait gets pushed up off the deeper water and onto the lip of the flat. Keeper fluke to five pounds have come off there on 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with a 4-inch chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullet, dragged dead slow along bottom so the jig ticks the mud without lifting off it. The bite window is short — an hour, hour and a half around that tide change — but it's been the difference between a slow day and a decent one.

Tices Shoal and the grass edges south of there are worth a look on the same tide stage if Double Creek gets crowded, though the fish there have been running smaller, more shorts mixed with the occasional keeper. The deeper water off Barnegat Light, where the inlet current keeps things stirred and cooler, has also been quietly productive for anyone willing to work bucktail-and-gulp combos or strips of squid on a fluke rig down in that 20-to-25-foot zone on the ebb.

Weakfish are around in modest numbers, which is about what you'd expect for this time of year, showing up on the same sedge-bank drifts that are holding croaker right now — that stretch of marsh edge from the mouth of Oyster Creek down toward Double Creek has given up a few keeper weakfish on Fishbites and small pieces of bloodworm fished on light tackle, bottom rig, incoming water. Nothing to build a trip around by itself, but a nice bonus if you're already anchored up working bottom for croaker or kingfish. Speaking of kingfish, the sandy stretches along Island Beach and the inlet flats have had a fair showing on bloodworm and Fishbites combos fished on a simple two-hook bottom rig — good action for anyone who wants a bucket of fish for the pan without much fuss.

The bigger story right now, and the one worth paying attention to for what's coming, is the snapper blues. They're thick in the back-bay creeks — thick enough that the docks and bulkheads from Forked River up through Toms River are loaded with kids and grandparents alike snapping them up on tiny gold spoons and bits of bunker under a bobber. We called this one too — said the heavy snapper showing was the leading indicator that the bigger bluefish would follow the bait out of the bay as the nights start cooling, and that's exactly the mechanism at work. Right now the adult cocktail blues are mixing into the fluke and weakfish catches out on the channel edges and around the inlet structure, nothing huge, two to four pounds mostly, but there are more of them showing every week, and that usually means the real fall run of blues isn't far behind once that water temperature starts sliding.

Striped bass are exactly where you'd expect them in this heat — in a holding pattern, not a migration. Daytime action has been next to nothing on the bay side; the better play has been working the inlet structure and the deeper holes off Barnegat Light after dark, when the current is still hard-running on the tide change but the sun's off the water. This is a summer pattern, not the start of the fall push — that real migration doesn't typically get going here until the nights turn consistently cool, likely not before mid-September.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full over the next several days, which should sharpen up the tidal swings and put a little more current through the inlet and the channels — good news for the fluke bite at Double Creek and around the Light, since stronger current means better oxygen turnover and a tighter bait push onto the drop-offs. Wind's expected to stay out of the southwest in the 8-to-12 knot range through the weekend, which keeps the bay calm enough to fish comfortably but won't do much to cool things down. If you've only got one day, the smart play is still the last two hours of the incoming at Double Creek Channel for fluke, with a stop at the sedges on the way in for a shot at weakfish and croaker. Keep an eye on the overnight lows next week — once they start dipping, this whole bay changes character fast, and that's when the real fall action starts to build.
