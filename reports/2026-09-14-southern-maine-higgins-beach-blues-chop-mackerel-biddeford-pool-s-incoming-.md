# Higgins Beach Blues Chop Mackerel, Biddeford Pool's Incoming Turns On Bass to 28
*Fall's first real bluefish blitz hit the sand this week while striper numbers build steady in the rivers ahead of the moon.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — September 14** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Higgins Beach saw its first legitimate bluefish blitz of the season this week, and the tackle shops from Scarborough to Wells were fielding calls about it by Wednesday morning. Blues in the 3-to-6-pound class chopped through a mackerel school pinned against the sand on the last two hours of the incoming, and anyone with a Deadly Dick or a 2-ounce tin in the truck got in on it. That's the kind of chaos that tells you the calendar's turning — bait's getting nervous, water's cooling, and the fall parade is starting to line up from Kittery to Cape Elizabeth.

We flagged this last week — said striper would build in the tidal rivers around Kittery and York as mackerel schools moved through, and that's exactly what's showing now. The Piscataqua and the York River both have more consistent bass reports than they did ten days ago, with schoolies to slot-sized fish working the current seams on the last of the ebb, chasing mackerel and small pogies out of the skinny water. Nothing gaudy yet, but the pattern is real and it's early in a run that typically doesn't peak until the back half of the month.

Biddeford Pool is still the money spot for anyone chasing size. The incoming tide there remains the best hour of striper fishing on this whole stretch of coast, and this week proved it again — a handful of bass in the mid-20s to a reported 28-pounder came off the flats and the mouth of the Pool on live mackerel and chunked pogies as the tide pushed bait up against the sandbar. The mechanics haven't changed in decades: water floods over the flats, forage gets trapped and disoriented, bass stack up on the edges waiting for the buffet to come to them. If you're throwing plugs, work a Danny-style swimmer or a bone-colored Gibbs pencil right along that drop-off where the flat meets the channel, timed to the first three hours of the incoming. Low light still wins — dawn and dusk are outproducing the middle of the day by a wide margin right now.

Pine Point has been a mixed bag. Some mornings the bass show tight to the Nonesuch River mouth working the last of the outgoing, other days it's dead water for hours. That inconsistency tracks with what the mackerel schools are doing — when they're pushed up into the river mouth, bass follow; when they slide back out toward open water, the bite goes with them. Worth a stop if you're already in the area, but not worth a special trip this week unless you can fish the tide change itself.

Prouts Neck has been fishing true to form on the southwest wind days this week, which there have been a few of. That wind pushes bait against the rocks on the north side and gives surfcasters a clean angle to work poppers and needlefish tight to the structure without getting blown off their feet. A few solid bass in the high teens and low 20s came off the rocks there on Sebile Magic Swimmers and black/silver needles fished on the last two hours of the ebb, when the current pulls bait off the ledges. If the wind holds out of the southwest into the weekend, that pattern should hold too — it's one of the more dependable setups on this beat when the geometry lines up.

Cape Elizabeth's rockier stretches are producing sporadic reports of bigger bass working after dark, which is typical for September — the bigger, warier fish move onto the rocks once the sun's down and the pressure eases. Black needlefish and darters fished slow along the ledges have taken a few fish in the mid-20s. This is patient-man fishing, though — long stretches of nothing punctuated by one good take, and you need to know your rocks well enough to fish them blind in the dark without losing a rod or an ankle.

Mackerel remain the story underneath all of it. Schools have been thick enough this week that jigging them off the piers at Wells Harbor and Ogunquit has been nearly a sure thing on small tins and mackerel jigs dropped straight down around the pilings on the tide change. That abundance is exactly why the striper pattern is building the way it is — there's simply more forage in the system than there's been in weeks, and predators are responding to it the way they always do this time of year.

Bluefish, meanwhile, look like they're just getting started. One blitz at Higgins doesn't make a season, but the size class and the aggression on display this week matches what's typically the front edge of a proper fall run. Expect more of these blowups over the next week to ten days as more bait gets pushed out of the rivers and onto the open beaches, especially on the building tides around the coming moon.

Cod and flounder have been an afterthought this week — not because they're absent, but because everyone's attention is on the surface action. Bottom fishing off the deeper structure near Cape Elizabeth has produced a few flounder on standard spearing-and-squid rigs for those willing to look past the blitz.

Looking ahead, the next few days should keep building on this pattern rather than break it. If the southwest wind holds, Prouts Neck stays in play. If the tides keep pushing mackerel onto the beaches, expect more Higgins-style blitzes to pop up with little warning — keep a rod rigged and ready if you're anywhere near the water at first light or last light. And if you only have one tide to fish this week, make it the incoming at Biddeford Pool. It's been the most consistent producer on this beat all season, and there's no sign of that changing yet.
