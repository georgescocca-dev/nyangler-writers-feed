# August 31 and Washington Still Hasn't Given Up the Warm Water
*The canyon's late-summer thermal setup is holding a beat ahead of the rest of the fleet, and the fish know it.*

**OCEAN CITY, MD — August 31** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Every year around this date I start checking the calendar against the water instead of the other way around, and every year Washington tells me the same thing: this canyon runs on its own clock. By the end of August most of the mid-Atlantic grounds are starting to think about the shoulder season, but Washington sits close enough to the Gulf Stream's western edge that it holds a mature warm-water push later than its neighbors to the north. That's not a hot take, that's just how the geography works — the Stream meanders in tight to this stretch of the shelf, and when it does, Washington gets first crack at the good water and holds it longest. We flagged this exact pattern last week — said to expect continuity with stable warm water in place over the next 72 hours — and that's exactly what's played out. Nothing about the setup has broken down.

What that means practically: the color change and the temperature break that split shelf water from blue Gulf Stream water are still sitting in a productive spot along the edge, and the eddies spinning off that break are still doing their job of stacking bait against structure. Late August into early September is usually when Washington's yellowfin bite matures — the fish have had all summer to settle into a rhythm, and the bait has had all summer to build up along the walls. This is the stretch where a well-run spread pays for itself.

Trolling remains the primary tool out here and nothing about that changes this week. Ballyhoo behind Islanders in dark colors — purple/black, green/yellow — worked with naked ballyhoo mixed into the pattern, continues to be the standard yellowfin approach along the temperature breaks. Spreader bars in the middle of the pattern give the school something to commit to, and the outside baits fished long and staggered pick up the stragglers that won't come all the way to the boat. Mahi are riding the same warm water and showing up opportunistically around any floating structure or weed line the fleet crosses on the way out or back — a rigged ballyhoo or a small skirted lure tossed at a school holding under debris is still the move, and this time of year those mahi can run bigger than the June fish, some pushing into double digits.

Wahoo remain the wildcard piece of this puzzle. Late August is prime timing for a hookup on a high-speed bait run tight to the temperature break, particularly early and late in the day when the light angle is low. A planer or a downrigger rigged with a hard-diving lure run off the corner, separate from the main spread, is the way most of the boats working this pattern give themselves a shot without disrupting the tuna trolling.

Bigeye continue to be a night bite here, and that hasn't shifted. The chunk-and-drift approach after dark, working squid strips or butterflied squid on a slow bottom-bounce presentation near the drop-off, is still producing for boats willing to put in the overnight hours. It's a different kind of fishing than the daytime troll — patience-heavy, gear-heavy — but the canyon's structure along the wall gives bigeye a reason to hold there once the sun goes down and the bait pulls up off the bottom.

White marlin is the piece of this report I want to be straight about. This is typically the tail end of the strongest white marlin window for Washington — the bite that built through July and August starts to thin out as we move into September, not because the fish disappear overnight but because the pattern gets less concentrated and more scattered as the season turns. If billfish is the priority, this is closer to the last good stretch than the middle of it. Ballyhoo behind small Islanders, worked at a moderate troll speed with the spread pulled tight, is still the right presentation while the window holds.

Looking at the next several days, the setup argues for more of the same. The moon is building toward full, which should tighten up the tidal push and help concentrate bait along the edge rather than spreading it thin — generally a good sign for consistency on the troll. Barring any tropical development in the western Atlantic, which remains the one variable worth watching on the forecast models, there's no obvious reason for this pattern to break down in the near term. That's the biggest wildcard on the board right now, not the fish.

For boats planning a run this week, the read is straightforward: work the temperature breaks and color changes along the canyon edge early in the day for yellowfin and wahoo, keep an eye out for floating structure for mahi on the way out and back, and if billfish is the goal, don't wait — this window is closer to its end than its beginning. Washington has been ahead of the rest of the fleet all season. No reason to think that changes before Labor Day.
