# Where'd the peanuts go? Absecon and Great Egg answer the September bell
*Bunker pods pushing the beachfront are pulling bass and blitzing blues right where we said they would — here's the tide window that matters this week.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — September 14** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

What's the first real tell that the fall run has switched on down here? That's the question I got three different ways this week — at the bait shop counter, on the phone, and from a reader who wanted to know why the surf at Ocean City looked "nervous" at first light. The answer is the same every September: watch the peanut bunker. When those pods start pouring down the beachfront in tight, nervous balls instead of loose scattered schools, that's the tripwire. It happened this week, right on schedule.

We flagged this last week — said the building moon would tighten those bunker schools from Raritan Bay on down and get bass and blues committing instead of just picking at stragglers. That's exactly what showed up along the South Jersey beachfront the last several days. The tide range has been building toward the new moon, current is running harder through Absecon and Great Egg, and that extra push is exactly what moves bait out of the back bays and stacks it against the beach and the inlet jetties. Water in the Bight has been sitting in the low-to-mid 70s offshore, cooling faster inshore on the outgoing, and that thermal drop is what's nudging the peanuts to bunch up rather than spread thin the way they were doing through August.

The bluefish showed up first, and they showed up with size. Brigantine surfcasters working the trough off the north end have been into choppers in the 4- to 8-pound class on Deadly Dicks and thin Tsunami metal-lip swimmers, worked fast through the wash on the last two hours of the outgoing when the bait gets pinned against the sandbar. That's the same pattern producing at the Ocean City and Sea Isle City jetties — bunker balling up tight against the rocks on a dropping tide, blues busting under them from mid-morning through dusk. It's not a wall-to-wall blitz every day. Some mornings the birds are working and the water's boiling; other mornings it's dead calm until the tide starts moving again. That's the honest read — good but not constant, and tide-dependent more than time-of-day dependent right now.

Striped bass are shadowing those same bunker schools, which is the part of this pattern worth teaching carefully. The bass aren't committing the way they will in three or four weeks — right now they're picking off stragglers on the edges of the bait balls rather than blowing through the middle of them. Surfcasters at Brigantine and the north end of Stone Harbor have had the best luck swimming bunker-colored paddletails and 2-ounce bucktails with a white or chartreuse trailer through the same current seams the blues are working, just fished a touch slower and deeper. Keeper-class fish, low 30-inch range mostly, with a few overslot fish mixed in on the last hour of dark. This will build fast over the next two weeks as more forage gets pushed out of the rivers and back bays — that's the pattern every year, and there's no reason to expect different this fall.

Fluke are still a genuine story, not a footnote. The sod bank program in the back bays behind Absecon and Great Egg is still producing on the last two hours of the incoming, working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails along the channel edges where the current cuts against the marsh grass. Live killies and peanut bunker on a fishfinder rig, fished tight to the drop-offs in 8 to 12 feet, have been the better bet for the bigger fish — several keepers over 20 inches reported out of the Great Egg back bay this week. This bite has maybe two weeks left before the seasonal exodus toward deeper water accelerates, so if fluke is on the list, don't wait on it.

Weakfish have been a mixed bag — a few nice fish mixed into the fluke catch on the incoming around the Townsends Inlet flats, taken incidentally on Gulp and bucktail combos, but nobody's targeting them with real success right now. Kingfish are still around the surf troughs at Ocean City and Sea Isle on bloodworms and Fishbites, small but steady for anyone soaking bottom rigs between the bigger-fish efforts. Triggerfish around the inlet structure at Absecon and Townsends have thinned out noticeably as the water starts its seasonal cool-down — that fishery is winding toward its close for the year.

Looking ahead, the next 48 to 72 hours should build on what's already started. The tidal range keeps increasing into the new moon, which means stronger current through the inlets and more bait getting pushed hard against structure — the exact recipe that turns picking bass into committed bass. If there's only one window to fish this weekend, make it the last two hours of the outgoing at Absecon or Great Egg, first or last light, with bunker-pattern swimmers and bucktails ready to switch to metal the second the gulls start working. Keep the fluke gear rigged as a backup on the same trip — the sod banks are still paying, and that opportunity is closing faster than the bass bite is opening.
