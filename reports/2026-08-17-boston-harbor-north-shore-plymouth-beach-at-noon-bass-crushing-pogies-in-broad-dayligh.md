# Plymouth Beach at Noon: Bass Crushing Pogies in Broad Daylight
*Boston Harbor stays a grind on the outgoing while Plymouth's daytime bunker blitzes make the drive south worth it.*

**BOSTON HARBOR, MA — August 17** — _by Sara Whitfield, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday morning to skip the harbor and run the truck down to Plymouth, and I don't regret it. When pogies stack tight to a beach and the bass decide feeding beats caution, you go where the bait is, not where the ledge is. That's the whole job some weeks — reading where the fish want to be, not where you wish they'd be.

What's happening off Plymouth Beach right now is the kind of thing you don't see every August. Full-sized pogie schools are holding tight against the sand, and stripers are blowing up on them at midday — not dawn, not dusk, noon, with the sun straight overhead. Hundreds to low thousands of fish working bait balls right off the beach, and hardly any boats or waders on them. We flagged this pattern building last week, said the dawn bite in the harbor would hold as long as bait stayed concentrated — this is the bigger version of that same story, just relocated south with more forage packed in tighter. When there's that much bunker in the water column, stripers stop caring about light levels. They just eat.

If you're running down there, don't overthink the presentation. A big topwater popper or a 6-inch soft-plastic swimbait chucked into the edge of the boil will get bit fast — the fish aren't picky, they're opportunistic. Same goes for a bunker-imitation glide bait worked just outside the melee; the bigger fish tend to sit under the school and eat the stragglers, not the ones getting worked in the middle. Keep the boat off the school itself and cast into it — running through it just scatters bait and shuts the window down early.

Back in the harbor, it's a different animal this week. Water's sitting warm — mid-70s inshore — and that's pushed the bass program back into the classic low-light pattern. Deer Island on the last two hours of the outgoing has been steady but not explosive: live mackerel drifted along the rip where the tide pulls off the flat, fish in the 26 to 32-inch range, mostly keepers with the occasional overslot mixed in. Spectacle Island's north side has been similar — eels fished slow along the drop into deeper water at first light before the sun gets high and pushes them down. Nothing blitz-worthy in the harbor proper, but a dependable pick if you're working structure correctly and hitting the tide change.

Nahant and the rocks up toward Gloucester have turned into bluefish country for now. Snapper blues are thick in the coves and river mouths — we called this building two weeks back, and it's playing out exactly like that, snappers everywhere setting up for the adult class fish that typically show as August turns to September. Right now it's mostly the smaller 1 to 3-pound blues hammering anything shiny — small Deadly Dick jigs, Kastmasters, silver spoons worked fast off the rocks at Bass Rocks and Brace Cove. A few 4 to 5-pound blues have mixed in on the outgoing, so keep a rod rigged heavier just in case. If the bunker schools that are lighting up Plymouth push north along the shore, expect that adult bluefish presence to build fast — that's the pattern to watch.

Cod and haddock offshore on Stellwagen have been a tough sell in this heat. Warm surface water has pushed whatever bottom activity there is deep, and the trips I've heard about have needed real depth — 180 feet plus — and even then it's been a slow pick, not a program. Not the priority right now with what's happening inshore.

Flounder's mostly an afterthought this time of year in the harbor — that bite really turns on again once the water starts cooling in September, so I wouldn't burn a tide targeting them right now.

Tide-wise, we're building back toward the August 28 full moon, and that means stronger current is coming both in the harbor and through the Cape Cod Canal corridor to the south. Stronger tides usually mean better current edges, which means better bait concentration, which is exactly what's driving the Plymouth pattern already. If that bunker stays tight to the beach through the weekend, I'd expect the blitz window to hold, maybe even sharpen up as the moon builds. The risk is weather — a stiff onshore blow or a push of cooler water could scatter that bait fast, and this kind of daylight event can shut off as quickly as it opened.

My plan for the coming week: mornings in the harbor working Deer Island and Spectacle Island on the last of the outgoing with live bait, then running south midday when conditions allow to check Plymouth Beach while that pogie show is still on. If the bait holds, that's where I'd put my time. If it slides offshore or gets pushed by wind, I'd shift back to the harbor rips and lean on the bluefish up around Nahant to fill the box while we wait for the next window.
