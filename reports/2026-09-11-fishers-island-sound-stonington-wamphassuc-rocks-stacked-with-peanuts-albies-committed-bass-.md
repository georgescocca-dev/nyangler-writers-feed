# Wamphassuc rocks stacked with peanuts: albies committed, bass crashing bunker on the new-moon push
*Bait is pinned tight to the boulder fields from Ram Island to Watch Hill, and everything with fins is leaning on it.*

**STONINGTON, CT — September 11** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

Peanut bunker are stacked so thick against the boulder fields off Wamphassuc Point and Ram Island this week that you can see the water pucker before anything even shows on top. That's the tell. The bait got squeezed hard by the new moon on the 10th — biggest tidal push of the young fall — and it's been pinning itself against every piece of structure from Esker Point down through Little Narragansett Bay ever since. When bait behaves like that, everything eating it starts behaving predictably too, and that's exactly what's happened out here.

We flagged this last week — said the albie run would keep pushing through the Race on the strong tides, and that's exactly what's playing out. The false albacore bite has gone from tentative pecking to a stabilized, dependable pattern across the Sound. Fish are working the rips off Fishers Island, the tide lines coming out of the Race, and the boulder-strewn water off Ram Island where the current bends around the rocks and balls the bait up tight. Bonito are mixed in on the same water, a little scattered but real — mostly picked out of the same schools chasing the albies, not holding as tight to structure. Epoxy jigs in the 1/2-ounce range and small Albie Snax in pearl or bone have been the standard, but on calm mornings with fish showing on top, a size 2 Bob Popovics-style epoxy fly on an intermediate line out of a poled skiff is still the deadliest thing you can throw at them. The fish are keying on something small — three inches, tight profile — and anything oversized gets refused hard.

We also called the bunker schools tightening under the building moon, and the stripers out here are no longer just picking at stragglers — they're committing. That's the real story of the week. Bass are working the peanut bunker pods tight against the Stonington Breakwater on the last two hours of the ebb, when the current pulls bait off the rocks and funnels it along the granite face. Live-lined bunker on a 20-pound leader, no weight, drifted right along the breakwater stones, has been drawing hard strikes from fish in the 28 to 36-inch class. Guys working bunker-colored soft plastics — 6-inch paddle tails on 3/4-ounce jig heads — off Ram Island and around the mouth of the Mystic River are getting the same grade of fish on the same tide stage. Watch Hill has been quieter for bass than the inside water, but that's typical this time of year — the exposed shoreline doesn't hold bait the way the sheltered boulder fields do.

Tog are just starting to show interest as the water eases off summer temps, though it's early yet — a handful of fish to 5 pounds have come off the Stonington Breakwater rocks on the slack, green crabs and Asian crabs fished tight to the base of the stones. That bite will build through the next few weeks as water cools further, but right now it's a bonus, not a target species. Fluke, on the other hand, have thinned out fast. We called that taper last week too, and it's holding true — the keeper-grade fish that were around Fishers Island Sound earlier in the summer have largely slid off toward deeper, cooler water, the way they do every September. What's left inshore is mostly short fish. If fluke is the priority, this isn't the week or the zone for it; the better-grade action has moved offshore to deeper structure well outside the Sound.

The why here is straightforward. New London is reading in the high 60s, Fishers Island Sound is running similar, and that's still warm enough to hold bait tight and keep predators aggressive, but it's cooling fast enough that the fall pattern is locking in rather than teasing. The bait isn't wandering — it's pinned against rock, against the breakwater, against the river mouth — and everything eating it is behaving with real purpose instead of the scattered, half-hearted pecking you get in late summer.

Looking ahead, the next several tide cycles should keep this pattern building rather than breaking down. The moon is still close enough to new that the tidal push remains strong, and as long as that bait stays concentrated against the boulder fields and the breakwater, both the albie run and the striper bite should hold steady or improve. If there's only one window to fish this week, work the last two hours of the ebb at the Stonington Breakwater for bass, then slide out to the Race or the rips off Fishers Island on the building tide for albies — the fish are moving through both in the same tidal cycle, which makes for a long, productive morning if the wind cooperates. Watch for any hard shift to sustained onshore wind, which would dirty the water along the boulder fields and push the bait — and the fish — off the rocks temporarily. Short of that, this is shaping up to be one of the better multi-species stretches the Sound sees all year.
