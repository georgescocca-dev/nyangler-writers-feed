# 78.8°F on the Bight Buoy — The Elbow Kink Paid Off Like We Called It
*A three-degree gap between shelf water and canyon blue held the yellowfin tight to the east elbow all week, but you had to find the seam to cash in.*

**HUDSON CANYON, NY — August 10** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Buoy 44025 out in the Bight read 78.8°F this week, wave heights running 2.6 feet at a six-second period out of the south-southwest — nothing that kept boats home, but enough chop to make you think twice about running the extra eight miles to the axis instead of working the wall. That 78.8 number matters because it's shelf water, not canyon water, and the gap between the two is where the whole story lived this week. On the canyon side, the break was running several degrees cooler and cleaner, and where that green-to-blue seam pinched hardest was right over the east elbow — 39°38'N, 72°32'W, give or take a half mile depending on the day. We flagged that corner last week as the one to watch if the post-front SST sharpened or kinked there, and that's exactly what happened. The eddy didn't spread thin across the shelf edge like it sometimes does in August — it pinched down tight against the 100-fathom line and held the bait, and the yellowfin, right on top of it.

That's the difference between a good trip and a long ride for nothing this week. Boats that ran straight to the classic Chicken Canyon numbers or spread out looking for color on the open bar had spotty results — a few knockdowns, a mahi or two, not much sustained action. The guys who worked the elbow, trolling green machines and Joe Shute bars with cedar plugs in the pattern, tight to where the water goes from a hazy green-blue to that gin-clear cobalt, found yellowfin stacked from 60 to 130 feet down, feeding hard on the temp break itself rather than scattered across it. Fish ran 25 to 45 pounds, mixed in with a few bigger ones that came unbuttoned before the gaff — August yellowfin on the Hudson tend to run a little leaner than the September fish, but there were enough of them to make the trolling spread worth the diesel. Skirted ballyhoo pulled slow and naked bally in the shotgun position both drew strikes, and once the fleet found the push, chunking bunker at anchor over the Dip after the trolling bite slowed turned into a steady pick through the afternoon into dusk.

Mahi were mixed in with the yellowfin along the same break, holding under any floating structure — lobster gear, weed clumps, the occasional stray pallet — and they didn't need much coaxing. A chunk of butterfish or a small spreader bar worked the edges pulled fish in the 8-to-15-pound range consistently, with a scattering of bigger bulls busting bait on the surface in clean patches of blue. If you saw birds working tight over a weed line inside that elbow zone, it was worth a stop even mid-troll.

White marlin numbers are building the way they should for mid-August, and this week bore that out — several boats reported multiple knockdowns on skirted ballyhoo pulled on the flat lines, with a few fish actually eating and coming tight. Nothing epic, but a legitimate uptick from three weeks ago, and if the pattern holds, the back half of August should see that number climb further as more fish push up the shelf edge behind the bait.

The night bite for swordfish stayed productive for the crews willing to put in the hours. Deep-dropping squid strips and whole squid on glow leads, working depths between 1,200 and 1,600 feet along the canyon's steeper contour lines off the Claw, kept a handful of boats in fish through the week. This isn't a volume bite — it's one or two quality fish a trip for guys who know how to read their sounder and hold position in current — but the swords are there, and the water column temperature profile out at that depth hasn't changed much despite the surface warming up top.

Bigeye sign showed up mixed in with the yellowfin on a couple of the deeper drops at last light, nothing consistent enough to call a pattern yet, but worth keeping an eye on if you're already rigged for the night shift. Wahoo were incidental — a couple of fish came on high-speed trolled Ilanders and wine-glass style lures worked over structure near the Texas Tower grounds, but nobody's targeting them specifically right now with the tuna and mahi keeping rods bent.

Bluefin sign has been thin to nonexistent on the Hudson itself this week — the real bluefin action right now is sitting up on the New England grounds, Stellwagen and Jeffrey's Ledge, and that's not changing in the short term. If bluefin are your target, that's not our water this week.

The full moon has kept current running stronger than usual on the drift, which is worth factoring in if you're chunking — go up a size or two on your fluorocarbon leader weight and let more line out before locking up, because the extra current pulls baits shallower and faster than you'd expect. As the moon eases past full over the next few days, expect that current to relax back to a more typical Hudson push, which should tighten up the chunk bite even further.

Best case for the week ahead: the eddy holds its pinch over the east elbow and the yellowfin stay concentrated instead of spreading out as the moon settles. If it slides east the way these things sometimes do by mid-week, I'd shift attention toward the Dip and re-check the SST before committing to a long run. Either way, find the seam first — don't just run numbers, run the break.
