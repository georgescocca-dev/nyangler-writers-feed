# Gator blues crash the Sound as bass bite turns technical in 68-degree water
*Big stripers still feeding on outgoing tides, but you need to match the hatch as water warms.*

**EXECUTION ROCKS, NY — June 24** — _by Frank "The Sound Pro" Marinaro, Zone Writer_

The Western Sound is in full summer mode now, and if you're still fishing like it's May, you're going home empty-handed. Water temps hit 68 degrees this week — that magic number where everything changes. The bass are still here, but they've gone selective, and the blues have moved in with an attitude.

I've been watching this transition for three decades, and it's happening right on schedule. The big stripers are still feeding, but they're keyed in on specific tide stages and structure. Execution Rocks has been the star performer on the outgoing water, especially that last two hours when the current really starts ripping over the ledge. The fish are staging deep — 35 to 45 feet — and you need to get down to them.

Trolling large Mojos has been the ticket for the oversized fish. I'm talking 8 to 10-inch baits, pulled slow over the structure. The morning bite has been soft, but when that afternoon outgoing tide kicks in and the water starts moving, it's lights out. Had reports of multiple 40-inch-plus fish this week, including one monster that went every bit of 50 pounds. That's Western Sound fishing at its finest.

But here's what's got me excited — the blues have arrived, and they're not the cocktail-size fish we usually see early. These are legitimate gator blues, 10 to 15 pounds, and they're absolutely crushing baitfish in the shipping channel. I've been seeing them blitzing on butterfish and spearing from Matinecock Point all the way west to the Throgs Neck. When you find them feeding on top, throw anything metal — diamond jigs, Kastmasters, even a simple bucktail with a white teaser.

The fluke bite has been steady but not spectacular. The fish are scattered from Eaton's Neck to Lloyd Harbor, mostly in that 20 to 30-foot range. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails have been producing the most consistent action. The key is working the grass edges where the sand eels are staging. I'm seeing plenty of shorts, but the keepers are there if you put in the time.

Porgies have made their appearance around the rocky structure, especially near the Stepping Stones and around the bridge pilings. Standard porgy rigs with clam have been working, but don't overlook the dock fishing opportunities. The fish are moving shallow in the early morning hours, and you can pick them on light tackle right from the bulkheads.

Here's what the conditions are telling me: that 68-degree water temp is the dividing line. Below that, the bass feed aggressively throughout the water column. Above it, they go deep and get picky. The thermal structure is setting up with cooler water on the bottom, and that's where the big fish are holding. The new moon we just had pushed a lot of bait around, but now we're heading into the full moon cycle, and that means big tides and moving water.

The squid bite has been phenomenal in the back bays — some of the best I've seen in years. They're thick around the dock lights at night, and if you like calamari or need bait for the big bass, get out there with a squid jig. The action has been consistent from Little Neck Bay all the way out to Hempstead Harbor.

Looking ahead, this full moon Friday is going to create some serious tidal movement. I'm watching for the inlet drains at sunset — that's when the weakfish typically show up for their first real push of the season. The water's warm enough now, and with all this bait around, conditions are perfect for them to move in.

The key to success right now is adapting to the conditions. The easy fishing of spring is over. You need to match your tactics to the thermal structure, work the moving water, and be ready to switch between species as opportunities present themselves. The Sound is alive with fish — you just need to fish smarter, not harder.
