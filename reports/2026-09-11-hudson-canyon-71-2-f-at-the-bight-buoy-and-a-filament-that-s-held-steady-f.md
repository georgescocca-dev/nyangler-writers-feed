# 71.2°F at the Bight Buoy — and a Filament That's Held Steady for Two Weeks
*A choppy ride out hasn't stopped a stubborn warm filament from keeping yellowfin and mahi honest through the canyon, even as bluefin stay scattered and bigeye pull the night bite deeper.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — September 11** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

The number worth knowing before anyone runs east this week is 71.2°F — that's what the NY Bight buoy is reading, with 3.9-foot seas riding a 5.0-second period. Short period like that means a sloppy, close-together sea, not a big rolling swell, and it's the kind of chop that makes for a wet ride out but settles down once boats get past the edge and into the canyon proper. That buoy sits well inside the transit line, so it's really telling the fleet about the crossing, not the bite itself. The bite itself has been dictated by something that's held in place for over two weeks now: a warm filament of water that's kept its position from the 100-fathom line out through the canyon, and it's the reason the yellowfin and mahi action hasn't fallen apart the way late-summer bites sometimes do once the first cool nights show up onshore.

That stability matters more than any single degree reading. Filaments like this move — sometimes they slide east, sometimes they collapse and mix out — and when they do, the bite usually goes with them inside 48 hours. This one hasn't budged in over a fortnight, which tells the fleet the water mass feeding the canyon edge is deep and well-established, not a thin skin of warm surface water that a few nights of cooler air will scrub out. Structure-wise, the Claw and the Dip have both been holding life along their edges where the filament brushes up against cooler water underneath — that temperature contrast is exactly what stacks bait and keeps predators working a defined line instead of scattering.

Yellowfin have been the most reliable player. Trolling spreads pulling small green machines, cedar plugs, and Zucchini-style bars in the 6- to 8-knot range along the color and temperature edges near the Dip have been drawing strikes through the morning hours, with fish running mid-30s to journeyman 50-pound class. Once the sun gets up and the troll bite softens, boats that shift to chunking — butterfish and sardines cut into a slow-sink chum slick over the deeper structure near the Claw — have kept picking away at yellowfin that drop out of the surface push but are still working the mid-column. That's a pattern worth remembering for September: the morning troll opens the door, and the midday chunk keeps it open a few more hours before the fish slide down out of casting range.

Bigeye have been more of a night fish this week, which is normal for this time of year as the surface bite tapers with the higher sun angle. The better reports have come from boats committing to an overnight anchor-up or slow drift over deep structure inside the canyon, running a chunk line with a bait suspended 100 to 150 feet down on a balloon or a slider, timed to the tide change rather than any set clock. Bigeye on this pattern have run bigger than the daytime yellowfin — fish pushing toward the century mark have shown up on 80-pound fluorocarbon leaders with a single 8/0 circle hook nose-hooked through a butterfish. It's not a bite for everyone; it takes patience and a willingness to burn a full night out there, and not every boat that tried it connected. Call it a two-out-of-five week for the overnight crowd, which is decent for early September but not a guarantee.

Mahi have stayed a bonus fish rather than a target, but a meaningful one — schoolies in the 3- to 8-pound range showing under any floating debris or weed line found along the filament edge, and they'll eat almost anything small and shiny. A few boats working spreader bars for yellowfin have peeled off mahi doubles on naked ballyhoo skipping just behind the pattern, which is worth rigging for even if mahi weren't the plan.

White marlin have been the pleasant surprise of the last two weeks, showing consistently on the troll for boats running a mixed spread of small ballyhoo and Sea Witch combinations off the flat lines near the canyon edge. Nothing about the white marlin bite has been a sure thing — it's a fish that shows up in ones and twos, not schools — but the frequency of raises has been high enough that crews are starting to rig a dedicated pitch bait for the switch-and-drop the moment a fish shows behind a teaser.

Bluefin remain the disappointing line item. Scattered reports, mostly smaller school fish mixed in with the yellowfin trolling grounds rather than a dedicated push, and nothing suggesting a concentrated body of fish has set up on the canyon edge the way they sometimes do in a good September. If that changes, it'll likely show up first as incidental hookups on the yellowfin troll before it becomes its own target-able pattern.

Looking ahead, the filament is the whole story. If it holds through the weekend the way it has for the last two weeks, expect the yellowfin troll and bigeye chunk pattern to carry through with similar numbers — not a blowup, but a dependable week's work for boats willing to run the distance and put in the night hours for bigeye. If the filament starts to slide or break apart under any wind shift, that's the signal to watch the SST charts closely before committing fuel, because a scrambled edge usually means a scrambled bite for a few days until things settle again. Sea state should ease some as the period lengthens behind this latest system, which will make the ride out more comfortable than the choppy 5-second stuff running through midweek.
