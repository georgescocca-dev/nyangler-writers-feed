# Trolled the Wilmington wall for six hours — bigeye, giant bluefin, and wahoo all showed up on the spread
*The run is long and the fuel bill isn't cheap, but late August has this canyon paying off in multiple species at once.*

**INDIAN RIVER INLET, DE — August 31** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one shot before Labor Day traffic clogs the ramps and the marina lots, the call this week is simple: run to Wilmington Canyon and troll the temperature break rather than sit on the anchor and chunk. The eddy that's been spinning off the Stream has pinned a clean, blue-water push right up against the canyon wall, and that kind of setup rewards boats covering ground with a spread out — not boats waiting for fish to come find a chunk slick. Chunking still has its window here, but it's a late-afternoon and dusk play once the sun's off the water and the current slacks. For daylight hours right now, trolling is putting more species on deck.

The why is straightforward. Warm, clean water has been holding tight along the drop, roughly where the bottom falls from the 50- to 60-fathom curve down into the canyon proper, and that edge has stacked bait against structure the way it's supposed to in late summer. The moon's building toward full over the next several days, which is going to push more tidal flow through the canyon and concentrate that bait even tighter along the wall. That's usually a green light for the bite to hold or even improve heading into the weekend — but it's also the kind of window that can turn on you fast if a tropical system organizes anywhere in the western Atlantic. That's the one thing worth watching daily before you commit to the run. Right now there's nothing forcing a change of plans, but late August is late August, and this canyon's weather can flip in 48 hours.

On the troll, the mixed bag has been the story. Bigeye tuna are coming on ballyhoo-and-skirt combos pulled off the boat's long riggers, worked slow across the temperature break at first light and again in the last two hours before dark — that's when bigeye seem willing to come up and eat instead of holding deep. Boats working spreader bars with a naked ballyhoo trailer in the middle have had the better shots at bigeye specifically, versus straight skirted-ballyhoo spreads, which have leaned more toward yellowfin. Yellowfin have been scattered but present anywhere the water color goes from green to that blue-green break — 15 to 20 knots of trolling speed, cedar plugs and small green machines in the shotgun and short rigger positions picking off fish that miss the bigger baits.

Giant-class bluefin — the true elephants — are the headline fish this week, and they're not coming on the troll. The producing method has been live-lining once the fish are marked or a school is spotted working bait on top: bridled live bait, whether that's a bigger bunker imitation, a live mackerel, or whatever's around the boat, sent back on a long flat line or slow-trolled just off the surface. Patience matters more than gear here — boats willing to sit on marked fish and let a bait swim naturally are the ones connecting on the big bluefin, not the ones running and gunning.

Wahoo have shown up as a bonus on the high-speed presentations — planers and heavy trolling weights pulling single skirted lures or a Ilander/ballyhoo combo at 12 to 14 knots along the same temperature break that's holding the tuna. If you're not rigging at least one or two rods for wahoo speed, you're leaving fish on the table right now, because they've been mixed in more than usual for this time of year.

Mahi have been the consolation prize around any floating structure, weed lines, or current seams inside the canyon — small stuff mostly, schoolies in the 3- to 8-pound range, but eager, and a few gaffer-class fish have come off deeper structure edges. Small skirted ballyhoo or a dropped bucktail once you spot the pod will get bit fast.

White marlin are still in the mix on dredge teams — dark, natural-colored dredges with a spread of ballyhoo skirts and dredge teasers pulled just off the transom, matched with a light bait or naked ballyhoo pitch bait ready when a fish shows in the spread. It's not a stacked bite, more a few quality shots per trip for boats running a proper dredge program and staying patient when a fish shows interest but won't commit on the first pass.

Be honest with yourself about the economics before you run. This is not a nearshore trip — the fuel burn to reach the canyon and work the grounds properly adds up fast, and a slow bigeye bite doesn't offset that math the way a good yellowfin day does. Right now the multi-species mix — bigeye, giant bluefin, yellowfin, wahoo, mahi, and white marlin all realistically in play on the same day — makes the run worth it for boats set up to fish more than one program. If you're only rigged to troll, you're leaving the bluefin opportunity on the table; if you're only rigged to chunk or live-bait, you're missing the wahoo and mahi.

Looking at the next few days, the pattern should hold if the tropics stay quiet. Building tide into the full moon should tighten the bait concentrations along the wall and could sharpen the bigeye and bluefin bite even further heading into the holiday weekend. Expect more boat traffic on the grounds as Labor Day approaches — get an early start if you want clean water and room to work the spread before the fleet spreads out. Keep one eye on any tropical development between now and then; that's the single factor that could shut this window down fast.
