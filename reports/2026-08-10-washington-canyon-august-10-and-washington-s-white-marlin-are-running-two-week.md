# August 10, and Washington's White Marlin Are Running Two Weeks Ahead of Plan
*The volume yellowfin bite is holding on the eastern wall while billfish numbers build faster than the calendar says they should.*

**OCEAN CITY, MD — August 10** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Every August I get the same question at the fuel dock: is Washington actually ahead of the pack, or do I just say that because it's my canyon? Here's the honest answer — this year it's not close. By the second week of August, Cape May boats are still hunting for consistency, Baltimore Canyon's still sorting out where the good water sits, and we've already had ten days of stacked yellowfin on the eastern wall with white marlin showing in numbers you'd expect closer to Labor Day. That's not luck. That's the Gulf Stream doing what it does off this stretch of the shelf — pinching warm filaments up against the canyon edge earlier than anywhere north of Hatteras, and this year it arrived right on schedule.

We flagged this back on the 3rd and again on the 6th — said the warm-water filaments would keep tuna concentrated on the break and that billfish numbers would build the way they should in early August. That's exactly what's happened. The yellowfin bite has been the bread-and-butter fishery all week, working the temperature break along the 50 to 60-fathom curve on the west and southwest walls, where the color change from canyon blue to green Mid-Atlantic water sets up a hard edge. Boats running spreader bars with cedar plugs up top and naked ballyhoo in the shotgun are getting bit consistently between 6:30 and 9 a.m., then again in the last two hours of daylight. Fifty to seventy pound class fish, mixed in with some smaller schoolies that'll test 20 to 30. Nothing giant, but steady — three to eight fish days for boats that stay on the troll and don't chase birds around all morning.

Mahi have been the bonus fish, and a good one. Anything holding structure — a lobster pot buoy, a mat of sargassum, a stray board — is holding fish. Small spinning outfits with quarter-ounce jigs, chartreuse or pink, worked around the pot lines have been putting dolphin in the box in the 8 to 15 pound range, with the occasional bull pushing 25. If you see one come up, stop the boat and pitch — they run in packs and the second and third fish are usually right behind the first.

White marlin is where Washington's really separating itself from the rest of the fleet right now. Numbers are building the way I'd expect for mid-August, but what's notable is how far along we already are — guys working dink ballyhoo and small circle hooks off the switch baits are getting into multiple releases per trip on good days, mostly along that same eastern wall where the water's held clean and blue in the mid-to-upper 70s. It's not a guaranteed six-bite day every trip — I had one boat radio in Thursday with just two bites all day after a slow start, then another crew twenty minutes away doubled up three times in an hour. That's canyon fishing in August — the fish are there, but you've got to find the exact seam where the temp break, the current line, and the bait are all lined up at once.

Wahoo have been a wildcard worth working for if you've got the gear. High-speed trolling with deep-diving lures — the heavy Braid or Ilander/Sea Witch combos rigged on wire — along the drop-off edges in 80 to 120 fathoms has produced a few nice fish in the 40 to 60 pound range for boats willing to run that speed spread alongside the slow-troll tuna gear. It's not a volume fishery right now, more a bonus if you're set up for it.

Bigeye have shown at night for the chunk-and-drift crews anchored up on the edge in 700 to 900 feet, working butterfish chunks on circle hooks with the lights down and patience up. Slow, but real — a couple of boats have iced fish over 150 pounds in the last week doing exactly that. Bluefin sign, for what it's worth, has been mostly a New England story this month — Stellwagen, Jeffrey's Ledge, even close to midcoast Maine — not really a Washington pattern in August, and I wouldn't burn fuel looking for them down here right now.

Conditions have cooperated. Surface temps on the wall have held in that mid-to-upper 70s range that keeps everything — yellowfin, mahi, white marlin — comfortable and feeding, and the moon's swinging past full this week, which has sharpened the dawn and dusk bite windows a little more than usual. If I had one day to fish this week, I'd run the eastern wall early, work the temp break hard on the troll until 9, then switch over to dink ballyhoo once the sun's up and the marlin start showing on the surface. Watch the afternoon sea breeze building through midweek — nothing that should shut down a canyon trip, but worth checking before you commit to a long run. Beyond that, keep an eye on the tropics; nothing's organized yet, but late August is when these systems start sending swell up the shelf, and Washington's the first canyon that feels it.
