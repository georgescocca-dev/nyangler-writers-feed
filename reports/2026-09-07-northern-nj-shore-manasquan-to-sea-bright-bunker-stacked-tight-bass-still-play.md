# Manasquan to Sea Bright: Bunker Stacked Tight, Bass Still Playing Hard to Get
*The bait's here, the blues are eating, and the rocks are waiting on the moon to finish the job.*

**SEA BRIGHT, NJ — September 7** — _by Brendan Doyle, Zone Writer_

First light on the Sea Bright rocks this week looked like a preview of something bigger than what actually showed up. Three, four guys spread out on the north jetty before the sun cleared the ridgeline, bunker spoons and darters going out over pods of peanut bunker that had balled up so tight against the beach you could've scooped them with a net. Blues were on them — not every cast, but enough. A guy working a white bucktail off the third rock down had two follows and a hookup in the first twenty minutes, then it went quiet until the tide turned. That's been the rhythm all week up and down this stretch: bunker everywhere, blues committed, bass mostly watching from the cheap seats.

We flagged this coming — said the building moon toward full would tighten these bait schools and that the first real fall blitz was likely within the week to ten days. The full moon hit last night into this morning, and sure enough the bunker are balled tighter than they've been all September, stacked from Sea Bright down through Belmar and into the Manasquan Inlet mouth. But tight bait under a bright full moon often means the bass feed hard at night and go quiet at dawn — moonlight lets them work the schools in the dark without needing the low-light window. That's exactly what the rocks are telling us. Night guys throwing swimmers and darters along the Sea Bright wall have had better luck than the dawn patrol standing in the same spot four hours later.

The next real trigger is the new moon on September 10. That's when the tide swing builds again, current gets stronger through the inlets, and those bunker schools get pushed and squeezed instead of just cruising along casually. When that happens, expect the first legitimate surface blitz of the fall — bass finally committing to bunker instead of picking at the edges. Manasquan Inlet, both jetties, on the back half of the outgoing tide is the highest-percentage spot to be standing when it flips on. Same goes for the Shark River Inlet jetties on the last two hours of the ebb, when bait gets funneled out through the throat and stripers stage right on the edge of the channel to ambush it.

Fluke are winding down but not dead yet. The bite in Shark River Inlet, working the channel edges and the deeper holes off the Route 35 bridge, is still giving up keeper fish on bucktail-and-Gulp combos — white or pink swimming mullet on a ¾-ounce bucktail, dragged slow along bottom in 15 to 20 feet on the last of the incoming. Live killies or peanut bunker on a simple fish-finder rig have been outproducing plastics for guys willing to fish bait instead of working it. Manasquan Inlet's channel has produced a few doormat-class fish too, but the overall numbers are thinning fast as water starts to cool at night. This is the tail end of the window — another two, maybe three weeks before the fluke clear out for deeper water.

Bluefish are the one thing nobody's complaining about right now. They're on the bunker from Sea Bright rocks down through Asbury Park and into the Spring Lake jetties, blitzing on the surface in short, violent windows — usually the first hour of light and again an hour before dark. Ava 27s and 37s, jigged and let flutter through the melee, are getting bit as fast as you can drop them. Bunker spoons trolled or cast and cranked fast along the jetty faces are just as effective if you don't want to jig. Sizes have been decent — 3 to 6 pounds mostly, with the occasional bigger cow mixed in tearing through smaller peanuts.

Striper action along the beach has been a mixed bag, which is the honest way to put it. Some slot fish, low to mid 20s, have come off live bunker chunks fished on the bottom at the north jetty in Manasquan on the outgoing tide, mostly in the last hour before low. A few guys working topwater plugs — Danny pins and Super Strike poppers — at Spring Lake and Belmar jetties around first light have connected on schoolies mixed with the blues, but nobody's stacking limits yet. That's the story right now: the ingredients are all sitting on the table — tight bait, cooling nights, a strong tide about to build — but the dish isn't cooked. It's close.

Porgies remain a dependable side dish for anybody bored waiting on bass to show up. Shark River Inlet, fishing bloodworms or clam on bottom rigs around the bulkheads and the Route 35 bridge pilings, has kept steady action going on the slower tide stages when the blitz stuff shuts down.

Looking at the next several days: expect the blues to stay reliable on bunker pods the length of this beat, expect fluke to keep fading gradually rather than dropping off a cliff, and watch the calendar hard for September 10. If the new moon tide does what it's supposed to and pushes those bunker schools into the inlets under real current, the first honest blitz of the fall should follow within a day or two of that push — not guaranteed to the hour, but that's the setup. Best bet for anyone with limited time: fish the outgoing at Manasquan or Shark River Inlet in that window, bring both a topwater plug and a bunker chunk rig, and don't leave the rocks just because the first hour was quiet. This time of year, the good stuff shows up late or not at all — there's no in-between.
