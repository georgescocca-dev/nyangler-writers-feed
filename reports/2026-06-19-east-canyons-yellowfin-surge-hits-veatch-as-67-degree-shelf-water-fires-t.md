# Yellowfin surge hits Veatch as 67-degree shelf water fires the eastern canyons
*Canyon water warmer than inshore — offshore trolling window wide open for early mahi.*

**MONTAUK, NY — June 19** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

The eastern canyons are lighting up this week as shelf water hit the magic 67-degree mark that gets yellowfin tuna feeding hard. I've been watching the buoy readings climb steadily, and when that 30-mile south of Islip station locked in at 67.3 degrees, I knew it was time to make the run.

Veatch Canyon has been the star performer, with yellowfin in the 40 to 80-pound class crashing spreader bars and cedar plugs in 200 to 400 feet of water. The fish are holding along the northwest wall where the thermocline sets up around 60 feet, creating that perfect temperature break that stacks bait. I'm running a mix of Ilander-ballyhoo combos on the long riggers and pulling daisy chains of small cedar plugs on the short lines. The key has been keeping your speed between 7.5 and 8.5 knots — any faster and you're pulling over the fish, any slower and the lures lose their action.

What's really interesting this week is how the canyon water is actually running warmer than the inshore stuff. Block Island is sitting at 62.4 degrees while we're seeing 67-plus out at the shelf break. That's backwards from the usual pattern, and it's creating a feeding surge as bait gets pushed offshore into that warmer water mass. The yellowfin are following, and they're hungry.

Atlantis Canyon has been producing solid mahi action in the 10 to 25-pound range, with most fish coming on the troll in 150 to 300 feet. The dolphin are hitting anything with green in it — green machine Ilanders, green and yellow feathers, even chartreuse cedar plugs are getting crushed. The trick has been finding the weed lines and floating debris where the mahi stack up. With this southwest wind pattern we've had, the Sargasso weed has been pushed up against the canyon edges, creating perfect ambush points.

Hydrographer Canyon gave up some nice wahoo early in the week before the wind picked up. The 'hoos were hitting high-speed trolled Rapala X-Raps and Yo-Zuri Bonitas in the 30 to 50-pound class. You need to be running 12 to 15 knots to trigger these fish, and wire leader is mandatory — I learned that the hard way after getting cut off three times on fluorocarbon.

The rough stuff building from the southwest is making the ride home interesting, but the fishing conditions themselves have been solid. Block Island is showing 8-foot seas at 8-second intervals, which means it's going to be sporty out there, but not unfishable if you time your departure right. The key is getting out early and being willing to run home in the afternoon chop.

Bait has been the story this week. The shelf is loaded with sand eels, small mackerel, and juvenile butterfish. I'm seeing massive bait clouds on the fishfinder from 40 feet down to 120 feet, and that's what's drawing the yellowfin up from the deep water. When you mark bait like that, you know the big fish aren't far behind.

The moon phase is working in our favor too. We're coming off a new moon, which means the tides are starting to build toward springs. That increased current flow is keeping the bait active and the predators feeding. I've been timing my canyon runs to hit the slack water periods when the fish are most likely to be up in the water column.

Looking ahead, I'm watching for this southwest wind to clock around to the west and then northwest by the weekend. That should knock down the seas and make for better running conditions. More importantly, a wind shift like that often triggers a feeding spree as it changes the current patterns and pushes fresh bait into the canyons. If the water temperature holds in the upper 60s, we could be looking at some epic yellowfin fishing through the weekend and into next week.
