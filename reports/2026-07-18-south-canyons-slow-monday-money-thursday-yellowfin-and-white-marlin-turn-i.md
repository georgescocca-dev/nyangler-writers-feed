# Slow Monday, Money Thursday: Yellowfin and White Marlin Turn It Around at Spencer
*A rough start to the week gave way to a warm-water push that put chunk bites and marlin flags up across the southern grounds.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 18** — _by Capt. Ray Lugo, Zone Writer_

Give this week a C-plus. Monday and Tuesday were a wash — a weak frontal boundary kicked up a sloppy 3 to 4 foot sea out past the 60-fathom curve and killed two charters before they even got their spreads wet. But Wednesday the wind laid down, the swell went from lumpy to lazy, and by Thursday we were running four boats out of Cape May and Ocean City into some of the better mixed-bag fishing I've had all July. That's the canyon game — you eat the bad days so you're ready when the good ones show up.

Spencer was the story of the week. We ran out to the northeast edge, working the 100-fathom curve around 38°44'N/73°52'W, and found a hard temperature break where warm Gulf Stream filament water was shouldering up against the cooler shelf water — the kind of seam that stacks bait and holds fish tight to the edge instead of scattering them across open water. Trolled a standard ballyhoo-and-Islander spread through there Thursday morning and connected on yellowfin in the 35 to 55 pound class, six for nine on the day, plus a double-header of white marlin that came off a naked ballyhoo pulled long on the flat line. We flagged this pattern last week — said the moon-driven current push across the south canyon shelf breaks would concentrate bait right along that edge, and that's exactly what we found. The white marlin bite in particular has been building since the Fourth, and Thursday's action fits right into that seasonal climb — expect more flags through late July as more fish filter up from the south.

Lindenkohl was quieter but not dead. We worked the 90 to 120 fathom stretch off the northwest corner and had a decent night chunk bite on butterfish — three bigeye between 60 and 90 pounds, all in that two-to-four AM window when the current went slack and the fish came up off the bottom structure to feed. If you're going to fish Lindenkohl right now, don't waste daylight trolling the middle — anchor up or drift the edge after dark with a slow chunk line and a couple of baits soaked deep on 200 feet of leader. That's where the bigeye have been holding.

Poor Man's did what Poor Man's does this time of year — mahi. Lots of them, mostly schoolie-size in the 5 to 12 pound range, stacked up under any floating junk we found working the color change closer in, around the 40 to 50 fathom line. Small spreader bars and green machines with a naked ballyhoo teaser pulled fish up fast, and a few times we had triples going off the corner. Not a tuna show out there this week, but if you want a fast, fun day and a cooler full of dinner, that's your spot. We did mark one respectable wahoo on the troll near a weed line — high-speed lure, planer down about 30 feet, single strike, no hookup. Worth keeping a wahoo bait in the spread out there even when you're primarily running for mahi.

Norfolk was the longest run and the toughest call this week, but the guys who made the trip weren't disappointed. Working out past the 150-fathom break on the south wall, we had a solid yellowfin bite mid-morning Friday — fish in the 40 to 60 pound range eating cedar plugs and small green/yellow machines pulled fast, 7 to 8 knots, classic tuna trolling speed. There was also a single bigeye pulled off a deep drop bait fished on the down-current side of the temperature break, which tells me that warm water is still holding structure down there even though it's a longer haul than most weekend boats want to make.

The pattern behind all of this is straightforward: the stable high pressure we've had since midweek let the warm-core Gulf Stream water settle in and hold its shape instead of getting torn apart by wind-driven mixing. That's kept bait concentrated on the shelf breaks across all four canyons, and it's why the troll bite has been steady rather than explosive — fish are there, but they're locked onto specific edges rather than roaming open water. Night chunking has outproduced daytime trolling at Lindenkohl and Norfolk specifically because that's where the bigger, more cautious bigeye class fish are sitting deep during daylight and coming up after dark.

Looking ahead, if this high-pressure pattern holds through the weekend — and right now there's no reason to think it won't — I'd expect the yellowfin bite at Spencer to keep building along that same temperature seam, and I'd keep a rod rigged for white marlin any time you're trolling that edge, because the flag count has been climbing steadily since the holiday. Poor Man's should keep producing mahi as long as there's floating structure to hold them, so scan hard for weed lines and debris on the way out rather than running straight to a number. Norfolk is the wildcard — it's a long run, but the bigeye and yellowfin combo down there has been worth the fuel for anyone willing to make the trip and fish it properly after dark. If the wind stays out of the southwest under 15 knots, that's where I'd point the bow next.
