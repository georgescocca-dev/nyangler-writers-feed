# Bay water hits 69 as weakfish crash the inlet and fluke fishing picks up steam
*Keeper weakfish showing in numbers while slot bass move out on warming water.*

**JAMAICA BAY, NY — June 23** — _by Denise "Dee" Vasquez, Zone Writer_

The bay hit 69 degrees this week and everything changed. The water's warm enough now that those big slot bass are starting to push out toward cooler ocean water, but before you get bummed about that, let me tell you what's moving in to take their place — weakfish. Real weakfish, not the scattered singles we've been seeing, but honest schools of them staging up in the deeper holes.

I've been watching this transition happen every June for twenty years, and it's like clockwork. Once bay water climbs past 68, the bass start their summer shuffle toward the inlet and ocean side, but the weakies love this warm water. They're stacking up in 15 to 20 feet from the Cross Bay Bridge all the way down to the Marine Parkway, and they're hungry.

The best action has been on the incoming tide, especially that last hour before slack. I'm talking keeper fish to 19 inches, with some pushing over 20. The technique that's been money is a simple bucktail and teaser rig — white or chartreuse bucktail on the bottom, small white teaser about 18 inches up the leader. Drag it slow on the drift, let it tick bottom every few bounces. When they hit, they hit hard.

Fluke fishing has finally turned the corner too. Water this warm gets them active, and I'm seeing keepers mixed in with the usual shorts parade. The 25-footer that came over the rail near the Marine Parkway Bridge this week was a beauty — thick as a doormat and probably pushing six pounds. That fish hit a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, worked slow in 30 feet on the outgoing.

Here's the thing about fluke in the bay right now — they're not aggressive. These are lazy, warm-water bites that feel like dead weight when you lift the rod. You need to slow everything down. I've been using longer leaders, lighter jig heads when I can get away with it, and really focusing on that subtle lift-and-drop retrieve that keeps the bait just off bottom.

The bunker situation is interesting. There's still bait in the bay, but it's thinning out and getting scattered. What bunker is left is mostly small stuff, peanuts and juvenile fish that the bass were crushing a few weeks back. The big adult bunker have mostly moved out to deeper, cooler water, which explains why those slot bass are following them.

For the chunkers still targeting bass, the North Channel Bridge area near Howard Beach has been producing. Sunset bite on the slack-to-incoming transition, bunker chunks on fishfinder rigs. The key is fresh bait — those peanut bunker need to be bleeding and smelling right. I've been seeing fish in the 28 to 32-inch range, mostly catch-and-release stuff, but they're there if you put in the time.

The water's been dirty from all the recent rain, that chocolate-brown color that makes you wonder if the fish can even see your bait. But here's the thing — in dirty water, scent becomes everything. Fresh bunker, bloodworms, even those Gulp baits that smell like low tide, they all work better when visibility is down.

Looking ahead, this warming trend is going to continue pushing the bass toward the inlet and ocean side. But that's not necessarily bad news. The inlet can fish lights-out in summer when conditions line up right — incoming tide, moving water, bait getting flushed out of the bay. And with weakfish finally showing in numbers, we've got a legitimate target species that actually likes this warm water.

Next week's new moon is going to create some serious tidal flow. Those spring tides will flush a lot of bait out of the back bay areas, and I'm expecting the inlet to light up. If you're boat fishing, focus on the deeper holes on the incoming. If you're working from shore, the Marine Parkway Bridge and Cross Bay areas should produce on the moving water.

The summer pattern is setting up exactly like it should. Warm bay water, scattered bait, weakfish moving in, bass moving toward cooler water. It's a transition period, but if you fish it right, it can be some of the most consistent action of the year.
