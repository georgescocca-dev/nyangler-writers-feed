# River Warms Past 70, Stripers Slide to the Night Tide at Saybrook Point
*Shad are a memory, snapper blues are thick in the coves, and summer flounder are finally showing where we said they would.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — July 18** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The silver maples along the Essex shoreline have that dusty, heavy-leafed look they get by mid-July, and the ospreys over Great Island are feeding fledglings now instead of sitting eggs. That's how I know where we are on this river without looking at a thermometer. The shad run wrapped up back in early June — those fish are long done spawning and gone back to sea, and if you're still swinging a shad dart above the Baldwin Bridge you're fishing a calendar that's already turned the page. This time of year the river tells a different story, and it's mostly a story about heat.

The water at the mouth has pushed into the low-to-mid 70s, tracking with what we're seeing out in the Sound — Kings Point up around 71.6, water off the Race and into Fishers Island Sound running similarly warm. That's plenty comfortable for a striped bass to tolerate, but not comfortable enough for one to want to feed hard in broad daylight anymore. I've watched this happen for fifty summers now: once the river mouth and the flats off Great Island cross that 70-degree mark and hold there, the bass go nocturnal. They don't leave — they just get smart about when they show themselves. We flagged this pattern last week, that the stripers would stay in the river right through summer, and that's exactly what's playing out, just not on the schedule a lot of guys expect.

My best fish this week came at 9:40 p.m. off the Saybrook Point breakwater, working the last of the outgoing into dead low, an eel drifted on a fish-finder rig with just enough weight to hold bottom in eight feet of water. Two bass in the 26 to 30-inch range came off that same rock pile in under an hour, then it went dead quiet for the rest of the tide. That's the pattern right now — short, sharp windows right around the tide change, particularly the last two hours of the ebb when the river's cooler water is still pushing out past the warmer Sound water and creating that current seam off the mouth. If you're fishing bucktails instead of eels, go dark colors — black or purple, half-ounce to three-quarter, and work them slow along the drop where the channel falls off toward Cornfield Point. Daytime, don't waste your good tide on the open flats. Push up into the shade lines along Old Lyme's marsh creeks or work the deeper water off the DEP Marina where there's at least a degree or two of relief.

Bluefish are the noisier story this week, and they're consistent with what we expected — bunker have been holding thick from the river mouth on down the beachfront, and where there's bunker there are blues. Snapper blues, some no bigger than your hand, are chopping through the coves at Essex and up around the Great Island flats on any incoming tide with sun on the water. Kids with a bobber and a sliver of cut bait can load up an afternoon on those. But there are bigger blues mixed in too — three- and four-pounders working the outer edges of the bunker schools right at the river mouth, and those will hit a Hopkins or a small metal-lipped swimmer worked fast through the froth when you see the surface nervous water. Best window there has been early morning, right at first light, before the boat traffic pushes everything down.

And here's the one I'll take some credit for — summer flounder have started showing in the lower river exactly like we said they would. Not a banner bite yet, but real fish: a few keepers to 18 inches coming off the sandy stretches near the mouth on the last of the incoming, drifting Gulp in white or chartreuse on a light jig head, three-quarter ounce, bounced slow along bottom in ten to fifteen feet. If you're marking bait on the graph near Cornfield Point or along the edge of the main channel off Saybrook, that's worth a few drifts. It's early yet for this pattern to be dependable — I'd call it two encouraging trips out of five so far — but the trend is right, and it should build through late July as more fluke work up out of the Sound looking for cooler bottom.

White perch, meanwhile, are doing what white perch always do in high summer — holding tight to the deeper bends above Essex, biting best on the last hour of daylight into dusk on small pieces of bloodworm or a tiny beetle-spin worked slow along the mud. Not glamorous fishing, but it's steady, and it's a good one to fall back on if the tide's wrong for the mouth.

We're heading toward the first quarter moon on the 21st, which means the tidal swings are easing rather than building — that generally favors the slower presentations I mentioned over anything you'd want to burn through fast current. If I had one night to fish this week, I'd be back at Saybrook Point on the evening ebb with an eel in hand, and I'd have a bucktail rigged as backup in case the current doesn't set up the way I expect. That's been the honest read on this river for fifty years running — the bass are here, you just have to fish the hours they're willing to give you.
