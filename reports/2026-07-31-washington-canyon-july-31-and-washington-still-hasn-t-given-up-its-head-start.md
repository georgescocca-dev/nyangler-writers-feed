# July 31 and Washington Still Hasn't Given Up Its Head Start
*A Gulf Stream filament pushing farther north than it should this late in July is keeping yellowfin, some bigeye, and scattered mahi stacked on the break while the other canyons play catch-up.*

**OCEAN CITY, MD — July 31** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Every year around this time I get the same question at the fuel dock: why does Washington fish two, three weeks ahead of Wilmington and Baltimore when they're barely thirty miles apart on the chart? The answer's the same every year too — geometry. Washington sits where the shelf break kinks just right to catch Gulf Stream filaments before they wrap north, and this July that filament came in early and it's still sitting there, stubborn, like it doesn't know the calendar says August is two days away.

I've had the boat on the color change most of this past week, working the edge that's set up loosely between 38°08'N and 38°14'N, 73°58'W to 74°03'W — call it the northeast corner of the canyon proper, where the 100-fathom curve starts to roll over into the real drop. Surface temp on the blue side of that break has been running warm, low-to-mid 70s, with a hard edge you can see in the water color even before the machine confirms it. That's the kind of break that holds fish, and it's held them for going on three weeks now without sliding on us. We flagged this pattern holding back on the 24th — said stable weather would keep the action steady — and here we are at the end of the month with the same filament still parked in the same water.

Yellowfin have been the bread and butter, mostly 25 to 45 pounds, coming on the troll along that break in the mid-morning and again in the last two hours of light. Green machines and small cedar plugs in the pattern, run tight to the boat, have been drawing the first strikes, and once you find the school a naked ballyhoo behind a Sea Witch will keep them interested longer than the hard plastics will. Depth's not really the story here — these are surface-oriented fish riding the temperature break, not fish you're marking on structure at 900 feet. The break itself is the structure.

Bigeye have shown, but it's a night program, and it's been hit or miss. Boats willing to sit on the edge after dark and chunk butterfish over a chum slick in 700 to 1,100 feet of water have taken a few in the 60-to-90-pound class, mostly on the second or third drift once the slick's had time to build. I wouldn't call it consistent — for every boat that connected this week, I heard from two that sat all night and never got a real bite window. That's bigeye fishing in late July on this canyon; you're buying a lottery ticket with better odds than most, not a sure thing.

Mahi have been scattered rather than schooled up — the kind of bite where you're picking off a pair here, a single there, off any bit of floating structure or weed line that's held together in this wind. Small stuff, mostly schoolies in the 5-to-12-pound range, though I did hear of a mixed pod pushing 20 pounds working bait balls a little south of the main break, closer to 38°02'N. Bailer-style with a spinning rod and a small skirted ballyhoo or a bubblegum-pink jig has been the fastest way to pick through them once you stop the boat.

White marlin have been around the edges of that same temp break, and we've had a handful of bites on the dredge — naked ballyhoo behind a squid chain, dropped back soft, has drawn the eats. Nothing to write home about in numbers, two or three raises on a good day, but the fish are there and they're eating when you get the presentation right. Wahoo have been more of a bonus than a target — a couple boats high-speed trolling the drop with Ilanders and wire leader picked off singles in the 30-to-50-pound range working the same stretch of break where the yellowfin have been holding. That tells you something: when the temperature line is this well defined, everything from mahi to wahoo to tuna is stacking on it rather than spreading out across the whole canyon.

I want to be straight with you about the week: it hasn't been a banner bite. It's been a good, honest, working-man's bite — you find the break, you work it methodically, and you get rewarded, but it's not the kind of week where every trolling rod goes off at once. The fish are concentrated on a narrow seam, and if you're not sitting on that seam you're not getting bit. Boats running blind lines north or south of that 38°08' to 38°14' stretch came home with a lot less to show for their fuel.

Looking into next week, the pattern I'd bet on holding is the filament itself — there's nothing in the wind or pressure setup that suggests it's sliding out anytime soon, and with the tide cycle easing toward neap before building back toward the August 12 new moon, I expect bait and predators to stay fairly concentrated rather than spread thin. If a thunderstorm rolls through midweek like they tend to in early August, it might knock a day off the calendar, but it shouldn't move that break more than a few miles. My plan is to keep working that northeast corner early and give the night bigeye program one more shot before the moon starts building — if the filament holds position like it has all month, I don't see a reason to look elsewhere yet.
