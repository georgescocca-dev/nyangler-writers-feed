# Wild Rice Greening the Coves — Bass Hug the Current, Fluke Show at the River Mouth
*With the river running warm and slack water gone soft, Lower Connecticut stripers and a fresh wave of summer flounder are both keying on moving water this week.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — July 21** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The wild rice is standing tall and green in the coves off Great Island now, thick enough that the marsh hens are working it, and the osprey chicks up on the Nott Island poles are down to their last few weeks before they're flying full circuits over the flats. That's where my calendar sits every July — shad long gone, the spring run just a memory by Memorial Day, and the river settled into its deep-summer rhythm. I've watched this happen fifty times now, and it never fails to surprise new guys how much the fishing changes once the water goes from cool-and-pushy in May to warm-and-lazy in late July.

The river itself is running warm in the coves and flats — the kind of warm that makes a striper sluggish and looking for a reason to move. But the main stem, especially from the Baldwin Bridge down through the Essex Elbow to Saybrook Point, is still getting a flush of cooler, oxygenated water off the Sound on every tide change. Kings Point over on the west end of the Sound is reading close to 71 degrees, New London out east closer to 69 — and Saybrook sits right in that gradient, catching the cooler edge of it on the incoming. That's exactly why the bass in this stretch haven't scattered into total lockjaw the way some of the western Sound backwaters do in a heat spell. They've just gotten smarter about when they'll eat.

Striped bass are holding tight to structure and current — the rock pile off Saybrook Point, the pilings under the Baldwin Bridge, the deep bend by Calves Island, and the channel edge off Nott Island where the current really rips on the ebb. Daytime, with the sun high and the flats baking, those fish are down in the deeper bends where the moving water keeps things livable. Dawn and dusk is when they'll slide up onto the edges to feed — I had two customers working live eels off the Point breakwater last Tuesday at first light, and they took a 28-inch fish and a schoolie inside twenty minutes before the bite went quiet as the sun came up. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, tight to the current seam below the bridge, have also been producing after dark — that's been the more consistent pattern than daylight casting right now. If you're set on daytime fish, work the deepest part of the channel bends slow, with a bucktail or a soft plastic swimbait bounced right along bottom where the current's still moving something.

White perch have pushed up out of the warmest shallow flats and into the deeper holes — Selden Creek, the mouth of Whalebone Creek, the deeper bends up toward Essex. A simple grass shrimp rig or a small piece of bloodworm on a perch spreader, fished slow along bottom in eight to fifteen feet, has been steady, not spectacular. It's a good afternoon fishery when the bass have gone quiet in the heat — not the kind of thing that fills a cooler fast, but reliable if you're patient and willing to move a little to find where they've stacked up.

Bluefish have been showing at the outer edge of the river mouth, out around the Griswold Point bar and the sandbar off the outer breakwater, chasing peanut bunker on the falling tide. It's not a blitz — more a scattered pattern of blues cutting through bait pods for twenty or thirty minutes at a time before moving off — but if you're out there on the last two hours of the ebb with a metal jig or a bunker spoon, you can find them.

We flagged it last week and it's holding up — summer flounder are pushing into the lower river now, right on schedule. I've had reports of keeper-class fluke coming off the sand flats inside the breakwater at Saybrook Point, drifted with three-inch white Gulp swim mullets on quarter-ounce jigheads, fished right on the bottom where that sand gives way to the channel edge. It's not a heavy showing yet, more a scout wave than a full push, but it's exactly the pattern I'd expect as water keeps warming through late July.

We're coming off the first-quarter moon into a neap tide cycle, which means less current overall through the next several days. That's going to soften up some of these current-dependent spots — the bridge pilings and the Point breakwater won't be ripping as hard on peak tide, which actually widens your window to fish them well past the top of the flood or ebb rather than just the hour around max current. I'd use that to my advantage this week — fish longer stretches of the tide instead of racing to catch the peak.

Looking ahead, I don't expect a dramatic change through the end of July. The river will stay warm in the coves, the bass will keep holding to the moving water and the low-light windows, and I'd expect that fluke push to build a little more as the week goes on. If I had one morning to fish, I'd be at the Point at first light on the last of the incoming, eel in hand, betting on that cooler water doing what it's done for fifty years running — pulling the bass up to feed before the sun gets high enough to send them back down.
