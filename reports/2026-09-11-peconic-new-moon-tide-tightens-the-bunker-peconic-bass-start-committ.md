# New moon tide tightens the bunker — Peconic bass start committing for real
*Porgies stay thick around Shelter Island, fluke thin out to the better-grade fish, and the scallop opener is close enough to start asking about the set.*

**GREENPORT, NY — September 11** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

The Peconic runs on its own clock, and right now that clock says early fall is arriving on schedule. The bay warmed ahead of the ocean all summer, and now it's cooling the same way — a few degrees quicker than the open Sound, which is still holding in the upper 60s to low 70s at stations like Kings Point and New London. That gap matters. It means the bunker schools that have been loafing around Gardiners Bay and the mouth of the Peconic all August are getting squeezed as the water eases, and Tuesday's new moon gave that squeeze some real teeth. The tide swung harder than it has in weeks, and for the first time this fall the bass aren't just picking at the edges of the bait — they're committing.

We flagged this pattern last week, saying bass activity would build as bunker schools tightened under the strengthening moon, and that's exactly the shape of things now. Boats working Orient Point, the Gut, and the flats off Nassau Point are finding bunker balled up tight against the beach on the last two hours of the ebb, with bass working underneath and occasionally busting the surface when a bigger push of current squeezes the school against structure. It's not blitz conditions yet — most of what's showing is subsurface activity, birds working nervously rather than diving hard — but the commitment level is up from where it was two weeks ago, and that's the story right now.

Plum Gut remains the engine room for this whole pattern. On the tail of the ebb, with the current ripping west out of the Gut and bunker getting funneled through, bass are stacking on the down-current side of the rip, particularly on the Orient side where the bottom breaks toward deeper water. Snag-and-drop with a live bunker on a fish-finder rig is still the most reliable way to connect with the bigger fish in that current, though bucktails in the 1.5 to 2-ounce range, worked with a slow lift-and-drop through the rip, are picking up fish too — especially in the last hour of daylight when the light angle drops and the bass push shallower.

Shelter Island continues to be the porgy factory it always is this time of year. The reefs off Cedar Point, the mussel beds around Coecles Harbor, and the drop-offs along the north side of the island are holding heavy numbers of porgies in 15 to 25 feet, and they're not picky — sandworms and clam strips on a simple high-low rig with size 6 or 8 hooks are still doing the damage. The average fish has been running a solid 9 to 11 inches with enough jumbos mixed in to keep the rail interested. This bite tends to hold right through October in the Peconic, so there's no urgency here — it's steady, and it should stay that way as the water cools further.

Fluke is the one piece of the puzzle that's genuinely thinning out, and it's worth being straight about it. The pattern across the region has been the bigger, keeper-grade fish sliding off toward deeper, cooler structure ahead of their fall exit, while the fish left behind in the bay and channels are trending smaller. That's playing out here too. The channels between Robins Island and North Sea, and the deeper cuts off Jessups Neck, are still giving up fluke, but the ratio of shorts to keepers has tipped the wrong way over the past two weeks. If you're targeting fluke, work the deeper edges of the channels on the last of the outgoing with a 3/4-ounce white bucktail tipped with Gulp, and don't be surprised if the better fish show up as a bonus rather than the target.

Weakfish are still showing in modest but consistent numbers around Sag Harbor and the mouth of Three Mile Harbor, mostly on the same bunker-adjacent structure the bass are working, and a few nice fish in the 3 to 5-pound range have come off diamond jigs worked slow along the bottom on the drift.

Bluefish are the other bright spot. Snapper blues are thick in the back bays from Noyack through Southold Bay, which is the usual signal that bigger blues aren't far behind as the water keeps cooling — that pattern has held true for the Peconic in past falls and there's no reason to think this year breaks from it.

One more thing worth flagging even though it's not a rod-and-reel story: the bay scallop opener is close enough now that early word on set strength should start circulating within the next week or two. Anyone planning trips around that opener should keep an ear out at the local shops, because early reports usually shape how the season gets fished.

Looking at the next few days, the tide is still working off Tuesday's new moon push, so the window for aggressive bass behavior at the Gut and along the ocean-side beaches should hold through the weekend before the current eases back toward neap. If the wind stays out of the north-northwest and skies stay clear, expect the bait to keep tightening and the bass to keep leaning in. If it swings onshore and stirs the bay, look for the bite to soften and shift toward the deeper holes where the water stays cleaner. Either way, this is the start of the real fall pattern in the Peconic, not the finish — the next two to three weeks should build from here as the bass continue staging through the bay on their way toward the ocean side.
