# Outgoing After Dark: Shinnecock Bass Commit, Bunker Push the Blues In
*The inlet bite belongs to the late ebb now, and the bunker schools funneling through after sundown are pulling both stripers and bigger blues with them.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — September 14** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

If you've only got one window this week, make it the back half of the outgoing tide after dark at the inlet — not the bridge, not the ocean side beaches, the inlet itself, working the deep hole off the east jetty rocks where the current stacks bait against structure. That's the call right now, and it's a change from where things sat even ten days ago. The daytime pattern at Shinnecock has gone quiet and inconsistent, but the night bite on the ebb has turned into the most reliable window on the whole South Shore complex east of Moriches.

We flagged this last week — said the striper bite would build steadily heading into the fall run proper, and that's exactly what's showing up now. Bunker schools that spent the summer holding in the bay are starting to evacuate on the tide, funneling out through the inlet on the last three hours of the ebb, and stripers are shadowing them tight instead of just picking at stragglers the way they were in August. That's a real shift in commitment, not just more fish around — it's fish that are actually hunting.

The moon is building toward more tidal range over the next several days, which matters here more than almost anywhere else on the Island because Shinnecock's inlet current gets genuinely dangerous and genuinely productive at the same time once that range opens up. Bigger swings mean harder-running water, and harder-running water means bait gets pinned against the rocks instead of drifting through loose. That's when the bass stop nosing around and start committing.

Water on the ocean side has stayed rough this week, which has done exactly what it usually does — pushed the good fishing inside. The open beaches haven't been worth much surf time; the swell has kept things churned and the fish have moved into the stable water behind the jetties and up into the bay proper. That's pushed effort onto the inlet and onto Ponquogue Bridge, and both have responded.

At the inlet, the play after dark is eels and bucktails worked slow along bottom in that deep hole between the jetties, right where the outgoing current funnels through the throat. Stripers from 26 to maybe 34 inches have been the bulk of it, with the occasional better fish mixed in on nights when the bunker are thick. A 6- to 9-inch live eel on a fish-finder rig, or a white bucktail with a strip of pork rind or Gulp trailer worked with a slow lift-and-drop right on the bottom third of the water column, has been the more consistent producer than plugs this week — the current is running hard enough that a swimming plug just gets swept through before a fish can commit to it. Blues are mixed in with the bass on the same tide, and this is where the bunker-driven size class matters: reports of bigger blues showing up at Montauk this week on thick bunker schools are part of the same push moving down the coast, and Shinnecock's inlet has been seeing better-grade blues than it did a month ago, some pushing into the 6- to 8-pound range, taking the same bucktails and occasionally busting up top when the bunker get pinned against the rocks on the last of the ebb.

Ponquogue Bridge has been the daytime and dusk consolation prize while the ocean stays sloppy. Weakfish have been showing on the pilings on the last two hours of the incoming and first hour of the outgoing, mostly in that 16- to 20-inch class, taking bucktails tipped with squid strips or 4-inch chartreuse Gulp swim shads worked slow and close to the structure in 15 to 20 feet. It's not a blitz — it's a pick, a fish here and there for anglers willing to work the pilings methodically rather than blind-casting the open water. Porgies have been mixed in on the same drift for anyone soaking clam on bottom rigs, which is a fine way to fill a cooler while waiting on the tide to turn for weakfish.

Fluke are still around but thinning out, which tracks with what's happening everywhere east of here — Block Island's still holding some of the season's best fluke fishing, and that tells you the exodus toward deeper water hasn't fully started, but Shinnecock's bay-side drifts off Tiana Beach and through the deeper holes toward the canal have gotten spottier by the week. Bucktail and Gulp combinations in white or pink, dragged slow on the last of the incoming through 12 to 18 feet, are still worth a drift, but this isn't the two-weeks-ago fluke bite. Give it another week or two before writing it off entirely, but don't plan a whole trip around it.

The albie push that's been working west along the south shore hasn't shown decisively at the inlet itself yet, though the pattern building from Rhode Island westward is the same one we've been watching, and Shinnecock sits right in its path. Worth keeping an eye on the ocean side around the jetty tips on calm mornings over the next week if the swell backs off enough to fish it.

Looking ahead: the building tidal range through this weekend should sharpen the inlet bite further, especially on the outgoing after midnight when boat traffic and daytime pressure are off the water entirely. If the wind cooperates and lays down even one night this week, that's the night to be on the east jetty working eels through the hole. Daytime plans should center on the bridge for weakfish and porgies until the ocean side settles down enough to make the beaches and jetty tips worth the walk again.
