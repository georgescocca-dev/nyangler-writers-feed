# Fourteen Hours Round-Trip to the Canyon Edge, and the Reel Never Stopped Singing
*A long run to a distant temperature break paid off in yellowfin and a lost bigeye, with tidal range building toward the new moon.*

**MONTAUK, NY — August 9** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We left the dock at 3:40 in the morning, and I told the mate we'd be back by six that night if the fish cooperated. They didn't entirely, but close enough. The grounds we ran to are a hundred-plus miles from Montauk depending on where you jump off the shelf, and at eighteen to twenty knots that's five hours out, five back, and whatever's left in the middle is your fishing day. Do that math before you leave the dock, not after. We burned through about three-quarters of our fuel just getting there and getting home, which left us maybe four hours on the grounds — enough to find them, not enough to feel greedy about it.

We found warm, clean water pushing along a wall in close to the 1500-fathom curve, a hard color change you could see from the tower before you could see it on the plotter. Ballyhoo on the surface, some small blackfin scattered under birds, and the kind of nervous water that tells you something's working bait underneath. We pulled a standard spread — Ilanders and ballyhoo down the middle, green machines and a spreader bar out back — and it didn't take long. First fish up was a yellowfin that ran the reel down to backing before we turned him boatside, better than sixty pounds, clean fat fish that had clearly been eating well. Twenty minutes later something bigger got into the pattern, took a short rigger bait and just kept going straight down instead of running off. We fought that one for the better part of forty minutes before the hook pulled. Bigeye behavior, that dive — we'll never know for sure, but that's what it felt like on the other end of the rod.

We can't speak to how other boats are doing on other pieces of structure right now — we only fished the one spot, and that's the only water we can vouch for. What we can say is that the temperature break we found was sharp and well-defined, and the bait activity on it was steady the whole time we were on the grounds. Anyone making a long run out should expect that kind of setup to hold for at least a few days if the water stays put, but we'd rather report what we saw firsthand than pass along secondhand claims about grounds we didn't fish ourselves.

If you've got the fuel budget for a run like this and the patience to work a temperature break once you find it, that's the play right now — cover the distance, find the color change, and let the spread do the rest.
