# The Cut's Holding Its Shape — And the Bunker Are Piling Up Against It
*Moriches Inlet's main channel is running clean and deep this week, and it's funneling more bait than bass right now, but that's about to change.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — September 14** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The inlet's been quiet on the surface but busy underneath. Anyone who's run the cut on a dropping tide this week noticed the same thing — the main channel is still tracking tight against the Cupsogue side, holding that deeper scour it carved out over the summer, with the shoal off the Westhampton spit pushing sediment into the flood side just enough to squeeze the current through a narrower gut than it was running in July. That constriction matters right now because it's turning the last two hours of the ebb into a bait chute. Peanut bunker and mullet coming out of the back bay — East Moriches creeks, the flats off Tuthill Point — are getting funneled through that narrowed channel instead of spreading out over the flood shoal like they do in slack summer conditions. When bait gets squeezed like that, predators find it fast. Right now it's mostly snappers and cocktail blues doing the finding, but the size class behind them is starting to change.

We flagged this last week — said the building moon and the stronger tidal push it brings would tighten up bunker school activity through the system, and that's exactly what's happening. The tidal range has been climbing daily, and every inch of added push is moving more bait through that inlet gut on each tide cycle. That's the mechanism. It's not complicated, but it's real, and it's why the next few days matter more than the last few did.

Inside the bay, the fluke bite has been fading the way it does this time of year — not gone, but thinning out as the resident fish start staging for their move toward the ocean side and deeper water. Drifts over the sandy patches off Pattersquash Island and the deeper holes north of Cupsogue are still giving up keeper fluke in the 18 to 22-inch range, but the counts are down from where they were three weeks ago. Bucktail and Gulp combos — white or chartreuse 3/4-ounce bucktails teased with a 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullet — are still the right call on the last two hours of the incoming, worked slow along the channel edges in 8 to 12 feet. Don't expect a banner day. Expect a few good fish if you put in the drift time.

The better story in the bay right now is bluefish, and it's a two-tier situation. Snapper blues are thick — genuinely thick — pushed in tight to the docks and bulkheads from East Moriches down through Mastic on peanut bunker schools that came through the inlet on the last few tides. Small green or silver Kastmasters, size 00 to 0, or just a piece of spearing on a snap swivel, and kids with ultralight gear are having a field day off any dock with current running past it. That's the fun bite, but it's also the tell — where snappers concentrate on bait, bigger blues aren't far behind.

And bigger blues are showing right at the inlet itself, exactly where the bathymetry says they should. The deep cut on the Cupsogue side, where the channel drops off fast into 15 to 20 feet, is holding blues in the 3 to 6-pound range feeding on the same bunker pushing through on the tide. This mirrors what's happening at Montauk right now, where bigger bluefish have been showing up hard on bunker schools — the same fall pattern is just arriving here on a smaller scale. Diamond jigs in the 2 to 3-ounce range, worked with a fast retrieve through the current seam where the inlet channel meets the ocean-side rip, have been the most consistent producer. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, cast into that same seam and left to sit on bottom in the deeper part of the cut, are also drawing strikes — particularly on the last of the ebb when the current is still ripping but starting to slow enough for a bait to hold position.

Stripers are the piece everyone's watching for, and they're not fully committed yet — but the staging is underway. The regional pattern of bass shadowing bait pods tight to structure and rips, from Raritan Bay on up the coast, hasn't fully landed inside Moriches yet, but there's no reason to think it stays that way with this much bait funneling through the cut. A handful of schoolie bass have been picked off the deeper edge of the inlet channel on bucktails meant for blues, which is usually the first sign before the real push arrives. Smith Point's ocean side, particularly the rip that forms off the jetty on the outgoing tide, is worth watching closely over the next week as bass start committing to the same current edges the blues are already using.

Looking ahead, the tidal range keeps building toward the fuller moon, which should keep pushing more bait through that narrowed inlet channel and keep concentrating fish along the same current seams. If the wind stays out of the northwest and holds the water clean, the window to fish that last two hours of ebb at the inlet — right where the Cupsogue channel dumps into the rip — is the best bet for connecting with the bigger blues and the first real striper commitment. If you can only get out once this week, make it that stage of the tide, right there.
