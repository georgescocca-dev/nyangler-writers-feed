# 5:52 Ebb at the Railroad Bridge: Bass Deep, Blues on Top
*The neap is softening the current, but the low-light windows on both ends are still putting fish on the deck — you just have to be there for the turn.*

**BOURNE, MA — July 20** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

5:52 AM. That's when the ebb hits full stride at the Railroad Bridge this week, and if you're not rigged and casting by 5:40, you're already behind the current. I've been watching this canal for thirty years and the clock still tells you more than any app ever will. Right now we're sliding into the July 21 first-quarter neap, which means peak velocity is backing off from the 5-knot spring tides we saw two weeks ago down into the high 3s, low 4s. That sounds like bad news, but it's actually opened the window up — instead of a tight 45-minute sweet spot at max ebb, guys are finding fish holding an hour on either side of peak current on both ends this week.

East end first. The rip line off the Bell Buoy has been the most consistent piece of water in the whole system. Bass are sitting deep in the trough where the ebb dumps out past the jetty, and they want something that gets down and stays down through the seam — a 2-ounce Kettle Herring bucktail tipped with a strip of mackerel belly, or if you're a plug guy, a slow-sinking Redfin worked with the current instead of against it. I had a report of a 34-inch fish taken right at 5:15 AM Tuesday, just as the current was building toward that 5:52 peak, and another keeper came off the rocks near Scusset Beach twenty minutes later on the same tide. That's the pattern right now — the fish are there for the buildup and the first half hour of max current, then they slide off the structure once it eases.

West end has been the bluefish show. Mashnee Island and the current seam off Hog Island are holding bait — pods of peanut bunker getting pushed and pulled with every tide change — and the blues have been all over them in the last hour of daylight. Poppers and 1-ounce metal like Kastmasters worked fast across the surface have been getting hit hard right at dusk, especially on the flood when that Buzzards Bay water pushes in past Wings Neck and stacks bait against the current seam. Nothing subtle about it — you see the bait get nervous, you see the surface break, and you've got maybe ten casts before the school moves off the seam. I had two guys texting me from Phinneys Harbor Thursday saying they lost count of blues in a 45-minute window right on the last of the flood before dark.

Bass on the west end have been more of a night bite than a daytime one, and that's exactly the pattern we flagged last week — night fishing carrying the load as the heat sets in. Daytime water temps on the Cape Cod Bay side are staying cooler than what the Sound and the South Shore beaches are dealing with right now, which is keeping our fish more willing to feed through the day than stripers south of here, but the biggest fish are still coming after dark. Guys working eels on the drop at the Bourne Bridge abutments after 9 PM have had the best luck — slow presentation, let the current do the work, and don't be afraid to fish it right through a full tide change since the neap current isn't ripping as hard as it will again in a couple weeks.

Fluke have been a bright spot for anyone willing to work the deeper water off the canal mouths. Both ends have channel edges dropping to 25-30 feet where fluke are holding on the last two hours of the ebb, and a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a bucktail, dragged slow along bottom, has been putting keeper-class fish in the cooler consistently. It's not blitz fishing, but it's steady, and on a week when the bass bite has been more about timing than volume, that steadiness is worth knowing about.

No albies or bonito yet — too early for the canal itself — but I'd keep half an eye on Buzzards Bay proper as you head toward Wings Neck and the mouth. Water's been climbing into a range where those fish start scouting, and if that pattern follows the rest of the coast, we could see the first scattered reports in the next couple weeks working up from the outer bay.

Looking ahead, the neap is going to keep softening current speeds through the weekend, which means longer bite windows but less violent current to concentrate fish tight against structure. I'd fish the buildup on both ebb and flood rather than waiting for dead-peak current — that's where the fish have been sitting this week, not right at the max. If you can only get one session in, make it the evening flood at Mashnee for blues, or the pre-dawn ebb at the east end rip for bass. Both have been the most dependable windows on the calendar, and with the moon easing off, the fish seem to be giving you a little more time to work them than they will again once we build back toward full.
