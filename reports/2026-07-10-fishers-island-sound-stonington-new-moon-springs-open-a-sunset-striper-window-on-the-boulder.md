# New-moon springs open a sunset striper window on the boulder fields
*Fluke have abandoned the warm bay for the cuts and channels, and the cold wall that's kept bonito west of Block Island just fell apart.*

**STONINGTON, CT — July 10** — _by Manny Pereira, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound did what it does in early July — it got warm fast, and it got lazy on the moon. We were coming off the full moon push from late June, tides flattening out into the neap cycle, and the bass that had been working the boulder fields off Ram Island and Wamphassuc Point at first light went quiet by mid-week. Water in the back coves and up into Little Narragansett Bay pushed hard into the mid-70s. Bait didn't disappear, but it scattered, and so did the fish that eat it.

That's changing. We're building into new-moon springs now, with the bigger tide swings stacking up through the weekend and peak alignment landing Saturday into Monday. The piece I'm watching hardest is the PM flood meeting sunset — that's the first evening window we've had since the June full moon where high water and last light line up instead of fighting each other. When the flood floods right at dusk, bait gets pinned against structure with the light dropping, and the bass that have been sulking in deeper water all day come up onto the rock to feed instead of just cruising through. That's the window I'd build a whole evening around this week, not just a stop on the way home.

Fluke have made their move, and it's not subtle. The bay water got too warm to hold them — we're talking upper 70s in the shallow flats off Mystic and up in the coves — and the fish have pushed hard into the channels and the mouth structure where clean, cooler water is getting pulled through on the tide. Mystic River channel on the outgoing, right where it dumps past the highway bridge into the Sound, has been holding fish in 15 to 20 feet. Same story at the cuts through the Fishers Island Sound reefs — anywhere there's a defined channel edge with current moving, that's where the fluke are stacking instead of spread out on the flats like they were in June. Chartreuse and white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing, have been the ticket. A few keepers to 22 inches, nothing to write home about size-wise, but a steady pick if you're willing to hunt the deeper edges instead of anchoring on memory from last month.

Stripers are still a rock-and-tide game right now, and that hasn't changed even with the warm water. The boulder fields in Fishers Island Sound — the stretch running from off Ram Island down toward the west end, and the rock piles tucked in behind the breakwater at Stonington — are still holding fish, but they've gone deeper and more nocturnal with the heat. Early morning, an hour before and after low, working topwater and darters over the exposed boulders has produced schoolies and a few fish in the high 20s, mostly on an incoming push. But the better window, and the one I'm circling for this week specifically, is that evening flood-sunset overlap I mentioned. Live eels and bunker chunks fished tight to structure off Esker Point and the outer breakwater rocks, right as the tide turns and the light goes gold, have been worth the mosquito bites. If the wind stays out of the south and lays down toward evening — which it's been doing — that's your night to be there.

Here's the piece that's got my attention more than anything else this week: the cold wall that's historically kept bonito and albies penned up around Block Island has broken down. Water temps in Rhode Island Sound jumped over ten degrees in a short stretch, which means that barrier between the warmer water tight to Block and the cooler water spreading west toward Newport and Point Judith just collapsed. That corridor is widening, and it's pushing toward us. We're not seeing bonito inside Fishers Island Sound yet — I haven't had a confirmed report tight to Watch Hill or the Reef — but the setup right next door has changed fast, and fast-moving warm water plumes have a way of showing up where you don't expect them. I'd be running light spinning gear with 000 Deadly Dicks and small epoxy jigs in the boat just in case, especially working the rips off Watch Hill Light and out toward the Fishers Island side on a clean tide. It's early. It might be another two or three weeks before we get a real bonito show inside our water, and the false albacore blitz everybody's really waiting for is still a September story, not a July one. But the ingredients are moving in the right direction faster than they have in past seasons, and that's worth paying attention to now rather than after the fact.

Black sea bass have been a consistent, unglamorous producer on the deeper reef structure out toward Fishers Island — 25 to 35 feet, squid strips and Gulp on a two-hook bottom rig, keepers mixed with plenty of shorts. Good action if you want dinner without overthinking it.

For the week ahead: if I've got one evening to give it everything, it's Saturday or Sunday's PM flood at sunset on the Ram Island boulder field or the outer breakwater rocks — the tide's building, the light's right, and that's the first real convergence we've had since June. Daytime, I'm running the Mystic River channel and the FIS Sound cuts on the outgoing for fluke rather than wasting time on the warm flats. And I'm keeping an eye east — if that Rhode Island warm water keeps sliding our way, Watch Hill could be the first place in our zone to see a bonito this summer, and I don't want to be the last guy to hear about it.
