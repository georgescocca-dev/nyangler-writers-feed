# Mackerel Pull the String: Stripers Follow Into the River Mouths on the New Moon Tide
*Forty years watching this pattern and it never gets old — the bass show up right behind the mackerel, same as always, right on the new moon flood.*

**BOOTHBAY HARBOR, ME — August 14** — _by Rusty Pelletier, Zone Writer_

My uncle Eldon used to stand on the co-op dock with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and tell anybody who'd listen that the mackerel come first, and the bass follow like they're tied to them with a string. I've watched that string get pulled every August of my life, and this week it happened right on cue at the mouth of the St. George River, where the tide runs hard past the ledges on the last two hours of the ebb.

We flagged this a week back — said the building tidal flow into the August 12 new moon would sharpen the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what showed up. The mackerel schools that had been scattered and thin through late July packed in tight against the breakwater at Boothbay Harbor and out around Ram Island, and the stripers moved in behind them like they'd gotten a phone call. Guys jigging small Swedish Pimples and diamond jigs in 15 to 20 feet off Ram Island are filling five-gallon buckets with mackerel inside twenty minutes some mornings, and that bait's not going to waste — the same water is holding bass in the 26 to 34-inch range working the current seams where the river spills into the bay.

St. George, Medomak, and Damariscotta river mouths have all been showing the same story on the last of the ebb into the new flood — bass stacked in eight to fifteen feet, hitting live mackerel drifted on a fish-finder rig or swimming a chunk under a slip float back into the seam. If you'd rather cast, a Rebel Jumpin' Minnow or a Doc's swimming mackerel plug worked across the moving water at first light has been drawing strikes, though the window's short — twenty, thirty minutes either side of the tide change and then it shuts down until the next stage. That's the honest truth of it this week. It's not a blitz. It's a grind you have to time right, and the guys putting fish in the boat are the ones who show up on the tide, not just whenever it's convenient.

Out around Monhegan the pattern's been steadier. The ledges on the southwest side are holding pollock working the surface on the early morning flood — schoolies mostly, but enough of them chasing bait balls that you can see the slicks from a half mile off. Under them, cod and a scattering of haddock are sitting in 100 to 140 feet, and jigging a diamond jig or bouncing a herring chunk on a two-hook bottom rig has been worth the trip out. It's not the haddock fishing of thirty years ago — nobody's pretending otherwise — but there's enough holding in that deeper water to make a respectable box if you work the ledges on the last two hours of the incoming.

Penobscot Bay itself has been quiet but honest. The deeper channels — Muscle Ridge, Two Bush — are still holding a mix of pollock and the occasional cod for guys running bottom rigs on the drift, and more than a few boats are coming back with a mess of lobster bycatch tangled in with the groundfish, which nobody's complaining about. Water's sitting in the low 60s across the bay, cold enough that we're not seeing any of the warm-water excitement working its way up the coast from southern New England. No albies showing here, no bluefish blitzes — this is Maine in August the way it's supposed to be, steady and a little slow to give it up.

Down around Eastern Egg Rock the puffins are still working but thinning out as the season turns, and the bass have been using the same bait-driven pattern — terns diving on mackerel schools pushed up against the ledges on the ebb, stripers underneath cleaning up what gets knocked loose. If you can find working birds out there in the last two hours of the tide, it's worth a few casts with a topwater popper before you move on.

Over toward Deer Isle and Stonington, the Eggemoggin Reach current has been the most consistent thing going — bass working the tide rips where the reach narrows, and pollock schooling on the surface at first light the same as at Monhegan. Isle au Haut's ledges are holding a few cod for the guys willing to run out that far, though it's a long haul for what's mostly a slow pick right now.

Looking ahead, I don't expect much to change through the week. The new moon tides are still building strength, and if that current keeps sharpening up at the river mouths and channel edges the way it has, the bass should keep following the mackerel right on schedule. Water temps aren't going anywhere fast in this zone — we're insulated from whatever's stirring up the coast to the south, and that's fine by me. If I had one morning to fish, I'd be at the St. George river mouth on the last of the ebb with a bucket of fresh mackerel and enough patience to wait for the tide to do its work.
