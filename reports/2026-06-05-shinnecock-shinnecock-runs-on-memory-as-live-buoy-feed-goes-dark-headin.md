# Shinnecock runs on memory as live buoy feed goes dark heading into the new moon
*With current SST and tide telemetry unavailable, early-June climatology and a building outgoing put the inlet night bite back in charge.*

**HAMPTON BAYS, NY — June 5, 2026** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

The analyst desk came up short this morning. There are no live buoy temperatures, no current tide station readings, and no fresh SST imagery past the June 3 package in the catalog — the pipeline gap is real and worth stating up front rather than dressing up. What we have to work with is the seasonal envelope: early June on the South Shore typically puts Shinnecock Bay surface temps in the mid-60s, with ocean water outside the inlet running three to six degrees cooler depending on wind history. That inside-outside delta is the single most important variable on this beat right now, and absent a hard number from the buoy, the working assumption is that the bay has continued its springtime warm-up while the ocean side is still lagging. New moon arrives June 14, so we're nine days out from peak tidal range, but the spring build is already underway.

What that means, mechanically, is that the inlet is doing exactly what it does every June — acting as a thermal and biological pump. Warmer bay water flushing out on the drop pulls bait with it, and the colder, denser ocean water pushing in on the flood stacks bass on the structure inside the jetty tips and along the rock piles on the west side. With the moon building toward new, outgoing tides through the back half of the night are going to run hard and long. That is striped bass water, and it is the dominant story on this beat until something changes. Slot fish should be the bread and butter on bucktails and soft plastics worked deep in the rip; the bigger class of fish — the overslot resident bass that hang on the inlet through June — will eat a live eel drifted through the same seams after midnight.

The Ponquogue Bridge has been the secondary node, and this is the window when weakfish typically reestablish on the south-side pilings on the last of the incoming and first of the dropping. Water temperature drives that bite as much as anything, and if the bay is sitting where it should be for the calendar, the tiderunners are in the system. Small soft plastics on light jigheads, fished tight to the pilings and let to swing in the current, is the technique that matches what the water is doing. Bluefish have been knocking around the bay in pulses — they will show up on the flats off Tiana on a warm afternoon and disappear by sunset, which is consistent with fish following bait pods rather than holding structure.

Fluke are the question mark. The drift in the channels between the inlet and the Ponquogue Bridge — eight to eighteen feet of water over sand and broken shell — should be producing keepers by now, but the class of fish this early tends to skew short, and you have to work through them. The deeper holes on the east side of the channel, twenty to twenty-five feet, are where the better fish stage before they push up onto the flats later in the month. Bucktail-and-teaser with a strip of squid or a Gulp trailer remains the rig that sorts size from numbers. The Shinnecock Canal, assuming the locks are running on their normal cycle, is the back door to the Peconic and will see its own porgy push any day if it hasn't already.

Looking three to five days out, the controlling factor is the moon. Each night between now and June 14 the outgoing gets a little stronger and a little later, and the back-tide window — that hour on either side of slack low — becomes the high-percentage shot inside the inlet. I'd be watching for any south or southwest wind event that pushes ocean water up against the beach and compresses the thermal gradient at the inlet mouth; that is the setup that triggers a real bass blitz on the outside, with fish hitting the wash at Tiana on the dropping tide. If the SST pipeline comes back online and shows a sharp front setting up off the inlet, that changes the conversation for the bigger fish.

Until the data comes back, the move is conservative and pattern-based: fish the outgoing at the inlet through the dark hours, work the bridge on the tide turn, and treat the bay drift as a daylight operation. The fish are where the calendar says they should be. The instruments are just catching up.
