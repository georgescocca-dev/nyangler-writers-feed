# Slow Read, Steadier Close: Yellowfin Hanging on a Thinning Warm Filament
*The south canyons graded out mixed this week — good enough water to keep the fleet moving, not enough concentration to call it a hot bite.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — September 21** — _by Capt. Ray Lugo, Zone Writer_

Call this week a C-plus. The water's still warm enough to hold fish, the bait's around, but nothing out there is stacked tight enough to make the run a lock. That's the honest read heading into the last full week of September, and it's why the routing decision — Spencer, Lindenkohl, Poor Man's, or Norfolk — mattered more than usual this week. Pick wrong and you burned diesel on empty water. Pick right and you had a mixed-bag day worth the trip.

Here's the setup. The southern end of that Hudson-Wilmington warm water signature is still reaching down into this stretch, keeping surface temps in the low-to-mid 70s out past the shelf break. That's enough to hold yellowfin in the general area, but it's a diluted version of what's sitting up north around the core Wilmington grounds — spread thinner, less punchy, more scattered. Translation: the fish are out there, but they're not balled up on one temp break the way they were earlier in the month. You're working for it, not walking into it.

The Harvest Moon swung through this week too, and that big a tide always changes the rhythm on the troll. Bigger current means the break lines move around more than usual, so a spot that was holding bait and birds on Tuesday can be dead water by Thursday. The fleet that did best this week wasn't married to one canyon — they checked water color and temp on the way out and weren't afraid to keep running if the first stop looked wrong. That's the whole game down here in a soft-bite week: don't marry a number, marry the conditions.

On the troll, yellowfin have come mixed with a few bigeye showing on the drop-offs where the bottom falls away hard — the areas where bait gets pinned against structure on the edge. Spreadsheet spreads with Ilanders and cedar plugs behind planers have out-produced straight ballyhoo this week, mostly because the fish are picking at bait balls rather than committing hard, and the subsurface presentation gets more looks. If you're marking scattered fish on the meter but nobody's coming tight to the surface, drop a bar or two deeper before you pull the spread — that's where the bigeye have been sitting.

Mahi have been the bright spot for anybody willing to stop and work floating structure. Any bit of grass, a lobster buoy, a stray pallet — throw a chunk of butterfish or a small spreader bar rigged with a chartreuse feather and let it sit. Schoolie-sized mahi have been holding under almost anything floating this week, and that's been the most reliable few fish of the day for boats that bothered to stop instead of running through it. Wahoo have been quieter — a few taken high-speed trolling black-and-purple Ilanders around 12-14 knots on the drop-offs, but nobody's calling it a pattern yet. White marlin have shown in ones and twos around the temperature edges where the color changes from that green-blue inshore water to the cleaner blue further out — dredge and dink baits still the right call if you're seeing them free-jump or tailing.

The thing worth watching is whether that warm filament holds its shape or starts breaking apart as we move deeper into fall. Right now it's stable enough to keep the extended range going, but it's not gaining strength — it's holding steady at best, and steady in late September usually means it's on the way down, not up. If it starts to contract toward the true canyon edges over the next several days, expect the bite to tighten up and concentrate rather than disappear — that's usually how it goes when the warm water starts retreating south. Tighter concentration can actually mean a better bite for the boats that time it right, just a smaller window to hit it in.

For the next few days, conditions look workable but not dramatic — no major blow on the horizon, tide swinging back down off the Harvest Moon peak into more normal current, which should settle the temp breaks back into more predictable lines by midweek. If you've only got one day to spend, I'd lean toward whichever canyon showed the cleanest water color on the last run out — right now that's been trading around week to week rather than settling into one favorite. Check the color and the birds before you commit, work the drop-offs subsurface if the surface bite is quiet, and don't be afraid to burn fuel checking a second spot if the first one looks dead. That's been the difference between a decent day and a skunk all week.

Bottom line: this isn't the kind of week to promise anybody a slam. It's a week where the fish are out there, the water's warm enough to justify the run, and the boats willing to move around and read the conditions in real time are the ones bringing fish back to the dock. If the filament holds through the weekend, there's still meat on this bone. If it starts sliding south, the smart money shifts toward tighter, more concentrated fishing on whichever edge holds the sharpest break — worth checking in with the dock before you commit to a canyon.
