# Jeffrey's in 195 feet: haddock stacked on the mud, cod picking at the gravel edge
*Mackerel are thick over the humps and the first bluefin marks of the year showed up on Tillies this week.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — July 13** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

One-ninety-five feet of water, soft grey mud, five knots of drift over the bottom — that's where I put the boat Tuesday morning on the north end of Jeffrey's, and that's where the haddock have been living for about two weeks now. Mud holds haddock this time of year, plain and simple. They stack up over that flat, featureless bottom in loose schools that move with the tide, and once you find the edge where the mud breaks into that slightly harder transitional bottom, you can anchor up or drift through it three, four times and keep connecting.

The gravel is a different story right now. I fished the classic cod ground on the southwest corner in about 140 to 160 feet — the rocky humps where the bottom goes from mud to broken gravel and shell hash — and the cod are there, but they're not stacked the way the haddock are. A few fish in the 24 to 30 inch class came over the rail, mixed with a lot of shorts we had to shake off quick. With the recreational cod season as tight as it's been in the Gulf of Maine the last several years, most of what we're doing on the gravel now is photo-and-release work, maybe keeping one if the regs and the size line up for the day. I tell my anglers up front: come out for haddock, treat any cod as a bonus, because that's the honest state of that fishery right now.

Haddock is where the real numbers are. We've been running hi-lo rigs baited with fresh clam bellies, two hooks, six to eight ounces of lead depending on the drift, right down into that mud in the 180 to 220 foot range between Jeffrey's and the approach to Platts Bank. On the better drifts — and you need drift, dead slack kills this bite — we've had guys with their two-fish limit of quality haddock, 18 to 22 inches, inside of ninety minutes. Diamond jigs tipped with a clam strip work too if you want to cover water faster, but the bait rig has been more consistent when the current is light, which it has been around the last few tide changes.

Pollock have been mixed in over the same mud, especially up off bottom ten to twenty feet, suspended over the haddock schools. A fast retrieve on a diamond jig or a Norwegian jig will pick them off — some nice fish in the eight to fifteen pound range, good eating and good sport, and they don't count against the same limits as the groundfish so they're a welcome addition to the box.

Bait's been the story on the surface. Mackerel are thick over the humps on Jeffrey's and out toward Tillies — Sabiki rigs loaded in a couple of drops, more than enough for chum and live bait both. That mackerel show is exactly why I've started seeing bluefin sign earlier than I'd expect for mid-July. We had a pod of school-to-medium fish, forty to eighty pounds by the look of the boils, working bait on the surface out toward the Tillies side Thursday afternoon. Nothing stacked, nothing you'd call a giant show yet — that's an October pattern on this ledge, when the bigger fish push in tight to feed ahead of their fall run south. But seeing tuna at all this early, with the amount of mackerel and pogy bait around, tells me the biomass is building faster than usual this season. I'd keep half an eye up while you're jigging haddock — a rigged spinning outfit with a stickbait or a topwater popper in the rocket launcher isn't a bad idea right now if you've got the crew to work it.

Water's been clean out there, no dirty water pushing off the coast, and the thermal layer feels normal for mid-July — comfortable on top, that hard drop-off into cold water once you get down near bottom on the mud, which is exactly what holds the haddock tight to that layer. Wind's been the bigger variable than temperature. We've had a couple of days where a stiff southwest breeze made the drift too fast to fish the mud properly, and on those days we shifted over to the gravel edges where the structure breaks the current a bit.

Looking ahead, I'd keep targeting the mud in that 180 to 220 foot band for haddock — that pattern has been the most dependable thing out there for three weeks running, and there's no sign it's slowing. If the wind lays down over the weekend, I want to run out past Tillies and check for tuna sign again; the mackerel schools aren't going anywhere and that's usually the tell. Cod fishing I'd treat as a bonus, not a plan — fish the gravel if you want variety in the box, but build your trip around the haddock and you won't go home disappointed.
