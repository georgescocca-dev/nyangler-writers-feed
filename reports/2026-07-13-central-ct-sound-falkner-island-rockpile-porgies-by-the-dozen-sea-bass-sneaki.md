# Falkner Island Rockpile Porgies by the Dozen, Sea Bass Sneaking in Underneath
*Bottom fishing carries the week on the rockpiles while stripers play hide-and-seek on the Charles Island bar.*

**NEW HAVEN, CT — July 13** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Wednesday, half-tide push on the New Haven outer breakwall, and my rod tip is doing the little porgy shimmy I live for — tap, tap, tap, then that steady bend that tells you it's not a false alarm. Hi-lo rig, two size 6 Chestertown hooks, green crab cut in dime-sized pieces, 20 feet of water tight to the rock. Ten minutes, four porgies, biggest one pushing 13 inches. That's the kind of start that makes you forget you almost didn't get out of bed for a 6 a.m. tide.

This is peak porgy season on my beat and the fish are cooperating, even if the water's been a little confused about what season it wants to be. Kings Point over on the west end of the Sound is reading 71 and change, New London out east is still stuck in the mid-60s, and central Connecticut — New Haven, Milford, the Thimbles — is sitting right in that gradient, probably high 60s pushing low 70s depending on the day and the tide stage. That kind of split means the bait's been moving around more than usual, and the porgies have been chasing it, but the breakwalls and rockpiles have held fish consistently enough that I haven't had a skunk day yet this month.

Falkner Island is the standout right now. I've been anchoring up on the northwest corner in 25 to 30 feet, current running, and dropping green crab and sandworm combos on double hi-lo rigs. The porgies are thick — not wall-to-wall like late August gets, but steady enough that a two-hour sit puts eight to twelve keepers in the cooler if you're paying attention to bites and not just soaking bait. Mixed into that action, sea bass have started showing with more consistency than they did three weeks ago. Squid strips on the bottom hook, or a small Gulp Shrimp on a light jighead bounced right off the rock, and you'll pick off sea bass in the 13 to 15 inch range, with the occasional 17-incher that makes you sit up straight. Charles Island's reef structure off the south end is doing the same thing — sea bass tucked into the rock seams, porgies working the sand pockets just off it.

Blackfish are off the table right now — Connecticut's summer closure kicked in June 1st and we don't get back into tog until September, so if you're marking fish on the rockpiles that look like blackfish, let them be and focus the crab and worm baits on porgies instead. No sense getting a citation for a fish you can't keep.

Stripers are the tougher conversation this week. The Charles Island sandbar has been giving up fish, but it's not the kind of bite where you back up the truck. I've worked the flood tide there three mornings running, casting bunker chunks on fish-finder rigs and swimming SP Minnows along the drop where the bar falls into deeper water, and I'm getting one or two fish a trip — mostly schoolies in the 22 to 26 inch range, with one better fish around 30 inches that ate a chunk sitting still on bottom just as the tide really started moving. The bigger bass seem to be staying deeper and further out, probably following that thermal edge rather than pushing up onto the flat in daylight. If you want a real shot at a bigger fish off the bar, first light on the last hour of the incoming is your window — after that the light gets hard and the bite shuts down fast.

Bluefish have been the wildcard, showing up in blitzes that come and go without much warning. Stratford Shoal has had a couple of good mornings where the birds gave it away — diving gulls, nervous water, and then blues busting bait on top for twenty minutes before it goes quiet again. Poppers and 1-ounce Kastmasters on wire leader will connect when it's happening, but you have to be ready to run and gun rather than sit and wait. I lost three lures to bluefish teeth this week and don't regret a single one.

Fluke have been a slower story around Milford Gulf. The drift there in 20 to 35 feet with 3/4-ounce bucktails tipped with Gulp Swimming Mullet has produced fish, but they're mostly short — throwbacks in the 15 to 17 inch range with a keeper mixed in here and there. Not a wasted trip, but not the kind of steady keeper action you want to plan a whole day around right now. If the water keeps warming through the week that could change fast, since fluke tend to spread out and feed harder once the thermal picture stabilizes.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full later this week, which should push tide swings a little harder and get more current moving over the rockpiles — good news for porgy and sea bass fishing, since those fish feed hardest when water's actually moving past the structure. I'd focus effort on Falkner Island and the Charles Island reef on the bigger tides, working the first two hours of the flood or the last two of the ebb. For stripers, I'd stick with early mornings on the sandbar and not expect volume — treat it as a quality-over-quantity game right now. If the wind stays out of the south and lays down like it has been, Falkner and the outer New Haven breakwall should keep producing steady porgy and sea bass action into next week. That's where I'll be.
