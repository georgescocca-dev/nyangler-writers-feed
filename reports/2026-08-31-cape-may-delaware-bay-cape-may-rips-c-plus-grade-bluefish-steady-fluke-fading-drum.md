# Cape May Rips: C-Plus Grade — Bluefish Steady, Fluke Fading, Drum Long Gone
*The rips are picky on the turn but honest on the ebb, while Delaware Bay panfish carry the back-bay rods and the first tuna-of-the-south-shore chatter starts turning heads toward Cape May Point.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — August 31** — _by Capt. Kate Brennan, Zone Writer_

Grade for the week: C-plus. That's the honest read on the Cape May Rips right now — not a bad bite, not a bite you plan a whole weekend around either. The water's warm, the current's still doing its job on the bigger tides, and there's enough bait moving through to keep things interesting, but the striper action out there is a holding pattern, not a blitz. Fishable, pickable, occasionally rewarding if you're on the right piece of the tide. That's where we sit heading into Labor Day week.

Here's the why. Delaware Bay is running warm — mid-70s warm — which is exactly the kind of water that shuts fluke down and turns on the panfish crowd instead. That's what's happened. The mouth of the bay and the reef sites are still giving up fluke, but it's softened the way it always does this time of year, bait scattering out in the heat instead of holding tight to structure. We flagged that shift a few days back and called it a normal seasonal transition, not a collapse, and that's holding up — there's still a fluke in the box for anglers working gulp and bucktail combinations slow along the channel edges, particularly the drop-offs near Brandywine Shoal on the last two hours of the ebb. Just don't expect the June numbers.

The rips themselves are current-dependent right now, and that's the piece anglers keep getting wrong. On slack water there's nothing doing out there — the bait's not concentrated, the bass aren't stacked, and you're just soaking bait in dead water. But get out to Cape May Point on a building tide, especially the last of the incoming pushing into the first hour of the outgoing, and the rip lines start setting up with real current seams. That's where the keeper bass have been coming from — mixed in with plenty of shorts, working bucktails and swim shads in 20 to 35 feet where the moving water stacks bait against the bottom structure. We also called the mackerel bite tide-dependent, best on the stronger moving water through the rips, and that's exactly what's played out — good pods of mackerel showing on the harder tides, dead on the slack.

Same story with croaker and kingfish in the back bay — we said that bite would hold steady with the high pressure sitting on us, and it has. Fortescue and the marsh creeks off the bay are producing weakfish spikes, croaker, and spot on simple high-low bottom rigs baited with bloodworm or Fishbites, worked on the bottom of the marsh drains on the outgoing. It's not glamorous fishing, but it's steady, it's a fish a drift most days, and it's keeping rods bent for anglers who don't want to fight the bigger water.

On the black drum — nothing to report, because there's nothing left to report. That run's done. May was the window, the fish pushed out of the bay weeks ago, and if anyone's telling you there's a late-season drum bite happening in Delaware Bay right now, they're selling you something. Save that trip for next spring and don't waste tide chasing a fish that's already gone.

Bluefish are the one predator out there behaving the way they're supposed to. Bunker schools are still working the mouth of Delaware Bay and along the Wildwood beachfront, and wherever that bait balls up, the blues are on it — hard, fast, aggressive, exactly the pattern we expected as bunker density built through late summer. Metal — Kastmasters and Hopkins spoons — worked through the surface wash off Hereford Inlet has been the go-to, and anglers throwing bait into visible slicks off the jetties at Cape May Point are getting steady action too. That should keep running through Labor Day week as long as the bunker schools stay put.

The striper situation is a wait-and-see. Fish are around — some being picked off the rips on the better tides, a few showing along the Wildwood beachfront on bait — but the real fall push hasn't started. The trigger is bait density, and it needs another week or two of building tidal flow behind the growing moon to concentrate those bunker schools tight enough to get stripers committing in numbers. Worth keeping an eye on, not worth betting the farm on just yet.

What's actually got the dock talking is the warm-water push working down the Jersey coast — bonito and false albacore have started showing along the central Shore beaches, and with the water sitting warm here at Cape May, this zone is a logical next stop if that push keeps sliding south. Nothing confirmed at the rips yet, but if you're the type who likes to be there first, small metal and a pair of eyes scanning for diving birds off Cape May Point over the next week wouldn't be wasted time.

Look-ahead: if you've only got one day, fish the rips on the stronger half of the tide — either side of the moving water, not the slack — with bucktails and swim shads for the bass and mackerel mix. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod, run to Fortescue and work the bottom rigs on the outgoing for weakfish, croaker, and spot. Watch the bunker schools off Hereford and Wildwood for the blues, and keep half an eye on the horizon for that first albie or bonito showing up where it hasn't been all summer.
