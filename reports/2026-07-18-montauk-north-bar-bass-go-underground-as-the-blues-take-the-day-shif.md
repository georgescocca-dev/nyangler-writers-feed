# North Bar Bass Go Underground as the Blues Take the Day Shift
*Warm water at the Point has stripers feeding after dark while jumbo bluefish own the daylight rips — and the canyon fleet is finally cashing in south of Montauk.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 18** — _by Rich Martin, Zone Writer_

Mid-July is a hinge point out here, and if you've fished the Lighthouse long enough, you learn to read it without needing a thermometer. The spring run bass — those chrome fish that came through on the herring and the worm hatch back in May — have settled into their summer pattern, and at the Point that means one thing above all: they've gone to the dark side. Surface water off North Bar and Shagwong Reef is sitting in the low 70s right now, matching what the open ocean buoys are showing out toward the Bight, and once that happens, stripers stop wanting to work hard in the sun. They don't leave the grounds. They just go underground until the light drops.

I've fished this rock pile since before I could see over the console, and this is the oldest lesson the Point teaches: when the day bite goes quiet, the night bite doesn't. I've been sliding out to the boulder field off the Lighthouse an hour before dark and working the incoming through the last two hours of daylight into full night, and that's where the better fish are coming from — 20 to 34 pounds, a few pushing into the 40s, on live eels drifted naturally through the white water where the rip stacks up against the rocks. Guys throwing bucktails are doing better working the wash at first light, before the sun gets on the water, than they are at midday. If you want a Point bass right now, you're either fishing the two hours around sunset and sunrise, or you're fishing under the stars. That's not a slump — that's just July.

What's filling the daylight hours is bluefish, and they're not shy about it. Skinner and the crew working the eastern beaches have been finding slot-and-overslot-heavy stripers mixed with genuinely big blues — 10, 12, 14 pounds — stacked tight on current edges and boiling visibly off Shagwong and out past Turtle Cove. These aren't scattered snappers. These are teeth-first cows that will straighten a hook if you're not paying attention. The pattern Skinner's using is the right one for these current seams: cast a 1.5-ounce bucktail across the far side of the rip, let it sink clean off the edge before it starts dragging, then work it back with a slow, rhythmic lift-and-drop along bottom. In the visible boils, don't bomb long casts into open water — feather it right into the seam where the bait's getting pinned. That's where the take happens.

Fluke fishing has been the dependable half of the week, and it should stay that way regardless of what the bass and blues are doing. The drifts off Frisbie's Bank and along the edges toward Southside have been giving up a mixed bag with enough keepers mixed in to make it worth the gas — white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, worked slow along bottom in 25 to 40 feet, outgoing water. Nothing spectacular, but steady, which after a week of chasing nocturnal bass is exactly the kind of fishing you want to fall back on with a full boat.

Now for the part of the report I've been waiting to write. We flagged last week that the Canyon Challenge wrapping up this weekend would bring fresh yellowfin and white marlin reports out of the Montauk canyon fleet — and that's exactly what's happened. Boats working the edges east of the tuna grounds are connecting on yellowfin in the 40 to 70-pound class, with a scattering of bigeye mixed in on the troll. We also called that the bait piles building south of Block Island would keep bluefin accessible to the smaller boats, and that's held up too — schoolie bluefin have been showing within range of center consoles running south out of the harbor, not just the big canyon runners. If you've got a half-decent weather window this week, that's where I'd put my effort if tuna's the target.

There's another piece of this building quietly that I want everybody watching. Squid concentrations are stacking up hard around Rhode Island and into Nantucket Sound as that inshore water pushes into the high 50s and low 60s in the protected coves. That's the bait that historically supercharges the Montauk rips once it works its way down here — it's the fuel behind some of the best fall-run bass fishing we get, and it's also usually the first sign that bonito and false albacore scouts aren't far behind. Nothing definitive yet on albies at the Point, but if there's anywhere on the whole coast where the first scout shows, it's here or the Vineyard corridor. I'll be watching Shagwong and the rips off the Elbow closely over the next two weeks.

Looking ahead: the moon's building toward first quarter on the 21st, which means tidal current will ease through the week — good news for the rip fishing, because slower current lets a slow-worked bucktail or a drifted eel stay in the strike zone longer instead of getting ripped through in seconds. If the wind stays out of the south and light like it has been, I'd fish the last two hours of daylight and the first hour of dark off the Lighthouse for bass, switch to daylight bucktailing on the current seams for blues, and keep the fluke rods rigged as your insurance policy. Tuna guys — get your window in before the pattern shifts.
