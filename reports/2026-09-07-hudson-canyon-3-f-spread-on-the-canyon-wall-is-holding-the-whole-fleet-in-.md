# 3°F Spread on the Canyon Wall Is Holding the Whole Fleet in Place
*A slow-spinning eddy east of the shelf valley has pinned bait and bigeye right on the color change — but the window only lasts as long as that filament stays put.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — September 7** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Three degrees. That's the spread between the 76°F water sitting over the canyon wall and the 79°F filament riding on top of it, and that number has been running the fleet's whole week. It's not a huge break as far as canyon standards go, but it's sitting in exactly the right place — right over the 100-fathom line, pushed up tight against the edge of the Claw and bleeding out toward the Dip. When a break that size sits still for four or five days instead of sliding through in a day, that's when it starts stacking bait instead of just passing it through, and that's what's been happening out there.

The mechanism is a slow anticyclonic eddy spinning east of the shelf valley. It's not a fast-moving feature — anticyclonic eddies like this one tend to hold their shape and their position for a stretch, which is exactly why boats have been running back to the same numbers three and four days in a row instead of hunting new water every trip. The chlorophyll edge — the green-to-blue color change — is sitting right on top of that thermal break, which means the bait isn't just near the temp line, it's pinned against it. That's the setup that turns a decent troll bite into a repeatable one.

We flagged this pattern last week and said it would hold as long as the thermal setup stayed in place. It has, and the bite has followed it — bigeye, yellowfin, and mahi all producing on the troll, with white marlin showing up on the warm edge of that filament as a bonus rather than a target. That's the good news. The honest news is that it's not an all-day, every-drop bite. The tuna have been eating hard at first light and again in the last hour of daylight, and going quiet under a high, bright sun — several boats reported a dead middle of the day this week before things picked back up toward evening. If you're planning a trip, build it around dawn and dusk, not noon.

The spread that's been working is straightforward canyon math: naked ballyhoo behind small Sea Witches on the short riggers, a Green Machine/Islander combo on the long riggers, and a spreader bar with mixed ballyhoo down the center of the pattern. Troll speed has been sitting around 6 to 6.5 knots, worked right along the seam where the 76 meets the 79 rather than blasting straight through it. Boats running the color change slow and working the edge — rather than trolling straight lines across it — have had the better mornings. Yellowfin have been the most consistent player in that spread, with bigeye mixed in on the deeper baits and coming better at dawn and in low light. Mahi have been showing as incidental fish around anything holding shade — lobster gear, floating debris, weed lines — worth a cast with a small spoon or bucktail if you see color flash near the surface.

White marlin have been working the warm edge of the filament specifically, not the cold side, which lines up with where the bait's been getting pinned. A dredge or small daisy chain of green-and-pink squid dragged short and slow off the corner of the spread has been drawing raises even on days when the tuna bite was slower than hoped. Nobody's filling a marlin box out there — it's one or two shots a trip for boats working that edge correctly — but the shots have been real, not phantom.

Swordfish continue to be the after-dark and daytime-deep play on the Dip. Daytime deep-drops sitting baits in the 1,300 to 1,600-foot range, with squid strip baits, glow strobes, and enough lead to hold bottom against the current, have kept a handful of boats in the fish. That's not a numbers game — it's a patience game, matching drift speed to the current and letting the rig sit where the swords are laying up out of the light. The boats doing it well are treating it as a full trip, not a bonus stop on the way home.

Looking at the next few days, the call is simple: as long as that warm filament and the eddy holding it stay put, the tuna and marlin bite on the canyon edge should hold roughly where it's been. The first sign of trouble would be the filament starting to break down or drift — that's usually the tell that the fish position is about to shift, and it's worth watching before committing to a long run. The moon is building toward new phase on September 10, and that tide swing is likely to be the pivot point for the week — expect more current push over the edge, which could either tighten the bait further against the break or start moving it. Either way, the smart plan for the next 72 hours is to keep working the seam while it's producing rather than searching for a new number, and to build the trip around the dawn and dusk windows rather than the middle of the day. If the color change holds its shape into the weekend, this is shaping up to be one of the more workable stretches the canyon's had all month — provided the fleet keeps its expectations tied to those two feeding windows instead of expecting an all-day bite that hasn't materialized yet.
