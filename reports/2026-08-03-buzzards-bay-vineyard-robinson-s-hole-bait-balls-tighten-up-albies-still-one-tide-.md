# Robinson's Hole Bait Balls Tighten Up — Albies Still One Tide Behind
*Silversides and peanut bunker are stacking in the rips between the Elizabeths and the Vineyard, and every sign says the first false albacore push is close, but nobody's boated one yet.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — August 3** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

I stood on the rocks at Robinson's Hole Thursday evening on the last hour of the ebb and watched a bait ball the size of a Volkswagen get pushed through the gut between Pasque and Nashawena, terns working it from above, a bluefish or two slashing underneath. That's the tell. That's the exact bait signature — small silversides mixed with peanut bunker — that shows up right before the albies find it. It's just not albie season yet, not officially, even though the water's telling me it wants to be.

We flagged this corridor last week — said Menemsha, Gay Head, and the Elizabeth Island rips were the prime staging ground for the false albacore and bonito push, and that the building tide cycle into the new moon would be the trigger. Here we are, bait stacking up in all the right spots, and I'll be honest with you: I haven't heard a confirmed albie or bonito hookup out of this zone yet. Scout fish, maybe — a few guys running out of Menemsha have had quick, unconfirmed flashes on the surface off Squibnocket that could've been bonito working bait and gone before a cast got out. That's how it starts. That's exactly how it starts every year. The new moon lands August 12, and the tide is already building toward it — bigger swings, harder current through Quick's Hole and Robinson's Hole, which is the fuel this whole run needs. If I had to bet, the first real hookups come in the next seven to ten days, right around that new moon and the few days after when the rips are ripping hardest.

While we wait on that, the bread-and-butter bite has been the scup, and it's been dependable. Fishing squid strips and sandworm chunks on standard high-low rigs off the Woods Hole pilings and around the Vineyard Haven harbor structure, guys are filling coolers with foot-long scup on the last two hours of the incoming. Nothing glamorous, but it's a sure thing when the flashier stuff is still a question mark.

Striped bass have gone quiet in the daytime, and that's not a surprise given how warm the surface layer has gotten — we're seeing that pattern hold right across southern New England, fish sliding deep and turning nocturnal rather than working the surface in the heat. Buzzards Bay side, the better bass have been coming off the deeper edges around Naushon and the Middle Ground on eels and bunker chunks fished after dark, outgoing tide, working the deeper holes in 25 to 35 feet where the water stays a few degrees cooler. If you're set up to fish the tide change at 10 or 11 at night off Woods Hole, that's been worth the lost sleep. Daytime bass fishing has been tough sledding — I won't sugarcoat it. A lot of casts for not a lot of fish unless you're specifically working that night window.

Fluke have been the most consistent producer in daylight hours, and that hasn't changed. Drifting the sand between Naushon and the Buzzards Bay mainland, and again off the Vineyard side toward Oak Bluffs, 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with Gulp Swimming Mullets have been picking up keepers mixed with a lot of shorts — steady rather than a blitz, which is about what you'd expect for early August with water this warm and stable. Depths of 20 to 35 feet on the last two hours of the ebb have been the sweet spot, when the current slows just enough to work the jig properly without getting swept off the structure.

Bluefish are the one species that's been unambiguously good, and that tracks with what's happening up and down the coast right now — warm surface water has these fish fired up everywhere. Off the Cuttyhunk rip and the Sow and Pigs reef, diamond jigs and topwater poppers worked through the白 boils on the outgoing tide have produced non-stop action, three- to six-pound blues mixed with the occasional bigger fish. If you want guaranteed bent rods this week, that's where I'd point the boat.

Looking ahead: the tide keeps building toward the 12th, and that's the window I'm circling on the calendar. Menemsha Bight and Devil's Bridge off Gay Head are the first places I'll be checking every morning starting midweek — small profile baits, Albie Snax in bone or pink-white, half-ounce Deadly Dicks, ready to go the second I see fish working bait on the surface instead of just birds working bait underneath. Until then, work the scup and fluke bite in daylight, hunt the bass at night in the deeper holes, and keep an eye on that bait. When the silversides start getting nervous and the terns start diving harder than normal, that's your signal — not a forecast, not a guess, just fish telling you what's coming.
