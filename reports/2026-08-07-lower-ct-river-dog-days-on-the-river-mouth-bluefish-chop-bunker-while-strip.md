# Dog Days on the River Mouth — Bluefish Chop Bunker While Stripers Hug the Salt Wedge
*With the river running warm and slack upstream, the real action has slid down to Saybrook Point and Great Island, where the Sound's cooler water still gives predators a reason to feed.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — August 7** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The shad are a memory now — gone back to sea in June, the way they always do, and the river has settled into that heavy, green-gold stillness that marks the dog days. Marsh grass on Great Island has gone from spring's tender shoots to a thick, tall stand, and if you've walked the causeway lately you know the greenheads have hatched out in force — that's as reliable a calendar marker on this river as the shad bloom itself. High summer on the lower Connecticut means one thing above the Baldwin Bridge and something else entirely below Saybrook Point, and this week that split has never been more obvious.

Upriver, from Essex on up, the water has gone slow and warm — too warm to hold striped bass with any conviction, and the coves are handing the show over to white perch. That's the honest read. If you want a bend in the rod without burning gas or nerves, the perch fishing in the quieter guts off Essex and around the mouth of the Lieutenant River in Old Lyme has been dependable on the tail of the outgoing tide, small grass shrimp or bloodworm bits fished on a size 6 hook under a split shot, tight to the pilings and the shaded banks where the water stacks a degree or two cooler. Nothing glamorous, but it's honest action on a river that's otherwise gone quiet in the middle reaches.

The real story this week is at the mouth. We flagged this two weeks back — said the warm river water would keep pushing bait and predators down toward the mouth through this stable stretch of weather, and that's exactly what's playing out. Saybrook Point, the outer bar, and the flats off Great Island are where the salt wedge from Long Island Sound pushes up against the river's outflow, and that mixing zone is holding water noticeably cooler and more oxygenated than anything upstream. Sound temperatures reported from Kings Point in the mid-70s and climbing toward the high 70s further east give you a sense of the gradient — the river mouth sits right in that transition, and this time of year that transition is where the fish want to be.

Bluefish have moved into that zone in real numbers, working bunker pods that have been stacking along the outer bar and off the Saybrook breakwater. These aren't finicky fish — when they find a pod of peanut bunker pushed up against the jetty rocks on a dropping tide, the surface goes to pieces fast, and it doesn't last long before the school scatters and regroups somewhere else along the beach. Fresh bunker chunks fished on a wire leader, or a heavy diamond jig with a strip of belly meat, worked at the edge of the melee, has been the most consistent producer. If you can get there when the birds are diving — terns and gulls both — don't waste time easing in quietly. These blitzes burn out in twenty minutes and move.

Striped bass are mixed into the same water but behaving differently — holding deeper, along the channel edges off the Point and the drop-off inside the outer bar, using the current seams rather than chasing bait to the surface the way the blues do. The better fish have been coming on live or fresh-dead bunker fished on a fish-finder rig, bounced slow along the bottom on the last two hours of the ebb, when the outflow is running hardest and pushing the most bait past the structure. A few reports of stripers taken on bucktails worked along the rock pile at the Point on the same stage of tide, which tracks — a 1- to 1.5-ounce white or chartreuse bucktail dropped and hopped slow through that current seam has always been a lower-river producer when the fish are holding tight to structure instead of chasing.

Worth noting: the stable high-pressure pattern that's been holding over the Sound is exactly the kind of window that keeps bunker schools thick and predictable in one place long enough to build a pattern around. If that holds through the coming week, look for the bluefish blitzes to keep concentrating around the same stretch of beach and bar, and for the bass bite to stay steady on the last of the ebb rather than turning on and off with the wind, which is what happens when a front comes through and scatters the bait. If a system does move in and stirs things up, I'd expect the fish to push even tighter to the deep water off the Point rather than abandon the mouth altogether — there's nowhere better for them to go this time of year.

For the coming week, the plan is simple: fish the last of the outgoing at Saybrook Point and the outer bar for stripers on bunker and bucktail, watch for diving birds off Great Island for the bluefish, and if the heat has you looking for something calmer, work the shaded coves at Essex and Old Lyme for perch on the same tide change. The shad run may be done for the year, but the mouth of this river is doing what it always does in August — holding the coolest, most oxygenated water for miles, and every predator on this stretch knows it.
