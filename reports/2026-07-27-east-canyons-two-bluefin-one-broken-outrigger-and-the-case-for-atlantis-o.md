# Two Bluefin, One Broken Outrigger, and the Case for Atlantis Over Veatch This Week
*The chunk bite held on the west wall while the troll spread paid for the fuel — here's how the run shook out and where I'd point the bow next.*

**MONTAUK, NY — July 27** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

We cleared the Point at 0330 Tuesday with 180 gallons in the tanks — enough to make Atlantis and back with a weather margin, not enough to go chasing a rumor another forty miles east to Veatch. That's the arithmetic you live with out here. Every mile past the canyon edge is a mile you're paying for twice, once going and once coming back with less fuel than you'd like if the wind decides to have an opinion on the way home. I made the call for Atlantis before we left the dock, and I'd make it again.

First light put us on the west wall in about 800 fathoms, trolling a spread of green machines and a naked ballyhoo off the shotgun, and we didn't have to wait long. A pair of bluefin, both mid-70s to low-80s, came up on the short rigger inside of twenty minutes of each other — textbook slot fish, exactly the size class that's been holding steady out here since the July 3rd trophy closure pushed everybody's attention down into that range. We iced both, then the troll bite went quiet the way it does once the sun climbs and the fish slide down off the warm skin water.

That's when we set up to chunk on the east wall, working butterflied bunker down a slow chum slick over a temperature break we found running right along the 100-fathom curve — water pushing 79 degrees on the warm side of the seam, dropping into the mid-70s on the cold side. Yellowfin have been sitting right on that kind of edge all month, and Tuesday was no different. We had three bites in about ninety minutes on circle hooks buried in bunker chunks, freespooled back with the reel in strike, and boated two — a 45-pounder and one closer to 60. Lost the third at boatside, hook pulled clean, which happens enough on chunk fish that I don't lose sleep over it anymore.

What I do lose sleep over is the wahoo that hit the long bar rigger at first light and cut us off before we ever saw color. That's the tax you pay running high-speed spreads through canyon water this time of year — you're going to donate a lure or two to something with teeth. We didn't see another wahoo sign the rest of the day, but the one bite told me they're around, which is worth knowing if you're setting up a dawn troll pattern on the drop-offs.

Mahi were scattered rather than schooled — a few teenagers came up under a mat of sargassum weed inside the canyon proper, and we picked off four on bucktail-and-ballyhoo combos flipped right into the shade line before they wised up and scattered. Nothing like the gaffer-class mahi push you sometimes get when a real weed line sets up, but enough to make it worth keeping a rod rigged and ready any time you cross floating structure. White marlin showed twice on the long teaser, both times refusing to commit to the pitch bait, which tracks with what I've seen most of the summer — plenty of interest, not much conversion. If you're marlin-focused, keep the naked ballyhoo dredge in the water and be patient with the pitch rod; they'll eventually make a mistake, but it won't be often.

We flagged this pattern last week and it's held — steady bluefin pace, yellowfin working the warm filaments, nothing dramatic changing day to day under this settled high-pressure setup. That stability is the whole story right now. No fronts pushing through, no hard temperature swings, just a canyon system doing what it does in late July when the Gulf Stream keeps feeding warm water onto the edges and the fish stay put on it instead of running.

For the boats coming out of Block Island and the RI ports, Hydrographer and Oceanographer are the longer commitment — figure another twenty-plus miles past what a Montauk boat burns to reach Atlantis, and that's before you factor current and sea state on the way home. If you're making that run, I'd want a flat forecast both ways and a full tank with margin, not a tank that gets you there and leaves you praying on the return leg. Worth it if the temperature break is sharp and the reports are fresh, not worth it on spec.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full in early August, and that means more current pushing through these canyon systems over the next week. Stronger current sharpens temperature breaks and usually tightens up where the bait — and the tuna behind it — decide to sit. I'd expect the chunk bite to stay productive on whichever wall is holding the sharpest edge, and I wouldn't be surprised to see the bluefin bite hold its pace as long as this weather window stays open. If it breaks down into wind and sea, the smart play is running less distance for more certainty — Atlantis over Veatch, the west wall over a Hail Mary sixty miles east. Fish the water you can get home from.
