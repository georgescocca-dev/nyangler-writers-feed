# 168 feet off the west side: haddock stacked on the mud, bluefin working the top of the Bank
*Stable high pressure held like we said it would, and Stellwagen rewarded anyone willing to run the gravel and watch the birds.*

**GLOUCESTER, MA — August 3** — _by Capt. Donna Shaw, Zone Writer_

168 feet, mud bottom, west side of Stellwagen off the Basin edge — that's where I found the best marks all week. Haddock were stacked two and three deep on the sounder, tight to bottom in that soft gray mud that runs along the 160-to-220 line between the SW Corner and the Basin. Jigged them on diamond jigs tipped with a strip of squid, and on the slower drifts we did just as well on a standard high-low rig with clam. Fish ran 18 to 24 inches, a few pushing better than that, and it didn't take long to fill a cooler once we found the right patch of bottom. The mud doesn't move much this time of year — it's the same real estate it's been for decades — but you still have to hunt for where the bait's sitting on any given tide.

Cod are a different story, and I'll be straight with you: recreational cod on Stellwagen right now means mostly catch-and-release, with the federal closures doing what they do in August. We picked up a handful of nice fish — 8 to 12 pounds — jigging the gravel patches up on the northern flank near the Fingers, 90 to 110 feet, but they went back over the side. If you want a cod dinner this month you're better off putting your energy into the haddock and letting the cod swim. That's just where the regs sit right now, and fighting it doesn't change anything.

The story everybody's asking about at the dock is the tuna, and I'll tell you what we're actually seeing rather than what people wish we were seeing. Bluefin are scouting — that's the right word for it. Mackerel schools have been thick on top of the Bank, 60 to 90 feet of water in spots along the eastern edge near Tillies, and we've had days where the birds are diving, the whales are working right through the same bait, and somewhere underneath all that commotion there are tuna cutting through. Marks on the sounder showing 40 to 60 feet down over 100 to 130 feet of water — that's your giant class fish pushing up on the bait balls. Some boats connected trolling green machines and spreader bars through the froth; others got shut out entirely on the same piece of water two hours later. That's the nature of scouting fish. They're here, they're not settled yet, and the bite windows are short and unpredictable day to day. If you're rigging for bluefin right now, I'd have a chunking spread ready to deploy the second you see the surface activity — don't wait, because it can shut off just as fast as it .

We flagged stable conditions holding through this stretch last week, and that's exactly what played out — light winds, clean water, no big fronts pushing through to stir up the Bank. That steadiness is a big part of why the haddock bite on the mud stayed so consistent; when the water's not getting kicked around, those fish sit predictably and you can work a drift methodically instead of chasing marks all over the chart. Same reason the mackerel schools have had time to build up on top of the Bank instead of getting scattered by a blow.

Pollock have been a nice bonus around the wreck structure on the northern end and along some of the steeper drop-offs into the Basin — 15 to 20 feet of jigging line off bottom in 100 to 130 feet, using the same diamond jigs we're running for haddock. They school up tight and when you find them you can load the box fast, though they don't hold like the haddock do — one pass and they scatter.

Whale watching boats have been running right alongside the same bait schools we're targeting for tuna, which tells you everything about where the forage is sitting this week. Humpbacks working the surface on mackerel, sometimes right on top of Tillies, sometimes drifting closer to the Basin edge depending on where the bait's pushed to. If you see whale-watch boats parked somewhere unusual, that's worth a look — they're following the same signal we are.

Hake have shown up mixed in with the haddock catches on the mud, nothing to target specifically but a welcome bycatch on the same rigs. Nothing unusual there for early August.

Looking ahead, I don't see a reason for this pattern to break hard in the next few days. The high pressure's been steady, the water's clean, and the bait's holding position on top of the Bank rather than getting pushed off by weather. If that holds, I'd keep working the mud for haddock as your bread and butter — it's the most reliable thing going right now — and treat the tuna as a bonus you chase when you see the birds and whales working. I wouldn't run offshore specifically banking on bluefin connections yet; the scouting pattern needs another week or two to solidify into something you can plan a whole trip around. What I would do is keep gear rigged for both on the same trip, because right now you genuinely don't know which one's going to show up first when the marks light up on the screen. Watch for any wind shift out of the southwest building through the weekend — that's the kind of thing that could stir the mud line and scatter the haddock, so if you've got a window before it, I'd take it.
