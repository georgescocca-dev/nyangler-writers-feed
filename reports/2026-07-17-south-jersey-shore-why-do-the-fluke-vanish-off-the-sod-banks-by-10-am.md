# Why Do the Fluke Vanish Off the Sod Banks by 10 AM?
*The answer is temperature, not luck — and once you understand the split, the back bay fluke program practically teaches itself.*

**ATLANTIC CITY, NJ — July 17** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I get this question at the bait shop counter every July, and I got it three times this week: why does the fluke bite at Absecon's sod banks turn off like a switch around mid-morning, then come back to life at Great Egg a few hours later when everybody else has already gone home? It's not luck. It's thermal stratification, and if you fish the back bays long enough you start reading it the way you'd read a tide chart.

Here's the lesson. Those shallow sod bank flats — four to six feet of water over mud and grass, the kind you find along Absecon Channel behind Brigantine and up in the Rainbow Channel flats off Great Egg — heat up fast once the sun gets on them. By 9 or 10 in the morning that water can climb into the high 70s, and fluke don't like sitting in a bathtub. They slide off the flats and drop into the channel edges, the 10 to 16 foot troughs that run alongside — Graveling Point, the deeper bends of Broad Thorofare, the cuts near the Route 52 causeway. That's where they'll hold through the heat of the day. The trick is to fish the flats hard on the first two hours of the outgoing, when the water's still cool from the overnight low, then follow the fish downhill into the channel as the sun climbs. Same fish, different address.

The bait that's been doing the damage on both ends of that migration is a 4-inch chartreuse or white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, dragged slow along the drop-offs where the flat meets the channel. Bull minnow on a simple fluke rig has been the better call in the afternoon, when the fish get sticky and won't commit to the plastic in the warmer water. Sizes have been mixed but honest — plenty of 15 to 17 inch fish to sort through, keepers in the 17 to 19 inch range showing up consistently off the outer flats at Great Egg, and a few fish pushing 4 and 5 pounds coming off the deeper edges near the Route 52 bridge on the last two hours of the outgoing tide. That's been the most dependable window all week — not the flood, the back half of the ebb, right before the bottom of the tide.

Weakfish have been a smaller story, but a real one if you're willing to get up early. The rock pile at Absecon Inlet has given up a handful of 3 to 5 pound weakfish in the gray light before sunrise, on Gulp shrimp bodies or a bucktail tipped with a strip of FishBites, worked slow through the rip on the tail end of the outgoing. It's not a bite you can set your watch by — some mornings it's two fish, some mornings it's nothing — but the class of fish has been worth the early alarm.

On the ocean side, kingfish have kept steady on the sandbars off 34th Street in Ocean City, down through Sea Isle and out to Stone Harbor Point, on bloodworm or FishBites strips fished on a simple high-low rig in two to four feet of water. The surf's had a lingering groundswell running through it much of the week — short-period, a little lumpy, enough to color the wash and keep the sight-feeders off their game — but kingfish don't care about clarity, they care about scent, and that bite has held up fine right through the chop. Best window is early, before the beaches fill and the wave runners start churning the inside bar.

Bluefish have been the reliable entertainment at both inlets — schools of cocktail blues in the 1 to 3 pound class working peanut bunker and spearing at first light and again at dusk, right at the mouths of Absecon and Townsends. Chrome spoons or a small popper worked fast across the surface will get bit fast when they're up, and they've been up most mornings this week. It's not the fall run yet — that's still a good six weeks off — but it's a fun half hour before the sun gets high.

Triggerfish have started showing as incidental catches around the inshore reef structure off Great Egg and Wildwood, mostly for guys soaking clam on bottom rigs while working the reef for fluke. Nothing to plan a trip around yet, but it's the first real sign of the pattern that usually builds through August.

I'll be straight with you on striped bass — July is the quiet month down here, and this year hasn't broken that rule. A few holdover fish have come off the rocks at Absecon Inlet after dark on eels, but it's a long, patient game for one or two fish, and I wouldn't call it a program right now.

Looking ahead, if that ocean groundswell backs off like it typically does by the weekend, I'd expect the kingfish bite on the outer bars to sharpen up as the wash cleans out. The sod bank fluke pattern should keep holding as long as that morning-to-afternoon temperature split stays in place — fish the flats early, chase them into the channels late. That's where I'll have my boat this week, and it's where I'd start if you've only got one tide to work with.
