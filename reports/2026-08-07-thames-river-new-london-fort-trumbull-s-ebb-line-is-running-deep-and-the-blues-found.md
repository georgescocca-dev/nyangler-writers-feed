# Fort Trumbull's ebb line is running deep — and the blues found the bunker
*Seventy-seven degree water pushed the Thames stripers nocturnal, but bunker schools off Ocean Beach are keeping the blues honest all day long.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 7** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

I stood on the Fort Trumbull wall Tuesday evening watching the ebb line pull away from the point, and it's narrower than it was two weeks ago — tighter, faster, folding back on itself right where the old submarine net used to hang. That seam is doing exactly what it does this time of year: concentrating everything that's moving with the tide into a rope of current about forty feet wide. New London's surface water is sitting at 77.4°F right now, which is warm enough that the stripers in this river have gone full nocturnal. If you're fishing that seam at two in the afternoon, you're probably fishing an empty house. Fish it an hour before dark into the first two hours of the ebb, and it's a different story.

The bass pattern right now is classic mid-summer Thames: fish holding deep and tight to structure during daylight, sliding up onto the current edges to feed once the light drops. Gold Star Bridge pilings have been the most consistent producer — I've had reports of fish in the 28-34 inch range coming off the downriver side of the pilings on the incoming tide, 30 to 35 feet down, eels worked slow along the concrete and bunker chunks fished on the bottom with just enough weight to hold in the current. Nothing explosive, but steady if you're willing to fish the dark hours. Fort Trumbull itself has been better on the back half of the ebb, working swim shads and bucktails through that deep hole off the point — 25 to 40 feet — letting the jig sink all the way to bottom before starting the retrieve. That's where the bigger fish have been sitting, using the depth to escape the heat the same way they would in December, just for the opposite reason.

We flagged this pattern two weeks back — said the outgoing tide would keep producing the most consistent bite as long as the weather stayed stable, and that's exactly how it's played out. The stable high pressure sitting over the Sound hasn't given us much current variation, so the tide change itself has become the trigger. No tide change, no bite. Simple as that this time of year.

Where things have actually picked up is the bluefish. Bunker schools have moved into the outer harbor and along Ocean Beach and Pleasure Beach in real numbers, and the blues found them hard. We called this one too — said the blues would track the bunker distribution and the back-bay action would spike, and that's exactly what's happening. Adult blues in the 3-6 pound class are working those bunker schools in open water off Ocean Beach, and when they push a pod up against the surface you'll see the whole thing erupt — bunker skying, blues slashing through, gulls dropping on the scraps. If you can get out there when it happens, a big topwater popper or a bucktail with a strip of pork rind thrown right into the boil will get bit fast. It doesn't last long — five, ten minutes sometimes — so keep the boat ready and don't waste time changing lures once you're on fish.

Inside the harbor and up into the river mouth, snapper blues are thick — good news if you've got kids or grandkids who just want bent rods. Small Kastmasters or a piece of spearing on a bare hook off any dock from Pleasure Beach up toward the Coast Guard Academy will keep them busy for hours.

Porgies have been solid out at Bartlett Reef and around Black Point, fishing the high tide with sandworms or clam on a simple hi-lo rig. Nothing huge — mostly 9 to 12 inch fish — but they're biting consistently enough to fill a cooler if that's what you're after, and it's a good way to spend a slow afternoon between striper sessions.

Blackfish are still a minor story right now. A few anglers picking at small ones around the rock piles near the bridge on green crab, but the real blackfish bite in this river doesn't turn on until the water starts cooling in September and October. I wouldn't make a special trip for them yet — file that one away for six weeks from now.

Looking ahead, the tide is rebuilding out of the recent neap phase, which should sharpen up the current-dependent spots — Fort Trumbull's rip especially, and I'd expect the Gold Star pilings bite to get a little more aggressive as the flow strengthens. If the stable pattern holds through the weekend, I'd plan around the tide change no matter what you're targeting — fish the last two hours of daylight into dark on the ebb for bass, and keep an eye on the bunker schools off Ocean Beach for blues any time of day. The one thing worth watching is the calendar — we're deep into hurricane season, and this kind of calm stretch tends not to last forever. If something starts spinning up in the Atlantic, I'd get my Thames trips in early in the week rather than betting on the weekend.
