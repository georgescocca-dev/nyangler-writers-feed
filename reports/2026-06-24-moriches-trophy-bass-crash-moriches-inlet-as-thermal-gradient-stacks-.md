# Trophy bass crash Moriches Inlet as thermal gradient stacks bait on the flood
*Night fishing with live spot produces doormat stripers while bay water hits 68 degrees.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — June 24** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The inlet's been firing on all cylinders this week, and the numbers tell the story better than any fish tale. Bay water climbed to 68 degrees while the ocean side holds at 63 — that five-degree gradient is creating a bait highway that's got stripers lined up like cars at a toll booth.

The action peaks after dark when live spot becomes absolute currency. I'm talking trophy-class bass, fish that make your drag scream and test every knot you tied. The sweet spot runs from the inlet mouth back to the first bend, where the current creates that perfect ambush zone. Fish a fishfinder rig with 4/0 circle hooks, keep your spot lively on the bottom, and be ready for the freight train hit.

What's driving this bite is pure physics. The flood tide pushes that cooler ocean water into the bay, but it rides underneath the warmer surface layer. Baitfish get compressed in that thermal sandwich, and the bass know exactly where to set up shop. I've been watching the current meter, and when we hit that sweet spot between slack and full flood — usually about two hours after low water — the inlet comes alive.

Daytime fluke fishing in the bay proper has been solid but technical. Water temps pushing 68 have the flatfish spread out across the shallow flats, but you need to think small and slow. I'm talking 1/4-ounce bucktails with 3-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets, dragged so slow you think you're doing it wrong. The fish are holding in 6 to 10 feet, right where the channel edges meet the flats.

Cupsogue Beach has been producing consistent action for surf casters working the early morning and evening tides. The key is reading the cuts — last week's southwest blow carved some new channels that are funneling bait right into the wash. Clam baits on fishfinder rigs are taking bass and blues, while bucktails worked through the deeper cuts are connecting with fluke.

The thermal structure is setting up perfectly for the summer pattern. That 68-degree bay water is warm enough to activate the food chain but not so hot that fish go deep or lethargic. The gradient at the inlet mouth creates a natural feeding station where bass can ambush bait moving with the tide.

Bait situation is excellent. Peanut bunker are thick in the back bay, and the spot population is robust. If you're live-lining, keep your bait in the lower third of the water column where the cooler water holds more oxygen. Dead-sticking works, but a slow retrieve every few minutes keeps the bait active and draws more strikes.

Weakfish are starting to show in better numbers, particularly on the outgoing tide when they follow bait out of the bay. Small bucktails with pink or chartreuse teasers are producing fish to 18 inches, mostly after sunset when they move shallow to feed.

Looking ahead, the new moon on Friday brings spring tides that should really fire up the inlet. Those big tide swings will flush more bait through the system and concentrate predators in the predictable spots. I'm expecting the night bite to get even better as we move into the full summer pattern. Water temps should stabilize in the low 70s, which is prime time for everything from bass to fluke to the first real push of summer weakfish.
