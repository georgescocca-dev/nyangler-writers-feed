# New moon springs push Moriches Inlet into overdrive as bay fluke run for the cut
*Hot bay water and building tidal exchange are stacking fluke and bass at the inlet throat — here's where the current is doing the work for you.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — July 10** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay did what it always does in the second week of July — it cooked. Great South Bay pushed past 78 degrees, and Moriches Bay wasn't far behind, running warm and soft on the flats behind Smith Point and up into the East Moriches guts. That kind of heat doesn't kill the fishing, but it moves it. Fluke that were spread across the bay in June start funneling toward anything with current and cooler water underneath it, and the inlet becomes the pinch point for all of it. We're coming off a stretch of light, variable wind — nothing that reshaped the bar, nothing that cut a new channel — so the inlet's holding the same basic structure it's had since the spring dredge: main channel favoring the Cupsogue side, a secondary cut working itself in off the Westhampton spit that I'd keep an eye on if we get any kind of blow this month.

This week the moon is the story. We're sitting right on the new moon, which means spring tides building through the weekend — peak alignment looks like Friday into Monday, July 11-13. Bigger tidal range means more water moving through that inlet throat on both ends of the cycle, and more water moving means bait gets flushed hard on the outgoing and pulled back hard on the incoming. For an inlet system like this one, that's the difference between a lazy bite and fish stacking up and feeding with purpose. Ocean-side water that's upwelled into the low 70s is far more comfortable to a fluke or a bass than the upper-70s bathwater sitting in the bay right now, so everything with a brain is sliding toward that gradient. That's the mechanism — it's not luck, it's thermal preference meeting tidal timing.

Fluke are the headline right now, and the pattern is textbook bay-to-inlet shift. Guys grinding the deeper holes off Tuthill Point and the flats behind Pattersquash are still picking at fish, but the quality bite has moved to the inlet itself — the deep scour hole just inside the jetty on the Cupsogue side, and the channel edge where it necks down before spilling into the ocean. Best water's been the last two hours of the outgoing into the start of the incoming, working 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, bounced slow along bottom in 12 to 18 feet. A couple of solid fish in the 4-6 pound range have come off that stretch, along with plenty of teen-inch fish that need to go back. If the bay bite dies on you by midday, that's your sign the fish already made the move — don't keep grinding shallow water, go find the current.

Striped bass are doing what they do on a building spring tide at this inlet — funneling through the throat on the last of the outgoing, ambushing whatever gets swept out of the bay. The rock jetty at Cupsogue on the ocean side has been giving up bass in the mid-20s to low 30-inch range for guys throwing bunker chunks on fish-finder rigs right at the base of the rocks, and there's been a scattered bite on white bucktails worked through the wash for anyone willing to walk the surf at first light. Nothing blitz-worthy, but consistent enough that two or three solid fish in a session isn't unusual right now, especially with that new moon current running hard.

Bluefish have shown up on the ocean side in decent numbers, chopping through the wash off Cupsogue and out toward the tip of the barrier — small to mid-size cocktail blues mostly, happy to hit anything metal. If you're throwing swimmers or bucktails for bass and getting bit off, that's your blues moving in behind the bait getting pushed out on the tide.

Weakfish I have to be straight with you on — the water's plenty warm enough, well above the old 70-degree threshold that used to trigger a Moriches Bay weakfish push, but I'm not hearing of real numbers. A few incidental fish mixed in with the fluke bite in the deeper bay holes, nothing organized. That old temperature rule isn't wrong, it's just not the whole answer anymore — something else has to line up, and right now it isn't. I wouldn't plan a trip around weakfish this week.

Porgies are steady but unspectacular around the inlet rock piles and the pilings at the Route 27 bridge — bloodworms and clam on a standard high-low rig in 10 to 15 feet will keep you bent, good for a mixed bag while you wait out the tide change for bass. Blackfish are off the table for most of you right now with the summer closure in effect on most of the coast — leave the tog gear at home until fall.

There's also chatter worth watching on the ocean side — the pelagic window that's been open since the Fourth is closing out, and early bonito and Spanish mackerel have started showing on the eastern inlets on clean incoming water. Moriches isn't typically a bonito address, but on a clean, calm morning with good visibility off Cupsogue, it's worth having a small metal or Deadly Dick rigged just in case something silver and fast comes through with the blues.

Looking ahead, I'd fish this weekend's spring tide hard. If the wind stays out of the south or southwest and light, the inlet current on Saturday's outgoing into Sunday's incoming should be about as good as it gets this month for both fluke and bass working that throat. If it swings onshore and stirs up the bar, I'd shift inside to the deeper bay holes off Tuthill and wait it out. Either way, that thermal gap between bay and ocean is doing the work right now — go find where it's biggest.
