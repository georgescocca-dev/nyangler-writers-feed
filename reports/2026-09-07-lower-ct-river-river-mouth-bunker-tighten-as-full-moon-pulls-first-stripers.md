# River Mouth Bunker Tighten as Full Moon Pulls First Stripers Into Position
*Cooler nights and a building tide are stacking bait at Saybrook Point — the bass are circling, not yet committed.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — September 7** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The marsh grass out along Great Island has that first bronze edge to it, the goldenrod is thick along the causeway into Old Lyme, and the ospreys that spent all August working the flats are thinning out, headed south ahead of schedule. That's the calendar telling you what the thermometer hasn't quite admitted yet — summer's grip on this river is loosening. Nights have dropped enough that the mouth is starting to feel the difference, and that matters more than any single number on a gauge. Cooler nights mean bunker schools stop wandering and start balling, and balled bunker is what turns a scattered September into a real fall run.

That's exactly what's setting up right now. The full moon that came through the night of the 6th into the 7th did what a September full moon on this river is supposed to do — it put muscle into the tide, and the extra current through the mouth at Saybrook Point and down along the Old Lyme side has been pulling bunker schools tighter than they've been in weeks. We flagged this coming — said the building moon would tighten bait and that the first real interest from migrating stripers wouldn't be far behind — and the reports out of the mouth this week back it up. Boats working the outer breakwaters and the flats off Great Island are marking bunker balled up tight on the sounder, with bass underneath them, nosing the edges rather than blowing through them yet. That's staging behavior, not blitz behavior. Anyone who tells you it's already busting wide open at the mouth is getting ahead of the calendar.

What's actually happening: stripers in the 24 to 32-inch range, with a scattering of better fish pushing 36, are working the current seams where the river dumps out past the Saybrook Point breakwaters on the back half of the outgoing tide. That's the window — roughly two hours before dead low through the first hour of the new flood — when the river's cooler, denser water pushes hardest against the Sound and creates the rip line bunker get pinned against. Live-lining a bunker on a fish-finder rig, hooked through the nose with just enough weight to keep it down in that seam, has been the most consistent way to connect. Bucktails in white or bunker-colored, 1 to 1.5 ounce, worked with a slow lift-and-drop along the same current edge, are picking off fish that won't commit to the livey but will eat something that looks like a straggler peeling off the school.

Great Island continues to earn its reputation as the nursery water it's always been. The flats there are holding smaller schoolie bass mixed with snapper blues, and that combination is making for some genuinely fun light-tackle sessions on the dropping tide, when bait gets flushed off the grass edges into the deeper guts that cut through the flats. Small soft plastics — 3-inch white or pearl paddletails on a light jighead — are outfishing bigger baits down there right now, simply because the forage moving through is small: peanut bunker, silversides, the last of the summer's grass shrimp. Snapper blues remain thick in the lower river from Essex down past the DEP Marina, and they're providing steady, uncomplicated action for anyone fishing small Kastmasters or Gotcha plugs off the docks — good news for families and kids getting their first taste of fall fishing before school schedules take over the weekends.

White perch haven't been the story this week, and it's worth saying so rather than pretending otherwise. They're still up in the tidal creeks and the deeper holes around Essex, but the bite there has been slow and inconsistent, more a byproduct bite for anyone soaking bloodworms for something else than a target worth planning a trip around right now. That's typical for early September on this stretch — the perch bite tends to pick back up once water temperatures ease further and the fish push out of the deep holes to feed harder ahead of winter.

The next several days matter. The moon is swinging toward new phase on September 10, and on this river that's traditionally the pivot point where staging turns into commitment. If the bunker schools keep tightening at the rate they have been, and the nights keep cooling the way they've started to, the window between the 9th and the 12th is where the first legitimate blitz behavior of the fall ought to show at the mouth — bass breaking the surface on bunker rather than just shadowing them from below. That's not a guarantee of fireworks on any single tide, but it's the kind of setup that's produced real fall runs on this river fifty times over. If there's only one tide worth planning around this weekend, make it the last two hours of the outgoing at Saybrook Point, working the current seam off the breakwaters with a livey or a bunker-pattern bucktail, and keep an eye on the birds working over Great Island — they'll tell you before the sounder does when the bait finally gets pushed into panic.
