# 77.4°F at New London: The Race Bite Squeezes Into the Slack
*Warm water at the mouth of the Sound is pushing bass activity into narrower windows — and the bonito clock just started ticking on Plum Gut.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 7** — _by Capt. Sabrina Cole, Zone Writer_

New London is reading 77.4°F this week, and that number tells you almost everything you need to know about how to fish The Race right now. That's warm water sitting right at the throat of the strongest current on the East Coast, and it changes the calculus. Bass don't want to sit in 77-degree water grinding against 4-plus knots of moving current burning oxygen they don't have to spare. So the bite isn't spread across the tide anymore — it's compressed into the transition, the last hour of the drop and the first hour of the build, right around the turn. Miss that window at Valiant Rock and you're marking bait on the sounder with nothing willing to commit.

Valiant Rock still tops out around 18 feet, and on a strong moon the water sluicing over it runs hard enough to turn a bucktail sideways if you're not paying attention to your retrieve angle. Right now, with the thermal load this high, the better program is fishing the last 30 minutes of ebb into dead slack with wire line and a 2-ounce bucktail — white or chartreuse, tipped with a strip of pork rind or a Fin-S fluke trailer — dropped down the upcurrent side and swept across the top of the structure. Once the water goes truly slack, that's your five-to-ten-minute window to work the deep pockets on the east side of the rock with a jointed swimmer before the current reverses and picks back up. Race Rock itself is fishing the same way — same tactic, same clock, just shift your position to the rip line forming off the lighthouse on the building flood.

Bluefish have been the more reliable player through this stretch, and that tracks with what's been building all week — bunker pods holding thick through the Sound corridor, and wherever they concentrate, the blues are turning it into topwater violence. That pattern's held under the stable high pressure, and there's no reason to think it breaks unless the wind shifts hard. Poppers and 1-ounce Kastmasters worked over visible bait balls off Plum Gut and along the Sluiceway edge are getting hit before the lure even settles. If you're marking bunker on the meter anywhere between Little Gull and Great Gull Island, work the edges of the pod rather than casting into the middle of it — the blues are working the perimeter, picking off stragglers.

Fluke are still there for anyone willing to grind the sand edges. The drift along the Plum Gut channel margins and the flat stretches south of Great Gull Island have been giving up keepers on white Gulp Swimming Mullets rigged on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow on the last of the outgoing. Nothing spectacular — a fish or two per drift, occasional shorts mixed in — but it's a livable bite if bass and blues aren't cooperating. Black sea bass are holding tight to the boulder fields off Race Rock in 40 to 55 feet, and small green crab or squid strips on a standard sea bass rig are still producing, though the better-sized fish have thinned out some as the season's pressure builds.

Now here's the part worth paying attention to. We flagged the bonito threshold building earlier this week — said the water in the Montauk, Block Island, and Buzzards Bay corridor was closing in on the numbers that typically trigger the first arrivals. Montauk's sitting at 74.5°F and New London's at 77.4°F, and that's exactly the kind of warm-water bridge that lets bonito push west out of the Block Island Sound approaches and into the Gut. Nobody's called them in yet on this stretch, but the water's right, and if you're fishing the Sluiceway or the rip lines off Little Gull Island this week, it's worth having a small epoxy jig tied on and ready — silver or pink, size 1/0, worked with a fast, erratic retrieve through any nervous bait you see dimpling the surface. False albacore typically lag a few weeks behind bonito in this zone, so don't expect them yet, but the bonito door looks like it's cracking open.

Honest read on the week: the bass bite is real but narrow — you need to be on Valiant Rock or Race Rock within that slack window, wire line down, ready to move the second the current starts building again. Outside that window, you're better off working blues on bunker or grinding fluke on the sand. The heat's the whole story right now — it's not killing the bite, it's just shrinking the workable hours.

Looking ahead, if this high-pressure pattern holds and the thermal gradient stays steep between the Western Sound and the mouth at New London, expect the bass pattern to stay slack-dependent through the coming week. Watch the bunker schools — as long as they hold thick through the Gut and the Sluiceway, the blues will keep working them over on top. And keep an epoxy jig rigged regardless of what you're targeting, because if the bonito show up on this stretch the way the water's suggesting they might, you won't want to be scrambling for the right lure when the first school comes ripping through Plum Gut on a moving tide.
