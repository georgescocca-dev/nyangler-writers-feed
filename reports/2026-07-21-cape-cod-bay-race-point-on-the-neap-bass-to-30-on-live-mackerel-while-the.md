# Race Point on the neap: bass to 30 on live mackerel while the rip goes quiet
*The tide slowed down this week and so did we — but the bass and the mackerel didn't mind one bit.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — July 21** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

Tuesday morning, half past five, I had the engine cut off Race Point and we were just drifting — no push from the current, no chop, just the boat easing along like it was on rails. My sternman Pauly leaned over the rail and said, "Donny, this don't feel like the Race." And he was right. We're coming off a first-quarter neap, and out here that means the rip that usually snaps your line taut and makes you brace against the gunwale was barely moving. Some guys hate neap water. I don't mind it. It spreads the bite out instead of cramming it into twenty minutes of slack, and this week that's exactly what it did.

We marked a thick pod of mackerel stacked about eighteen feet down on the sounder, right where the bar drops off into deeper water north of the Point, and we started jigging a few for the well. Didn't take long — small hooks, no weight, and they came over the rail two and three at a time. Once we had eight or ten flopping in the bucket, we rigged them live on 5/0 circle hooks, balloon set about twelve feet under a slip float, and drifted them back over that same edge. First fish came within ten minutes — low twenties, nothing to write home about, but a keeper is a keeper. Then around 6:15 the drag started peeling and Pauly's rod bent into something with real shoulders. Twenty minutes later we had a thirty-one pounder in the net, fat, chrome, full of herring. That's been the pattern all week off Race Point and down along Peaked Hill Bars — not a blitz, but a steady, honest bite on live mackerel and menhaden chunks, best in that first hour of light and again as the sun's dropping, though I'll say the Bay's cooler water has kept a few fish willing to eat through the middle of the day too, which is more than I can say for the beaches south of the Cape right now where the bass have gone strictly nocturnal in that warm water.

Billingsgate has been the story for anybody wanting bottom fish. We've been running out there in 35 to 55 feet, working the edges of the shoal where the bottom breaks up into rubble and mussel bed, and the haddock have cooperated better than I expected for late July — fish from 17 inches up to a nice 6-pounder on diamond jigs tipped with a strip of squid, worked with a slow lift-drop right off bottom. Cod have been mixed in but scattered, mostly smaller fish, and you have to hunt for them a bit more than the haddock. If you're drifting Billingsgate, keep an eye on your speed — anything over half a knot and you're dragging your jig sideways instead of working it. Neap tide again works in your favor here; slower drift means more time in the strike zone.

Down toward Sandy Neck and into the Barnstable flats, fluke fishing has held up about as well as anywhere on the whole coast this summer. We've been drifting the channel edges off the Neck in 15 to 25 feet, dragging a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a half-ounce bucktail, tipped with a sliver of squid for insurance, and picking through a lot of shorts to find keepers in the 18 to 22 inch range. Best water has been the last two hours of the incoming, when clean water pushes across the flats and pulls bait with it. Nothing dramatic, but steady — the kind of fishing where if you put in three or four hours you'll have fish for dinner.

Bluefish have shown up behind the bunker schools that have been working their way along the beach from Barnstable up toward the Neck — we flagged this pattern a couple weeks back, said the blues would follow the bunker up the coast, and sure enough they're here now, chopping through pods in the wash close to shore, visible some mornings right off the beach in Sandy Neck's surf line. Nothing like the blitzes they're seeing further south, but enough that if you see the bunker balled up and nervous, it's worth a few casts with a metal lip swimmer or a topwater popper before you move on.

Looking ahead, the neap tide's going to keep softening through the weekend before the current starts building back toward the next full moon push. That's going to keep widening the bite windows at Race Point and Peaked Hill — good news if you're working live bait and don't want to be a slave to a thirty-minute slack. I'd focus mornings on the Race with mackerel, midday on Billingsgate for haddock if the wind stays manageable out of the southwest, and save the last light for a pass along Sandy Neck watching for bunker and blues working the surf. If the wind swings northeast and kicks up any real sea, Billingsgate gets ugly fast in that shallower water, so keep an eye on the forecast before you commit to a long run out there. Otherwise, it's been an honest week on the Bay — nothing explosive, but steady enough that anybody willing to put in the drift time is coming home with fish.
