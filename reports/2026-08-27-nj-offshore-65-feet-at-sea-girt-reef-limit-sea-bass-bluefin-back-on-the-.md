# 65 Feet at Sea Girt Reef: Limit Sea Bass, Bluefin Back on the Barnegat Ridge Lumps
*The reef wrecks are stacked with keeper sea bass while a warm eddy pushes tuna and squid up onto the Klondike and Barnegat Ridge.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — August 27** — _by Capt. Rey Santos, Zone Writer_

Sixty-five feet of water over Sea Girt Reef, water temp holding better than 71 degrees on the meter — that's the number that's mattered most this week. Sea bass season is open in New Jersey through the end of the month, and the reef network from Shrewsbury Rocks down through Axel Carlson has been paying off for anyone willing to work bottom instead of running past it looking for something flashier offshore.

High pressure has parked over the region for the better part of ten days, southwest wind in the 8 to 12 knot range most afternoons, and that's kept the reef boats fishing clean, slick-calm water on structure that gets ugly fast when the wind swings onshore. That stability is the whole story right now. Bottom temps on the reef sites are steady, bait is holding tight to the wrecks instead of getting pushed around, and the sea bass have settled into a predictable pattern — tight to structure on the up-current side, feeding hard on the first two hours of a moving tide and going quiet in slack water.

Sea Girt Reef and Axel Carlson Reef, both sitting in that 55- to 70-foot band, have been the most consistent producers in the fleet reports coming back to the dock. Green crab and clam on standard sea bass rigs are taking the numbers, but the better fish — the 15- to 18-inch class that make a trip worth the gas — are coming on 4- to 6-ounce diamond jigs tipped with squid strips, worked with a short, sharp jig stroke a few feet off bottom. Shrewsbury Rocks has been holding a similar grade of fish in slightly shallower water, 40 to 55 feet, and it's the better call on a sloppier day since it sits tighter to the beach.

Triple Wrecks, deeper and further out, has been the spot for size. That structure holds up better in a bit of leftover swell, and the sea bass running out there have been noticeably bigger — book-worthy fish in the 3- to 4-pound class mixed into the limits. It's not a fast bite the way the shallower reefs can be, but it's a quality bite, and worth the extra run if the forecast holds. Blackfish won't be legal again until the fall opener, so leave the crabs in the bucket for tog and stick to the sea bass program on these wrecks for now.

The bigger story this week has been offshore of the reef line. The warm eddy that's been sitting south of us and holding clean blue water has finally pushed up onto the shelf the way we flagged it would — we said last week that eddy water working the canyon edges would eventually spill onto the inshore structure and trigger a bite, and that's exactly what's showing up on the Klondike and Barnegat Ridge lumps right now. Bluefin have been working both areas, and there's been enough yellowfin mixed in to keep the trolling spreads busy on the better mornings. Ballyhoo behind cedar plugs and small Spanish flags have both scored, along with the 180-gram vertical jig program on marks that show bait stacked tight to the bottom structure.

What's driving it is squid. Jumbo squid have shown up inshore of the ridges this week, a classic late-August signal that the warm-water forage base has hit its peak and is starting to push shoreward ahead of the first real cool-down. Where the squid balls are thick on the Klondike lumps, the tuna have been sitting underneath them, and that's produced some of the better jigging windows — bait marks stacked from 80 feet down to bottom, tuna slashing through on the sonar, and a short window before the school moves off the structure. It's not a wide-open bite. Boats working the lumps have connected in bursts rather than steady action, so plan on covering water and being ready to drop fast when the marks show up rather than sitting on one spot all day.

Mahi have been scattered around the same warm structure, holding on current edges and any floating debris near the ridge, with fish coming to small spreads and to bucktails dropped near the color changes. It's a bonus fish more than a target right now — nothing like the wide-open pot bite this pattern can produce later in the season, but worth a look if the spread's already out and the water color is right.

Looking ahead, the moon is building toward full over the next several days, which should sharpen the tidal swings on the reef sites and tighten up the bite windows — better to fish the first hour of a building tide than to grind through slack water hoping something turns on. The stable high pressure is forecast to hold through midweek, which should keep both the reef bite and the tuna window on the Klondike and Barnegat Ridge intact. If there's a change coming, it's later than that — early signs of cooler overnight air working into the region by the weekend, which won't touch these bottom temps yet but is worth watching as the calendar turns toward September. For now, the play is simple: reef wrecks for a solid box of sea bass, and the ridge lumps for tuna if the squid stay put and you're willing to run and watch the meter instead of parking on hope.
