# September 14 and Washington's Edge Still Runs Warm While the Rest of the Coast Cools
*The canyon's holding its late-summer temperature break into mid-September, and the fleet is still finding yellowfin, mahi, and the first real bigeye of the fall on it.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON, NY — September 14** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Second week of September and Washington is doing what Washington does this time of year — holding its temperature break longer than boats running the other canyons expect. That's the position advantage. Sitting where it sits relative to the Gulf Stream, this canyon tends to catch the warm water eddies a little earlier in June and hang onto them a little later in September than the canyons to the north. This week that pattern is playing out again. The east wall and the northeast edge are still carrying a clean, sharp temperature break, and as long as that filament holds its lane, the bite here should keep tracking the same warm-water signature that's kept the string of canyons up the coast productive all summer. We flagged this last week and the week before — said the eddy stability that carried this stretch through July and August would keep this zone in the game into September, and that's exactly the pattern that's showing up on the water right now.

The bite itself is honest work, not a blowout. Yellowfin remain the most dependable player out here, holding on the color change where the canyon's warm side meets the cooler shelf water pushing off the edge. Trolling spreads of small skirted ballyhoo and green machines, staggered off the shotgun and long rigger, are picking up fish in the 20 to 60-pound class on the troll, mostly in that 100 to 250-fathom stretch where the wall starts to drop away hard. Boats working the 39°30' to 39°45' line along the northeast edge have had the most consistent action, running the troll early and switching to the chunk once the sun gets high and the fish go quiet on the surface. Butterfish chunks fished on circle hooks, light fluorocarbon leader, and just enough weight to get down through the top 60 feet have kept rods bent through the slower midday stretch.

Bigeye are the story worth watching. Overnight chunk drops on the deeper structure, working bait down past 300 feet after dark, have started putting bigeye in the box — not in numbers yet, but enough that boats are staying out past sundown on purpose instead of by accident. That's a real fall pattern starting to assert itself a few weeks early, and it's worth noting because bigeye showing up this soon usually means the water column out here is stacked the way we like it, with a strong thermocline holding bait tight instead of scattered.

Bluefin have been the disappointment of the week — scattered, not schooled, showing up as incidental fish on the yellowfin troll rather than a dedicated push. Nothing wrong with the water for them, they're just not committing to any one piece of structure right now. If that changes, it usually shows up first as smaller single fish crashing bait pods on the surface before the bigger schools follow, so it's worth keeping an eye peeled on the troll rather than writing them off.

Mahi have thinned out from where they were a month ago, which tracks with the calendar — the big grass and debris lines that held them through July and August are breaking up as the season turns, and what's left are smaller pods holding tight to floating structure and lobster gear. Anyone marking a temperature break with any junk floating on it should slow down and make a pass with a bucktail or a small spreader bar before committing to the troll pattern elsewhere. White marlin have been present but winding down, which is normal for mid-September — a few fish still coming on the naked ballyhoo skipped behind the spread, mostly on the morning troll before the wind builds. Wahoo haven't shown in real numbers yet, but the conditions that usually bring them — a stable warm filament holding position into fall — are exactly what's in place right now, so a high-speed wahoo bomb dropped down deep in that 15 to 20-knot window on the way out or back is not wasted time.

Looking at the next several days, the call is straightforward: if the filament holds its position through the weekend, this canyon should keep tracking the same pattern it's carried since midsummer — steady yellowfin on the troll and chunk, a developing bigeye window after dark, mahi around anything floating, and bluefin as a bonus rather than a target. If the eddy starts to slide or break down, the first sign will likely show up as a slowdown on the mahi and marlin before it touches the yellowfin bite, so that's the piece to watch. Weather windows are getting shorter as the calendar turns toward fall, so anyone planning a run should pick their day off a settled forecast rather than forcing it — the fish aren't going anywhere fast, but the ocean out here gets less forgiving by the week from here through October.
