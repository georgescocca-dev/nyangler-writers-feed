# 180 feet of mud off the Ledge is stacked with haddock; the gravel's holding shorts
*Bluefin sign is building on the north end of Jeffrey's while the bottom bite settles into a steady, honest pattern.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 10** — _by Capt. Sonya Nguyen, Zone Writer_

One-eighty feet, soft mud, marks stacked eight to twelve feet off bottom on the sounder — that's the read on the west side of Jeffrey's Ledge right now, and it's been the most consistent thing I've run this month. Haddock don't care much about the calendar once they find a mud flat with sand eels working through it, and that's exactly what we've got between the 42°N line and the deeper basin edge north of Tillies. Clam bellies and squid strips on standard haddock rigs, two hooks, six ounces of lead to hold in the current, and you're not waiting long between bites. Average fish this week has been 20 to 24 inches, with enough 3- and 4-pound fish mixed in to make the bushel baskets heavy by noon.

The gravel is a different story, and it's the honest part of this report. Cod on the hard bottom — the classic humps and boulder edges on the east side of the Ledge, 130 to 160 feet — have been there, but they've been small. Plenty of 16 to 19 inch fish coming over the rail, a few keepers pushing 24, 25 inches mixed in, but nothing like the slow, heavy cod bite we want to see building into fall. I fished three trips out to the gravel this week and connected on legal cod every time, but it took working four or five different pieces of structure to put five keepers in the box. Jigs — the diamond jigs and the newer glow-tipped teasers — are outfishing bait on the gravel right now, probably because there's enough bait activity up in the water column that the fish aren't glued to the bottom the way they are in deep winter. If you're going after cod specifically, fish the up-current side of the humps on the drift, let the jig fall all the way to bottom, and work it up ten or fifteen feet before resetting. Don't waste time on a piece that doesn't show fish in the first two drifts — move.

Pollock have been a nice bonus on both bottom types, hitting jigs meant for cod up higher in the water column, sometimes 40 or 50 feet off bottom over structure. If you mark a school suspended mid-water on the sounder near the gravel edges, that's pollock, and they'll hit a bucktail or a diamond jig worked with a fast erratic retrieve better than anything worked slow.

Now the part I know everybody's asking about — the tuna. We're seeing bluefin sign build on the north end of Jeffrey's and up around the Stellwagen approaches, which is right on schedule for mid-August. Bait is the key here: mackerel schools have been thick on the surface in patches, and where you find the mackerel balled up and nervous, that's where the bluefin are working. I haven't seen the giant class fish yet — that's an October pattern once the herring and mackerel really concentrate along the ledge edge — but we're getting reports of school-size fish, 60 to 100 pounds, working bait on top early in the morning and again toward evening. Green stick and chunk baits both have accounted for fish this week, chunked mackerel drifted back on circle hooks in a slick, no weight, letting it settle naturally with the tide. If you're set up for it, keep the spread light and quiet — these fish have been touchy, showing on top for ten or fifteen minutes and then disappearing, which tells me they're moving through rather than holding tight to one piece of structure yet.

Hake have shown up in the deeper basin water, Scantum Basin especially, on the same mud that's holding haddock but usually a little deeper, 200 feet plus. If you're marking soft returns low to bottom and the haddock bite goes quiet for a stretch, drop a bait down and let it sit — hake will often save what would otherwise be a slow hour.

Wind's been manageable this week, nothing that's kept me off the Ledge, and the tide has been running with enough push on the mud to keep bait moving and haddock feeding through most of the day rather than just at the change. That's the kind of steady current pattern that makes the mud bite reliable rather than a two-hour window you have to hit exactly right.

Looking ahead, I expect the haddock bite on the mud to hold through the week — that pattern doesn't shift fast once it's established, and there's no reason to think the sand eels are clearing out anytime soon. The gravel cod should improve some as water starts to cool into September, but right now I'd plan a trip around the mud if numbers matter more to you than size. For the tuna, if the mackerel schools stay put on the north end through the weekend, that's where I'd spend a morning — but I'd have groundfish gear rigged as a backup, because that bluefin sign has been here-today-gone-tomorrow more than it's been a sure thing. Worth the run out if you can get an early start and the wind stays under 15.
