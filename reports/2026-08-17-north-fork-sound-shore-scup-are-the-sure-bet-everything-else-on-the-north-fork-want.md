# Scup Are the Sure Bet; Everything Else on the North Fork Wants Convincing
*Porgies are stacked over the rock piles from Mattituck to Orient while bass and fluke make you work mornings only.*

**MATTITUCK, NY — August 17** — _by Pat "North Fork" Reilly, Zone Writer_

There was a kid down at the inlet Tuesday morning, maybe twenty-two, twenty-three, throwing a topwater plug that cost more than my first outboard motor. Worked it beautiful too — walked it right along the current seam where the inlet dumps into the Sound, the kind of retrieve you'd teach in a video. Nothing touched it. I was twenty feet away soaking a clam belly on a simple hi-lo rig, not even paying attention, and I had two porgies and a blowfish before he'd finished his third cast. He looked over at me like I'd cheated somehow. I hadn't. I'd just been fishing this water since before his father was born, and some years the Sound doesn't care what your lure costs.

That's about the size of it out here right now. If you want a guaranteed bend in the rod, it's porgy and scup — they've moved up onto the structure the way they always do in mid-August, and they're not shy about it. Rocky Point reef, the rock pile off Horton Point, the boulder fields tucked in around Orient — anywhere there's broken bottom in fifteen to thirty feet, drop a hi-lo rig with squid strips or clam belly and you'll be busy. Small hooks, size 6 or 8, because these fish have particular mouths and they'll strip a big hook clean without you ever feeling it. This has become the honest bite on my stretch of water. Not glamorous. Reliable.

Striped bass are a different conversation. The water's warmed up enough — Kings Point over on the west end of the Sound was reading close to 71, and even out toward New London it's holding around 69 — that the bass have gone and gotten sensible on us. Sensible meaning they feed early, before the sun really gets up on the water, and then they slide off into whatever deeper current edge they can find and sulk through the afternoon. I've had my best luck at first light off Horton Point, working the edge where the current sets against the point on the last of the outgoing, using bucktails in the half-ounce to three-quarter range, white or chartreuse, tipped with a strip of squid. By nine, nine-thirty, it shuts off like somebody threw a switch. That's not a complaint, that's just how August works on this shore. Plan around it or don't bother.

We said last week the bass would hold through this tide cycle as bait kept moving through on the current, and that's exactly what's happened — the fish are here, they're just disciplined about when they'll eat.

Bluefish are the story I'd watch closest going into the back half of the month. The creeks and coves from Mattituck down toward the Gut are thick with snapper blues right now, which anybody who's fished this coast a while knows is the tell — those snappers are next year's adults, but some years the bigger cousins show up fast on their heels, and I've started seeing exactly that. Had a report of slammers working bait pushed up against the rocks near Rocky Point earlier this week, chopping through peanut bunker with that nervous, boiling surface disturbance you can spot from fifty yards off. We flagged this — said the bluefish resurgence would spread east along the North Fork as bait redistributed with the tide, and here it is, right on schedule. If you find working birds anywhere between Rocky Point and the Plum Gut approach, don't overthink it — a metal jig or a bucktail thrown into the mess will get bit fast, before they push through and disappear.

Fluke, I'll be straight with you, have been mostly frustrating. Plenty of them around the deeper channel edges off Mattituck Inlet and drifting the flats east toward Horton Point, but the water's sitting in the mid-70s and that's put most of the keeper-sized fish into a lazy, uncooperative mood, while the sound stays full of shorts that'll steal your Gulp before a legal fish ever sees it. If you're set on fluke, fish the deeper water on the last of the ebb when there's still some cool water moving, white Gulp swimming mullet on a three-quarter ounce bucktail, dragged dead slow. You'll cull through some shorts to find a keeper, but they're there.

Weakfish have been scarce, which is nothing new for this stretch the last several seasons — a few showing mixed in with the porgy catches on clam, nothing to build a trip around. Blackfish season's not open yet on this end, but I've seen a couple of boats out scouting the structure around Plum Gut approach already, marking rock piles on the machine ahead of the fall push. Good habit. That water gets crowded once the season opens and you want your spots dialed in now.

Plum Gut itself has been running hard on the bigger tides, current ripping through there enough to make boat control the real challenge, more than finding fish. Bass and blues both use that current as a highway, and the edges where the rip meets slower water off Orient have been worth a drift if you can handle the boat traffic funneling through.

Looking ahead — we're building toward that full moon at the end of the month, and bigger tides usually mean more water moving and more bait getting stirred up along this whole stretch. If that holds, I'd expect the bluefish push to keep building east and maybe pull some bigger bass up onto the structure with it. I'd fish the early tide, watch for birds working anywhere from Rocky Point to the Gut, and not waste too much daylight chasing fluke that don't want to be caught. Bring patience and bring clams — the porgies, at least, won't let you down.
