# White Island cod are deep and stingy, but the Piscataqua stripers aren't
*Thirteen miles of coast doesn't sound like much until you fish it like it's a hundred and thirty — right now that means less time on the Shoals and more time on the river.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — August 7** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

People hear thirteen miles of coastline and figure there's nothing here worth the trailer time. I've never fished it that way. Thirteen miles with the Isles of Shoals sitting six miles off the beach, a river mouth that moves like a fire hose, and groundfish structure that most of Massachusetts would kill for is plenty of water if you know which six feet of it to be sitting on at the right tide. Right now, in the first week of August, that means the Piscataqua more than the Shoals, and it means fishing early or not at all.

The groundfish bite out at the Isles has gone the way it always goes this time of year — tighter and deeper. Cod and haddock aren't gone from the structure around White Island and Appledore, but the fish that were sitting up on the broken ground in fifty and sixty feet in June have slid down into the colder water on the back side, closer to seventy and eighty feet, and they're not as eager to chase a jig as they were in the spring. Diamond jigs and teasers still work, but the retrieve needs to slow down — short lifts off bottom, let it fall on a controlled drop, and don't be in a hurry to reel through the water column. Haddock have been the more consistent draw of the two, showing on clam bellies and small jigs fished right on the bottom on the slower half of the tide, when the current isn't ripping the boat off the mark. Cod are there but you're earning every one — this isn't a limit trip, it's a few solid fish for the patient boat that stays on top of the structure instead of drifting off it after two casts.

The better story this week is the river. The Piscataqua doesn't care what month it is — that current runs hard regardless, and right now stripers are using it exactly the way they always do in high summer, stacking on the current breaks below the bridges and around the eddies off Pierce Island and Peirce Island Bridge on the last two hours of the incoming and the first hour of the outgoing. Live mackerel and eels fished on a fish-finder rig, dropped right into the seam where fast water meets slow, have been the more dependable producers than plugs, though topwater has had its moments early, right at first light before the boat traffic starts. Bluefish have been mixed into that same current structure, chopping up anything that looks like a peanut bunker, and they've made a mess of a few rigged eels meant for bass. Not a complaint — a blue on light tackle in that current is its own kind of fun, it just means retying more often than you'd like.

Hampton Harbor has been doing what it does on a summer flood — stripers pushing up through the inlet chasing bait as the tide fills the marsh, then sliding back out as it drains. The channel edges off Great Boars Head and the deeper cuts inside the harbor mouth have given up schoolie-to-mid-slot bass on soft plastics, five-inch white or bone paddletails on light jig heads, worked slow along the bottom on the outgoing when the bait gets funneled out with the current. Nothing explosive, but steady enough that it's worth the trip if you're working the tide correctly and not just showing up whenever.

Mackerel have thinned out some from where they were stacked in June, but there are still pockets holding around the Shoals and off Rye Harbor, enough to jig up a bucket for bait without much trouble if you find the right piece of structure and the birds are working. Flounder have been quiet but present in Hampton Harbor and up in Rye Harbor on sandworms and clam, fished on the bottom on the slower stages of the tide — not a bite to plan a whole day around, but a fine way to fill a cooler while you wait for the tide to turn on something better.

We flagged this pattern a couple weeks back — said to expect a steady seasonal push through the stable stretch of summer weather, and that's more or less what's played out. Nothing exploded, nothing collapsed. The Shoals groundfish bite softened the way it always does in August heat, and the inshore stripers and blues picked up the slack right where the current does the work for you.

Looking ahead, I'd keep the groundfish trips shorter and earlier — get out to the Shoals before the wind builds and be back working the deeper piece of structure while the tide's still moving. For stripers, the river and Hampton Harbor are the better bet through the coming week if the weather holds stable, working the last of the incoming and first of the outgoing on eels and paddletails. If a front comes through and stirs up the harbor, I'd shift straight to the Piscataqua — that current clears itself out faster than anywhere else on this coast, and it doesn't care what the sky is doing.
