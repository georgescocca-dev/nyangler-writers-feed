# Double Creek fluke stack the drop-offs while the sedges finally give up weakfish again
*Warm bay water has fluke tight to the channel edges and put the weakfish bite back on schedule — bluefish are riding bunker right through the inlet on the flood.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — July 21** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

Back when I was mating on the Point Pleasant boats — I couldn't have been more than sixteen — there was an old captain named Sal who used to say the bay teaches you twice: once when the water's cold and once when it's warm, and if you only learn one lesson you're only half a fisherman. I think about that every July when Barnegat Bay goes soft and green and everybody assumes the good fishing packed up with the mayflies. It didn't. It just moved to the edges.

That's exactly where I found the fluke this week — tight to the drop-offs along Double Creek Channel, right where the mud flats fall off into eight, ten, twelve feet of water. The flats themselves are running warm, mid-70s by feel, and that pushes the fluke off the open bottom and down onto the lip of the channel where there's a little more oxygen and a little less current chop. I worked the outgoing tide Tuesday and again Thursday morning, drifting from the Route 9 bridge down toward the Forked River mouth with a half-ounce white bucktail tipped with a four-inch chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullet, bounced slow along bottom. Kept three keepers to four and a half pounds each day, threw back better than a dozen shorts. Nothing to write home about, but steady — and on a summer bay bite, steady is what you take.

The better news, honestly, is the weakfish. We said a couple weeks back that the evening bite would rebuild once the water settled into a calm, warm pattern, and that's exactly what's happened. The sedge banks south of Waretown and up around the mouth of Oyster Creek have started giving up weakfish again on the last hour of daylight into dark — one-to-three pound fish, mostly, with the occasional four hanging around the deeper pockets. Live grass shrimp under a popping cork is still the deadliest thing you can throw at them, but a three-inch pink Gulp shrimp on a light jighead worked just as well for me Wednesday evening, twitched slow along the edge where the sedge grass gives way to open water. Weakfish don't like a hard tide push, so target the slack around the tide change or the first hour of the new flood — that's when they come up off bottom and commit.

Bluefish are the loudest thing happening on the bay right now, and it's all bunker-driven. Big pods have been pushing through Barnegat Inlet on the higher stages of the tide, and the blues are riding right in behind them into the back bay. I've been anchoring just inside the inlet mouth, off the rock jetty on the Barnegat Light side, working the last two hours of the incoming with diamond jigs and metal — half-ounce Kastmasters, chrome or gold, worked fast through the bait. When the bunker get pinned against the rocks the blues go into a slashing frenzy you can see from fifty yards off, and for ten or fifteen minutes it's about as good as bay bluefishing gets. It doesn't last, though — once the tide turns the bait scatters and the blues follow it back out. Time it right or you'll miss the whole show.

Striped bass are around, but this warm shallow water has them doing what warm shallow water always makes them do — feeding low-light and after dark, then sulking on deeper structure through the heat of the day. I've had my best luck working bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig right at the inlet mouth in that first gray hour before sunup, and again for a short window right at dusk. During the day, if you want a shot at a bass, you're better off working the deeper holes off Barnegat Light with a bucktail dragged slow than casting the flats — the fish just aren't up there feeding when the sun's high and the water's this warm.

Don't overlook the smaller stuff either. Kingfish are showing on the ocean side along Island Beach State Park in the surf troughs, small but steady on bloodworm-tipped high-low rigs fished right in the wash. And Tices Shoal has turned on for blowfish the way it does every summer once the bay water settles — clam strips on a small hook, patience required, but a fun way to spend a slack tide with the kids if the fluke and weakfish aren't cooperating.

We're coming off the first-quarter neap now, which is softening the current through Double Creek and around the inlet — good news for anybody working slower presentations, because the bite windows widen out when the water isn't ripping. Expect that to hold for the next several days before the tides build back toward the full moon. My read for the coming week: the fluke pattern along the channel edges should hold steady as long as the bay stays warm and clear, the weakfish should keep improving into early August if these calm evenings continue, and the bluefish will keep riding the bunker in and out of the inlet as long as those pods keep pushing south. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd be at the inlet mouth at first light with bunker chunks out for bass, then switch over to bucktails on the last of the outgoing to chase fluke down into Double Creek before the sun gets too high.
