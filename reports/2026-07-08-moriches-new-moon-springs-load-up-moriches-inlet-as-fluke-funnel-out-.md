# New moon springs load up Moriches Inlet as fluke funnel out of an overheated bay
*Great South Bay pushing 79°F has fluke stacking in the inlet throat, while a closing bonito window and building tides set up a big week at Cupsogue.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — July 8** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

Last week the bay did what overheated bays do in early July — it pushed everything with fins toward cooler water. Great South Bay and the western reaches of Moriches Bay were sitting at 78.8°F by midweek, which is fine for snappers and bunker but starts to shut down the metabolism on fluke and push weakfish deep and lockjawed. That kind of heat doesn't just make fish uncomfortable, it stratifies the water column — warm skin layer up top, cooler water pinned against the bottom in the deeper channels. Fluke don't fight that fight. They ride the outgoing tide down the guts of Narrow Bay and the Forge River channel and stack up where the current funnels them: the inlet throat and the ocean side, where upwelled water sitting around 71°F is a straight upgrade.

This week the moon is doing the heavy lifting. We're building toward new moon, with peak spring tide alignment landing July 11-13. That means bigger tidal exchange through the inlet cut than we've seen in weeks — more water moving faster through a system that's already narrow and scoured out from the last few nor'easters. The west cut off Cupsogue has held its depth better than the main channel this season, running 15 to 18 feet through the throat before shoaling out onto the ebb bar. When the springs peak, that current lane sharpens, and so does the bite window around it. Wind's been manageable, mostly light out of the south and southwest, which keeps the ocean side clean enough to fish the surf without getting worked over by a sloppy groundswell.

The fluke bite is real right now, but it's not a bay bite anymore — it's an inlet-and-ocean bite, and that's an important distinction if you're planning a trip. I've been working the drift from the Coast Guard station down through the throat on the last two hours of outgoing, dragging 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 4-inch Gulp Swimming Mullets right along the bottom where the scour drops off. Fish are sitting in that 15 to 18 foot cut, using the deeper water as a highway between the bay and the ocean side sandbars. Nothing enormous — most fish going 17 to 20 inches — but there's enough volume that a two-hour outgoing drift is producing four or five keepers if you're covering ground and not anchoring up. On the ocean side, the same fish are pushing out onto the bar in that cooler upwelled water, and guys drifting bucktail-and-Gulp combos in 20 to 25 feet off Cupsogue are picking off a mix of fluke and the occasional short-to-keeper bass that's using the same current seam.

Stripers are still around Cupsogue and the west jetty, but they're playing the tide stages, not the clock. Best window has been the last hour of the ebb into the start of the flood, right at the jetty rocks where the current compresses and bait gets pinned. Bucktails with white or chartreuse teasers are working, and a few guys throwing bunker chunks off the jetty on fishfinder rigs have connected with fish in the mid-20s. It's not a blitz — you need to be there at the right tide stage, not just fishing the whole day and hoping. Bluefish have been mixed in on the same structure, mostly cocktail-sized, chopping bunker pieces and hitting metal on the outgoing.

Here's the piece worth watching closely: the pelagic window that opened around the Fourth is closing out over the next few days, and Moriches sits right in the zone for it. First bonito and Spanish mackerel reports are trickling in from the ocean side of the eastern inlets, and with clean incoming water pushing that upwelled 71°F water against the beach, this stretch — Moriches and Shinnecock ocean side — is exactly where I'd expect the first real showing here. If you've got light tackle and some small metal or epoxy jigs, the next three or four days on an incoming tide at first light off Cupsogue or the Smith Point bar is worth the gas money. This window doesn't stay open long, and once it closes, you're waiting for a second wave later in the summer that isn't guaranteed to show at all.

Weakfish, I'll be straight with you — nothing worth reporting. The bay's been above 70°F for over two weeks now, which used to be the trigger everybody watched, but it's not producing. I've had zero solid weakfish reports out of this zone, and I'm hearing the same from other bay systems. Temperature alone isn't doing it this year; there's something else missing, probably bait density or an absent year class. I wouldn't burn a tide targeting them right now.

Looking ahead: the new moon springs peaking mid-week should sharpen the inlet drift for fluke and give the striper bite at the jetty a stronger push on the tide changes. If the wind stays out of the south and the ocean stays clean, I'd spend my mornings on the incoming working bonito metal off Cupsogue, then shift to the fluke drift through the throat as the tide turns. Plan B if the pelagics don't show — there's always fluke stacked in that scour hole, and that bite isn't going anywhere as long as the bay stays this warm.
