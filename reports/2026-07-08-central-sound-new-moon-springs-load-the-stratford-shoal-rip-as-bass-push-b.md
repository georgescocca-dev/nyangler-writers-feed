# New-moon springs load the Stratford Shoal rip as bass push back on the moving water
*Bluefish crash bunker off Charles Island, fluke slide into the deep guts, and the first evening bite since June's full moon is set to reopen this weekend.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 8** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound sat in that mid-summer lull where the wind never really commits — light sou'westerlies most afternoons, glassy mornings, water building heat every day it wasn't stirred. That kind of pattern spreads bait thin. Peanut bunker and silversides scattered into every harbor mouth from Oyster Bay to Port Jeff instead of stacking anywhere obvious, and the bass and blues followed suit — around, but not concentrated, not predictable. The moon was working against us too. We're coming off the June full moon and sliding through the neap-to-building stretch, current softening at the rips, fish spreading into eddies instead of holding tight lanes. That's the kind of week where you have to go find them instead of having them come to you.

That changes fast. We're running into a new moon around July 13, and the spring tides that come with it are already building — bigger swings at Kings Point and up through the Norwalk Islands, more water moving through the pinch points that make this stretch of Sound what it is. The forecast I like most: the PM flood tide is converging with sunset from Friday the 10th through Monday the 13th, which is the first evening high-water window we've had line up with dusk since before the last full moon. When that flood stacks against the last hour of light, bait gets pinned against structure right when predators want to feed on it, and the whole middle Sound corridor — Stratford Shoal, Middle Ground, the Norwalk chain — narrows down the options for where the bass have to be. Wind looks manageable through the weekend, nothing that should blow the shoal rip apart. If it holds, this is the setup I've been waiting on.

Striped bass are still the most honest read on the week. The Stratford Shoal rip has been giving up fish on the outgoing, right at the top of the drop where the current pours off the shoal into deeper water — 20 to 35 feet, bouncing bucktails tipped with a strip of fresh bunker, working the seam where the sandy bottom breaks toward the channel. Nothing enormous, mostly schoolies to low-20-inch fish with a few better ones mixed in, but consistent enough that three or four boats a morning are camped on it during the last two hours of the outgoing. Trolling umbrella rigs through the same zone has worked too if you're not into jigging — keep it slow, right on the edge where the water color changes.

Bluefish have been the more reliable violence this week. Charles Island and the stretch of beach running toward the Norwalk Islands have had genuine peanut bunker showers at first light, gulls working tight, blues busting through from underneath hard enough to put spray in the air. Poppers and metal — Kastmasters, Ava jigs in white — have been getting hit as fast as you can cast into the melee. It doesn't last long, usually thirty to forty-five minutes before the bait scatters and the blues sound, so if you're running out from Norwalk or Westport, be on the water before sunrise, not after.

Fluke have been the trickier story. Bay and harbor water has been running warm enough that the better fish have been sliding out of the shallow flats and into the deeper guts — the channel edges off Middle Ground Light and the drop-offs around the mouth of Port Jefferson Harbor have both outfished the flats proper. Five-inch white or pink Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow through 25 to 40 feet on the last of the outgoing, have been the ticket. It's not a banner fluke week — I'd call it steady with occasional keepers, not a slam-dunk — but the fish that are biting are quality, several pushing 4 to 5 pounds off the deeper structure.

Porgies remain the dependable bread-and-butter around the Norwalk Islands reefs and the rockpiles off Middle Ground — small hooks, sandworms or clam, anchored up on the last of the drop or the first of the push, and you can fill a cooler without much drama. Simple fishing, but it works, and it's a good call for a slower tide stage when the bass and blues aren't cooperating. Blackfish season is closed through the summer in both states right now, so leave the crab pots in the garage until fall opens back up.

Weakfish, I have to be straight with you, have been a non-event. Water's plenty warm enough on paper, but warm water alone hasn't been translating to fish in the net anywhere I've heard of this year, and that's held true again this week. If you're targeting them specifically, I'd shift that effort to the bass and blues instead — better odds for your gas money right now.

Looking at the bigger picture, the bonito corridor down around Block Island and Newport has been widening as that cold wall broke down, and history says that push eventually walks west along the Sound's eastern approaches by August. Too early to chase them here yet, but it's on my radar for late month. For this coming week, my time is going into that Friday-through-Monday evening flood window at Stratford Shoal and the Norwalk Islands. If the wind stays cooperative and that new-moon spring tide does what it should, sunset on the outgoing-to-incoming transition ought to be the best few hours we've had in a month. Plan B if the bass don't show is the same deep-water fluke drift off Middle Ground — that bite's been honest even when nothing else is.
