# The East Bar at Cupsogue Built Out 20 Yards — And the Fluke Followed the New Edge
*Rough ocean and a shifting inlet geometry pushed the bite inside — here's where the channel structure is holding fish this week.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — August 7** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

I stood on the jetty rocks at Cupsogue on a low tide Tuesday and watched a hump of sand break the swell that wasn't there three weeks ago. That's the east bar, and it's built out a solid twenty yards since the last blow pushed through in July. This inlet never sits still — every summer it reshapes itself under steady southwest wind and moderate surf, and this year's no different. What matters for us is what that new bar geometry does to the current: it's tightening the ebb flow through the main channel, speeding it up on the last two hours of outgoing, and that's exactly where the fluke have set up shop.

Offshore, the ocean's been a mess — NY Bight buoy readings had seas around 3.6 feet at a short six-second period most of this week, which is choppy enough to keep the surf fishing at Cupsogue's ocean side inconsistent and push bait and predators into the more protected inlet channel and back bay. Water out there is sitting around 76 degrees, plenty warm, but that short-period slop doesn't give bass or bluefish a clean feeding lane along the open beach. So the fish have moved to where the structure does the work for them — current edges, channel drop-offs, the deeper cuts behind the bars.

Fluke have been the most dependable read all week. The best fish are coming off the channel edge on the bay side of the inlet, right where that new bar drops into eight to twelve feet of water. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on ¾-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing tide, have been picking up keepers to five pounds. A few guys working the deeper hole off Pattersquash Island with squid strips tipped on bucktails have done even better — that hole holds current on both stages of the tide and it's been quietly one of the better producers in the whole system this month. Don't sleep on it just because it doesn't get talked about as much as the inlet itself.

Bluefish have been the more reliable action for anyone who just wants bent rods. Snapper blues are thick in the back bay creeks off East Moriches and Mastic — small metal and Kastmasters worked fast under diving terns will get you all the light-tackle action you want for an evening. The adult blues are stacking closer to the inlet mouth, chasing bunker schools that have been pushing through on the last of the incoming. If you can find the school — and this week they've been showing up off Tuthill Point in the last hour of the flood — bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig or a bunker spoon trolled slow through the pod will get you into eight- to twelve-pound blues without much trouble.

Weakfish have been mixed in on those same deeper holes, but they're not thick. A few decent fish, two to four pounds, have come off the channel structure near the Coast Guard station on soft plastics fished slow along bottom during the tide change — that dead window right at the top or bottom when current goes still for twenty minutes is when they've shown best. It's not a bite you can plan a whole trip around yet, but worth a few casts if you're already out there working fluke.

We flagged this two weeks back — said to watch for the first peanut bunker showing up in the back bay, and marked the Aug 11-13 springs as the window that current over the new bar geometry should turn into the best bass stretch of the month. The peanuts have started showing along the grass edges off the East Moriches flats this week, small and scattered but there. That's the signal. Bluefish will track that bait expansion fast — expect the cocktail blue action in the back bay creeks to spike hard over the next ten days as those peanut schools grow and spread toward the inlet mouth.

Striped bass have been the quiet story so far — mostly a nighttime bite, working the deeper cuts on the outgoing with bunker chunks or eels, and mostly schoolie-sized fish in the 24- to 28-inch range around the inlet structure. That's typical for early August with surface water sitting in the mid-70s; the bigger fish are holding deep and feeding after dark. But if that peanut bunker keeps building toward the new moon springs on the 11th through 13th, the hard current running over that reshaped bar should concentrate bait right where the bass want to feed — and that's the window I'd circle if I only had one shot at a decent striper this month.

Porgies have been a steady, unglamorous producer for anyone anchoring up on the hard bottom inside the inlet — clam or squid on a hi-lo rig, nothing fancy, and you'll fill a cooler in an afternoon. Blackfish are still a few weeks off from their real season but a handful have come off the rock piles near the jetty on green crab for patient anglers fishing slack tide.

If I had one day this week, I'd fish the last two hours of the outgoing at the Pattersquash hole for fluke, then swing over to Tuthill Point on the last hour of the flood to intercept those bunker-chasing blues. And I'd keep an eye on that bar off Cupsogue — inlets like this one don't hold still, and the next good blow could move that structure again before the springs even get here.
