# Double Creek Fluke to Four Pounds, Snapper Blues Thick in the Sedges Behind Tices
*Forty summers on this bay taught me August is a shallow-water game before nine and a deep-channel game after — this week proved it again.*

**FORKED RIVER, NJ — August 7** — _by Gus Papadakis, Zone Writer_

I remember an August like this one back in the early nineties, running the skiff out of Forked River before first light because my old mate Sonny told me the bay would cook by ten and we'd be wasting bait after that. He was right then and he's right now, forty summers later — this bay doesn't change its clock just because the tackle's better and the boats are faster. When the surface water sits in the mid-to-upper 70s like it has all week, everything that swims in here gets smart about when it feeds, and if you're not on the water by six you're fishing the leftovers.

That's exactly the pattern I've been working the past several days, and it's paid off, though not without some early frustration. The shallow flats and grass beds — Tices Shoal, the marsh edges behind Good Luck Point, the guts running into Oyster Creek — have been loaded with snapper blues since the bunker started pushing peanut-sized pods into the back bay. We flagged this coming a couple weeks back, that the cocktail blues would follow the peanut bunker in as it spread through the system, and that's exactly what's happened. Small blues, mostly cocktail-sized this week with a few pushing bigger, are hammering anything shiny worked fast through the grass on the early flood and the first two hours of the outgoing. Half-ounce Kastmasters, chrome or gold, and small Got-Cha plugs worked on light spinning gear will get bit as fast as you can cast, but only in that window before the sun really climbs. By nine-thirty the blues drop off the flats into deeper water and the bite goes dead until the evening tide starts moving again.

Weakfish are mixed in with that bunker push too, which is a little unusual for August — I mostly associate the tiderunners with May around the sedge banks, but the peanut bunker have drawn some weakies into the bay this month, holding tight to the deeper edges of the grass flats where the current keeps things a few degrees cooler. Nothing huge, mostly schoolie-sized fish in the fourteen to eighteen inch range, but they're there on the last of the incoming, working the drop-offs along the Route 37 bridge flats and the deeper cuts near Tices. Small bucktails tipped with a Fishbites strip or a sliver of squid, bounced slow along bottom, has been the ticket.

Fluke fishing is where the real consistency has been, and it's holding up because those fish have done what fluke always do in the dog days — they've abandoned the baking shallow flats for the deeper structure where the current keeps bottom temps workable. Double Creek Channel has been my go-to this week, drifting the deeper bends in twelve to eighteen feet on the last of the outgoing and the first hour of the incoming, when the current's still got some push to it but isn't ripping. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter ounce bucktails, tipped with a strip of squid for insurance, have produced keepers to four pounds, with a good number of shorts mixed in that you'll have to sort through. The inlet-adjacent water off Barnegat Light channel edges has also been holding fish, though that bite's been spottier — some days you load the box, other days you work three hours for two keepers. I wouldn't call it lights-out fluking, but it's steady enough to plan a trip around if you're willing to move around and find where the current's working for you.

Striped bass have gone quiet by day, which tracks with what I'd expect this time of year — with the bay running warm, those fish that are around have shifted to a nighttime pattern, holding on deeper structure and feeding after dark when the water cools a touch. I haven't put much effort into bass this week because the daytime bay isn't the place for them right now, but if you want to chase them, fish the channel edges near the inlet after sundown with bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig, and be patient.

Blowfish and kingfish have been a nice bonus on the bottom for anyone soaking bait in the deeper holes — Oyster Creek's warm outflow area has had a few blowfish mixed in on small hooks with squid strips, and kingfish have shown along the channel edges on sandworms fished on light spinning tackle. Nothing to build a whole trip around, but a welcome diversion between fluke drifts.

Looking ahead, if this stable weather pattern holds — light winds, no fronts pushing through — I expect the bunker-driven action to keep rolling through the coming week, with the same early-and-late rhythm holding true. The snapper blues and weakfish should stay tucked into the grass flats as long as that peanut bunker keeps working through the system, so I'd keep working Tices and the Oyster Creek flats at first light. For fluke, Double Creek Channel remains my pick, especially on that last hour of outgoing when the current's still moving but manageable. If you're only getting one trip in, I'd make it a dawn start — the bay rewards the early boat this time of year, same as it always has.
