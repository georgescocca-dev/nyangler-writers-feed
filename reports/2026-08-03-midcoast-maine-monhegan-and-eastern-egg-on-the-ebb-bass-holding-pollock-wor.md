# Monhegan and Eastern Egg on the ebb: bass holding, pollock working the top
*Groundfish still down deep in the bay channels, mackerel thick enough to jig your own bait, and the stripers want structure, not open water.*

**BOOTHBAY HARBOR, ME — August 3** — _by Rusty Pelletier, Zone Writer_

Forty-some years back, an old lobsterman named Cyrus Pratt told me something I never forgot: "The tide don't care what time your alarm went off." I was twenty-two, running my own skiff for the first time out past the Cuckolds, and I'd shown up two hours late on a dead slack thinking fish would wait for me. They didn't. Cyrus laughed about it for a decade. I think about that lesson every August when the water gets bright and lazy and guys want to fish whenever's convenient instead of when the tide says go.

This week's been a Cyrus week. The bite's here, but it's tide-dependent and it doesn't forgive a late start.

Stripers are holding tight to structure rather than roaming open water, which is normal for early August but still catches people off guard. Best fish have come off the last two hours of the ebb around the mouth of the Damariscotta River and along the ledges off Boothbay Harbor — 24 to 32 inches, a few pushing 34, taken on live mackerel and pogies fished on a fish-finder rig in 15 to 25 feet. If you're throwing hardware, a white Zoom Fluke on a 3/8-ounce jighead worked slow along the drop-offs near the Thread of Life has been drawing strikes right at first light, before the sun climbs high enough to push fish deeper. North of Cape Cod we still get the daytime bite the southern guys have lost to warm water — that's the one advantage of fishing cold. Don't waste it sleeping in.

Mackerel are running thick enough that you can fill a bait bucket in fifteen minutes off Ocean Point or around Monhegan's harbor mouth. Mackerel trees, no weight needed most mornings, schools right on the surface chasing bait. That's been the real engine behind the striper and bluefish action — plenty of fresh mac around means the bigger predators don't have to work hard, and neither do you if you use it right.

Bluefish have shown up in scattered pods around Eastern Egg Rock, mixed in with the puffins working the same bait balls. Nothing like the Jersey shore blitz you hear about from farther south, but enough 3- to 6-pound choppers on cut mackerel or a fast-retrieved Hopkins spoon to make a morning worthwhile. They're moving with the bait, not holding, so if you find birds diving, get there quick — that window can close in twenty minutes.

Pollock are the fish surprising people right now. Big schools working the surface off Monhegan's western ledges and around Eastern Egg Rock on the incoming tide, sometimes visible from a quarter mile off by the slick, nervous water. Sight-cast a chartreuse Slug-Go or a topwater popper into a boil and hold on — these aren't the harbor pollock you catch off the dock, some going 8 to 12 pounds. Best window's been an hour either side of the tide change, before the current gets ripping and pushes the bait too deep to chase on top.

Groundfish are still where they should be for this time of year — cod and haddock holding in the deeper channels of Penobscot Bay, particularly the water between Vinalhaven and Isle au Haut and out toward Matinicus, 100 to 160 feet, on structure you'd recognize if you've ever hauled a trawl through there. Diamond jigs tipped with a clam strip, worked on the bottom third of the water column on a strong ebb, have been the ticket. It's not the September run yet, but it's steady enough that a half-day trip out of Stonington or Rockland is worth the fuel if you're patient and willing to move around until you mark fish.

We flagged this last week — said the pattern would hold stable through the first days of August, and that's exactly what's played out. No thermal surprises, no big shift in where the bait's sitting. The Gulf of Maine stays cold enough that these fish don't need to run for deep water like the stripers south of the Cape do; they just settle into their usual seasonal addresses and wait for the tide to bring the groceries.

I won't pretend it's been easy fishing. Plenty of boats went out this week and came back with less than they hoped, especially midday when the sun's high and the water goes slick and dead. The fish that are biting are biting hard on the moving water and shutting off almost completely on the slack. That's worth knowing before you plan your day around lunch instead of the tide chart.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward new phase over the next week and a half, which should tighten up the currents around Monhegan and the Thorofare and sharpen the bite windows even further — shorter, more violent, more predictable if you're there for them. If I had one morning to fish this coming week, I'd be anchored off Monhegan's western side on the top of the incoming, mackerel jig in one hand and a bass rod rigged with live bait in the other, waiting for the birds to tell me where to look.
