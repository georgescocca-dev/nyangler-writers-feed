# The Bunker Are Balling Up: Montauk Stages for Its First Real Blitz of the Fall
*Derby blues, staging stripers, and a bait supply that's about to get squeezed hard by the moon.*

**MONTAUK, NY — September 7** — _by Rich Martin, Zone Writer_

This is the week of the season where the Point stops being patient. Anyone who's fished Montauk long enough knows the rhythm — the bunker show up thick in August, the blues chew on them all summer, and then sometime in early September the moon and the cooling nights start squeezing that bait into something tighter, something the bass can't ignore anymore. That's exactly where we are right now. Water off the Lighthouse is holding around 69 degrees, still warm enough to keep everything comfortable, but the nights are starting to drop, and that's the signal that matters more than the thermometer. We flagged this pattern last week — said the building moon would tighten the bunker schools and that the first legitimate blitz activity of the fall was likely within the week to ten days. The bait is doing exactly that right now, balling tight off the south side and around the Point, and the stripers are starting to show real interest instead of just picking at stragglers on the edges.

The headline number of the week belongs to the bluefish, not the bass — an 18.49-pound blue took down a $25,000 derby prize, and that fish is no fluke. The blue run this year has been thick and aggressive, with fish in the 8-to-12-pound class common on bunker schools from the Lighthouse rip down through Shagwong and out toward the Elbow. Anyone snagging bunker and drifting them back on wire or a stout fluorocarbon leader has been getting bit fast, sometimes before the bait even settles. Jigging with metal — Deadly Dicks, Hopkins, or a diamond jig in white or bunker-scale finish — has also been drawing blues up out of the boils when the bait pods surface and get pushed by current against the rocks.

Striped bass are the story to watch, and right now it's a staging pattern more than a full-on blitz. Boats working bunker schools off North Bar and along the boulder field on the ocean side have been marking good numbers of bass sitting under and around the bait, but the bite has been more methodical than explosive — a few fish committing on each pass rather than the kind of surface chaos everyone's waiting for. Live-lining bunker on a fish-finder rig with a 5/0 circle hook, letting the bait swim naturally in the current seam where the rip meets slack water, has been the most consistent producer. For the surf crowd working the Point itself, especially on the last two hours of the incoming when the current really starts stacking against the rocks, bucktails in the 1.5 to 2-ounce range with a white or chartreuse teaser have picked off bass holding tight to structure. The fish aren't stupid — they're staging on the edges of the bait, not necessarily in the thick of it, so working the perimeter of a bunker pod rather than blind-casting into the middle of it has been the difference for guys getting bit.

Offshore, the tuna bite has stayed dependable for the boats making the run, with bigeye and yellowfin coming on the popping bite — black-and-purple and green Rapala-style stickbaits worked hard on the surface early and late in the day. That pattern has held all week with stable water and no real weather disruption, and there's no reason to think it changes in the next few days unless a front moves through and stirs things up.

Albies and bonito are still working the rips east of the Point, and that fishery has been a bright spot for guys willing to run light tackle and cover water. Epoxy jigs and small metal — half-ounce to three-quarter-ounce — thrown into the boils around Shagwong Reef and Turtle Cove have been drawing strikes, though the bite has been more about timing than volume. First light and the last hour of daylight have been the windows, when the bait gets pushed hard by the tide and the false albacore commit rather than just slashing through and disappearing.

Fluke fishing is winding down from its summer peak, as expected this time of year, but there's still enough action on the drift in 30 to 50 feet off the south side to make it worth a few hours if bass and blues aren't cooperating. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the sandy stretches, have picked at keeper-class fish, though the bigger doormats have started their push toward deeper water as the month goes on. Porgies remain a solid backup on the structure spots, and blackfish season is still a few weeks off, but tackle shops are already fielding questions about it — a sure sign everyone senses the calendar turning.

Looking ahead, the next several days should tell the tale. The moon is building toward new phase around September 10, and if that tide does what it typically does at the Point, expect the bait to get squeezed even harder against the rocks and rips, and the bass to stop staging and start committing in earnest. If the wind stays cooperative and doesn't blow the water dirty, the window from Wednesday through the weekend is shaping up as the best shot yet this fall for the first real blitz off the Point. Anyone with flexible plans should watch the forecast closely and be ready to fish the building tide on short notice — this is the kind of setup that doesn't wait around for a convenient weekend.
