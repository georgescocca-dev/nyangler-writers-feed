# A Lost Bigeye at Midnight and the Case for Staying the Course to Atlantis
*The fleet running out of Montauk, Block Island, and the Bay lost one at the leader this week — but the warm water holding the tuna in the east canyons hasn't budged.*

**MONTAUK, NY — September 4** — _by Capt. Steve Brock, Zone Writer_

The radio chatter out of the Montauk fuel dock Tuesday night told the story better than any log book could. A boat working the east wall at Atlantis had a big bigeye up on the shortest flat line at 11 p.m., worked it boatside under the spreader lights for the better part of twenty minutes, and lost it at the leader when the hooks pulled. No cursing, no drama on the channel — just a flat "that's the one that got away" and a request for someone to check the fuel dock hours before the 3 a.m. run home. That's the tenor of this week in the east canyons: fish are there, the work is real, and the margins are thin enough that one bad hook set turns a good trip into a long ride back with an empty fish box.

The warm-water regime that's carried this canyon bite since July hasn't broken down. That's the headline, and it's the one worth trusting through Labor Day week. We flagged this last week — said the tuna action would hold and that this stretch of ocean was worth watching as a bellwether for how far the warm water was pushing north — and here it is, still intact, with swordfish now showing on the deep drop gear pushing further into Gulf of Maine water than is typical for early September. That's not a coincidence. When the filament that's been feeding Atlantis, Veatch, Hydrographer, and Oceanographer starts pushing swordfish that far north, it tells you the warm signature has some staying power left in it.

The run math hasn't changed and it never will. Out of Montauk, Atlantis is the closer of the four — call it an eight-to-ten hour round trip depending on sea state, which makes it the default pick when the weather window is tight. Veatch and Hydrographer sit further east and are better played from Block Island or the Rhode Island ports — Point Judith and the Bay boats are looking at a longer commit, often a true overnight, but the fuel burn pays for itself when the fish are stacked tight to the wall. Oceanographer is the long ball from any of these ports and this week it wasn't the play — the better reports were concentrated closer to home water at Atlantis and the western edge of Veatch, where the canyon wall holds structure and the current seams have been setting up clean.

Yellowfin have been the bread-and-butter fish, running mixed sizes from schoolie-class up to 40-plus pounds, taken both on the troll and on the chunk once the boats shut down over showing marks. Spreader bars and cedar plugs pulled off the shelf and down the canyon wall accounted for the bulk of the daytime yellowfin, with green machines and small Ilanders in the shorts working the middle of the pattern. Once the sun got low, the chunk bite on butterfish took over — small chunks, no weight, drifted back on a slow current, has been the difference-maker on Atlantis for the bigger fish showing at dusk and through the first few hours of dark. That's exactly the pattern that produced the bigeye lost this week — boats shutting the engines down, setting up a slow chunk slick over structure on the wall, and working the bite methodically rather than covering water.

Bluefin have been mixed in with the yellowfin on the troll, mostly smaller school fish in the 40-to-80 pound class, though there have been a handful of reports of bigger fish breaking off gear rigged for yellowfin — a reminder to check hooks and leader wind knots before every set when working these grounds, because the fish showing up don't always match the tackle you brought for them. Bigeye have been an after-dark proposition almost exclusively, coming on the chunk or on deep-dropped baits worked below the thermocline once the sun's off the water.

Wahoo and mahi have been a bonus rather than a target, picked up on high-speed trolled lures worked over structure on the way out to the grounds — small blue-white and pink-white lures pulled fast over the drop-offs have accounted for a few wahoo in the 30-to-50 pound range. White marlin sightings have been sparse this week compared to earlier in the summer; a few boats reported raising fish on the troll but conversions have been low, which tracks with this point in the season as the marlin bite starts to thin out ahead of the fall transition.

Looking ahead, the moon is building toward full, which should tighten up the tidal push through the canyons and could sharpen the bite windows over the next several days — worth planning trips around the stronger current stages rather than fighting slack water. The bigger variable sitting on the watch list is tropical development in the western Atlantic. Nothing organized is threatening the grounds as of this week, but that's the one thing that can shut this bite down fast regardless of how good the water looks right now. If the pattern holds and no storm intrudes, Atlantis and the western Veatch wall remain the better bets for boats running out of Montauk and Block Island through the holiday weekend — just budget the fuel and the sleep for a real offshore day, because nothing about this run gets shorter just because the fishing's good.
