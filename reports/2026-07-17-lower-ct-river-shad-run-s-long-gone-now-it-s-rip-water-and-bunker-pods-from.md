# Shad Run's Long Gone — Now It's Rip Water and Bunker Pods from Saybrook to Great Island
*The river's shifted into its dog-days rhythm, and the fish that matter now are working current edges, not the flats at midday.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — July 17** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

By the middle of July the river tells you plainly where you stand in the season. The shad are three months gone — that silver run up past Essex and the Salmon River tributaries wrapped by early June, and even the tail-end hickories that hang around into the solstice have pushed out or died off on their spawning run. What's left now is marsh grass gone chest-high on Great Island, ospreys feeding fledglings off dead snags along the flats, and bunker pods — real ones, thumb-sized peanuts pushed up into the coves — moving with the tide instead of against it. I've watched this river do this dance for fifty springs and just as many Julys, and the lesson never changes: once the shad clear out, the striper game here becomes a current game, not a structure game.

Out in the Sound proper, the numbers back up what I'm feeling at the mouth. New London's reading around 67.5 this week, a few degrees cooler than what's pushing out of our own river mouth on the ebb, and that temperature seam matters more than people think. The river itself runs warmer than the open Sound in July — freshwater flow off the Connecticut watershed heats up fast under a string of sunny days — and that warm outflow meeting the cooler Sound water right off Saybrook Point creates a real thermal edge. Bass and blues both know it. They sit right on that seam, especially on the last two hours of the ebb when the warm water's pushing hardest out past the breakwaters.

The rip off Saybrook Point and the Calves Island rip upriver have both been holding fish, but it's not the easy, stack-em-up bite you get during the shad run. This is scattered, tide-dependent work. I fished the Point rip Tuesday morning on the last of the ebb into the top of the flood, and it took me forty minutes of drifting a one-ounce white bucktail with a curl-tail trailer before I connected — a 26-inch fish that came up right where the current line folded against the rock. Slow, deliberate hops along the bottom, not a fast retrieve — these fish are eating what the current brings them, not chasing anything down. Chartreuse worked just as well when the water carried a little color after Monday's rain runoff.

Great Island flats are the other piece of this puzzle, and they're producing better numbers if not better size. Juvenile bass and a growing push of bluefish are working the edges of the eelgrass beds on the incoming tide, feeding on those peanut bunker schools I mentioned. Light tackle guys — 7-foot spinning rods, small Kastmasters or half-ounce Deadly Dicks — are getting into snapper blues and schoolie stripers together in the same drifts, which tells me the size classes are still mixed and the bigger fish haven't fully separated out yet. That's normal for mid-July. The real slot-and-up bass are mostly holding deeper, working the channel edges near DEP Marina and the mouth itself, and they're not showing themselves during the heat of the day. Dawn and the last hour of light are doing all the work right now.

For those bigger resident fish, bunker chunks on a fishfinder rig, fished right on bottom in the deeper cuts off the marina and along the edge of the main channel, have been the most consistent producer for anyone willing to sit still through a tide change instead of covering water. I talked to a guy at the ramp Wednesday who'd taken two fish over 30 inches doing exactly that on the outgoing, using fresh-cut bunker he'd snagged himself off the surface schools near the railroad bridge. Fresh matters here — these fish have seen enough frozen chunk bait by midsummer that they get particular.

White perch fishing has actually been the most dependable part of the week, if not the most glamorous. The deep holes below Essex and around the mouth of the Lieutenant River are holding good numbers, and small hooks tipped with grass shrimp or bloodworm strips, fished on light spinning gear right on bottom during the slower parts of the tide, are putting decent perch — eight to eleven inches — in the cooler for anyone patient enough to sit on them. It's not a bite that makes headlines, but it's honest fishing and it's there every single day right now, which is more than I can say for the striper action.

Looking ahead, I don't expect this pattern to change much through the next week unless we get a real cool-down or a hard blow that stirs the river bottom and pushes more bait around. If the wind stays light out of the southwest like it has been, I'd fish the rips at first light and last light, work the current seams hard on bucktails, and save the bunker chunks for the deeper holes when the sun's high. The bigger bass are here — they're just making you earn every one of them this time of year, which is exactly how this river runs once the shad have gone home.
