# The Rip at the Hook holds blues under bunker while channel-edge fluke keep paying at Keyport
*First light on Sandy Hook's rip, midday on the channel edges, evening on the Navesink for weakfish — this week's bay is a three-tide puzzle.*

**PERTH AMBOY, NJ — August 7** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

I made the call Monday night sitting on my tailgate at the Amboy launch ramp: mornings this week are for the Hook, not the flats. Here's why. Bunker schools have been stacking hard against the rip at Sandy Hook on the tail of the outgoing, and where the bunker pile up that thick, you don't leave them to go poke around Keyport in the dark. You fish the meatball first, then you go find your fluke once the sun's up and the bait scatters.

And that's exactly how the week has gone. First light Tuesday, we ran out past Horseshoe Cove and found bunker balled up so tight along the rip line you could see them nervous on the surface before the birds even committed. Blues came up under them in typical August fashion — 3 to 6 pounds, ravenous, cutting bunker in half on top. We didn't even need lures at times, just drifted a chunk of fresh bunker on a fish-finder rig, 2 ounces of lead, right into the edge of the boil, and let it sink eight to ten feet. Hookups came fast. Mixed in were a few schoolie stripers, 22 to 26 inches, sliding along the bottom of the bait ball rather than up top — that's the pattern right now, bass staying low and working the scraps while the blues do the violence up top. If you want a bass specifically, don't fish the surface chaos. Drop a bucktail — white or chartreuse, ¾ ounce — down through the bait and let it flutter on the bottom before you start working it back. That's where the stripers are eating.

We flagged this pattern a few days back — said the bunker pushing into the bay on tide and wind would keep the blitz intensifying, and that's held true. The bait hasn't thinned out, and as long as it stays parked on that rip through the week, the blues and the low-riding bass will keep using it as a buffet.

By 9 or 10 a.m., though, that bite goes quiet — sun gets high, boat traffic picks up out of the Hook, and the bait pushes off. That's when I point the boat back into the bay and go to work the channel edges off Keyport and Flynn's Knoll. This has been the more consistent, less flashy program all summer, and it hasn't slowed a bit. Fluke are sitting tight to the drop where the flats fall off into 18 to 25 feet, right where the current sweeps bait off the shallows and dumps it into deeper water. Standard drift with 4-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on a bucktail, or bounced bare bucktail tipped with squid strips, has been the ticket. Sizes have been mixed — plenty of shorts, but we're seeing enough 19- to 22-inch fish mixed in that a limit isn't a stretch if you put in the drift time. Best window has been the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide, when the current is still moving enough to keep your bait working but hasn't gone slack yet.

Water's sitting in that mid-70s range through the bay right now, which is exactly the kind of warmth that pushes weakfish and peanut bunker into the back rivers. We caught wind of this building a week ago, and it's playing out now on the Navesink and up into the Shrewsbury — weakfish showing on the flats near the Highlands bridge and up past Red Bank, chasing peanut bunker that have moved into the skinny water. Light jigheads, ¼ ounce, tipped with a 3-inch pearl or chartreuse paddletail, worked slow along the channel edges on the last of the ebb, has been drawing strikes from weakfish in the 16- to 20-inch range. It's not a numbers game yet — two or three fish an evening if you work it right — but the pattern is building, not fading, and I expect that to keep filling in as more peanuts get squeezed into that skinnier water through the rest of August.

Porgies are still a solid backup at Romer Shoal if the wind kicks up and you don't want to fight the open bay. Anchor up on the shoal in 15 to 20 feet, clam or sandworm on a high-low rig, and you can put together a respectable mess of porgies without burning a lot of gas or nerve.

Wind's been the swing factor. When it lays down out of the southwest in the early morning, the rip at the Hook fishes clean and the bunker stay concentrated. Push any harder and that bait scatters before you even get the lines wet, so I'd plan around the calm windows rather than fighting a stiff breeze out there. Inside the bay, the channel-edge fluke bite doesn't care much about wind — it's tide-driven, not surface-driven — so that's your fallback no matter what the morning throws at you.

Looking at the week ahead, if this bunker stays parked on the Hook rip and the wind cooperates at first light, I'd keep hammering that pattern hard before it inevitably breaks up and scatters into the bay proper. Once that happens, expect the blitz energy to spread thinner across Flynn's Knoll and Romer Shoal rather than concentrate in one spot — good for coverage, tougher for consistency. Either way, the fluke on the channel edges aren't going anywhere soon, and the weakfish push into the rivers is just getting started.
