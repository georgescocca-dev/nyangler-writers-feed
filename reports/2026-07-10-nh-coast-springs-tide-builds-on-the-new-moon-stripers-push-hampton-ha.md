# Springs tide builds on the new moon — stripers push Hampton Harbor, groundfish hold tight to the Shoals breaks
*NE wind upwelling sharpened the structure off White Island while the incoming build stacked bass against the Hampton inlet rocks.*

**RYE HARBOR, NH — July 10** — _by Cole Robinson, Zone Writer_

We're heading into the new moon, and if you've fished this stretch of coast for more than a season you already know what that means before I say it: springs tides building all weekend, bigger swings on the Hampton and Piscataqua gauges, and current at the Shoals that'll flat-out move a boat if you're not paying attention to your scope. New moon lands midweek, and the buildup through Friday-into-Monday is when I want to be on the water, not after. Last week's wind sat out of the northeast more than I'd like for comfort but exactly where I want it for fishing — that upwelling pattern pushed cooler, cleaner water up onto the ledges around White Island and Halibut Rocks, and it sharpened the edge on those breaks the way it always does. Cold water stacking against warmer surface water off the Shoals concentrates bait, and bait on an edge means cod and haddock sitting tight to structure instead of scattered over mud. That's the setup I fish for.

Water temp in the harbors has been climbing on the flood — nothing shocking, just steady summer warm-up — while out at the Shoals the bottom stayed cooler under that NE push. That thermal split matters. It's why the mackerel showed up thick around Star Island and Appledore this week, working the surface in the morning calm before the boat traffic scatters them, and it's why the groundfish stayed deep and tight rather than spreading out. Going into this week, expect the wind to back around more southerly by the weekend as the new moon springs peak — that usually softens the upwelling signal, so if you're going to fish the Shoals structure hard, earlier in the week is your window before that cold edge relaxes.

Stripers are the story inshore right now, and Hampton Harbor is doing exactly what it does on a building spring tide — pushing bait and fish through the inlet on the last two hours of the incoming. I had a report of fish to 28 inches taken right off the jetty rocks at Hampton on the top of the flood, working white and chartreuse soft plastics — 4-inch Slug-Go style baits on a light jighead — swung tight against the rip where the harbor current meets the ocean push. Same pattern's working at Rye Harbor breakwater on the same stage of tide, though the fish there have been more scattered, more schoolie-sized, mixed with the occasional bluefish that'll cut your leader if you're not running at least 20-pound fluoro. Great Boars Head has been worth a look on the outgoing at first light — bait pods of pogies pushing off the beach have pulled some bass tight to the surf line, and a swimming plug worked parallel to the beach has produced better than anything thrown straight out.

The Piscataqua is fishing the way it always does in July — you need to time the tide precisely or you're wasting gas. Stripers are holding on the current seams below the Memorial Bridge and down toward Henderson Point, and they want the tide moving, not slack. Live mackerel under a slip float, drifted through the seam on the last hour of the ebb, has been the most consistent producer I've heard of, though it's not a numbers game — one or two quality fish a trip is realistic, not a bent rod every cast. Flounder are still around in Portsmouth Harbor and up in the back channels off Pierce Island — small hooks, sandworms or clams, fished right on bottom in that softer mud current away from the main channel. Nothing to write home about size-wise this week, mostly 12-14 inch fish, but steady if you're patient and anchored in the right spot on the bottom of the tide.

Offshore, the cod and haddock bite at the Shoals has been honest work, not easy work. Boats working Sunken Ledge and the rock piles south of White Island in 80 to 130 feet found haddock stacked tight on the NE-sharpened edge I mentioned, taking clam bait and diamond jigs tipped with a sandworm strip on the drift — but you had to hit the tide right. Slack water scattered them; moving water on either side of the tide change held them tight to structure. Cod have been mixed in but smaller than we'd like this time of year, mostly short fish thrown back, with the occasional keeper in the 24-26 inch range coming off bottom bounced jigs worked slow. If I had a boat this week I'd fish the two hours around the tide change, not the middle of the flood or ebb when everything's moving too fast to hold bottom without a pound of lead.

Looking ahead — the new moon Tuesday means the biggest tides we'll see this month, and that PM flood window at Hampton Harbor lining up with sunset over the next several days is worth building a trip around. Bass tend to commit harder on that last push of light with a strong tide behind them, and if the bait stays stacked at the inlet the way it has been, I'd expect that pattern to hold through the weekend. Offshore, watch the wind — if it backs southerly like it's forecast to, that cold upwelled edge at the Shoals will soften and the groundfish will spread back out over more bottom, which usually means slower, more marks-and-miss fishing instead of the tight, predictable bite we've had. Best case, one more good push of NE wind before it shifts gives us another day or two of that sharp edge. Either way, I'd rather fish the tide change with a plan than the middle of it hoping.
