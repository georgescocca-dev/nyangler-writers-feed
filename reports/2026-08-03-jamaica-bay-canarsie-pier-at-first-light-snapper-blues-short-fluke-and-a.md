# Canarsie Pier at First Light: Snapper Blues, Short Fluke, and a Sneaky Keeper or Two
*The bay's stuck in its August groove — light tackle up top, big bass hiding deep and feeding after dark, until the tide starts building toward the new moon.*

**JAMAICA BAY, NY — August 3** — _by Denise "Dee" Vasquez, Zone Writer_

I made the call Tuesday to launch the kayak out of the Motts Basin ramp instead of running the boat down to the inlet, and it was the right one. When the bay's this warm and this still, you want to be somewhere you can slide into skinny water quiet — no engine noise, no wake — and work the marsh edges before the sun gets high and pushes everything down. That's been my thinking all week: pick the access point that matches what the water's actually doing, not what I feel like fishing.

And what the water's doing right now is running warm, stable, and a little sleepy in the middle of the day — classic August bay. We said last week this was a pattern that needed a tide change or a front to really turn over, and that's exactly what's played out. Nothing's blown up, nothing's crashed. It's just steady, workmanlike fishing if you're willing to adjust your hours and your targets.

From the kayak, working the flats off Ruler's Bar and the channel edges near Big Egg Marsh on the last of the incoming, I've been finding short fluke almost everywhere — sandy pockets in 8 to 12 feet, drifting a 3-inch white Gulp swimming mullet on a 1/4-ounce jighead, just enough weight to feel bottom without plowing it. Mixed into those shorts, maybe one in six or seven fish, is a keeper. Nothing spectacular, but honest work if you cover water and don't marry one drift too long.

Canarsie Pier has been the money spot for anybody fishing from shore with kids or without a boat at all, because the snapper blues showed up hard the last ten days and they haven't left. Small hooks, strips of spearing or a sliver of bunker under a bobber, fished right off the pier pilings on any stage of tide — that's been steady fun, and every so often a bigger cocktail blue muscles in and surprises somebody using gear that's way too light for it. If you want a slightly bigger grade of bluefish, work the Cross Bay Bridge rocks on the outgoing with a small metal — quarter-ounce Deadly Dick or similar — cast up-current and let it swing.

The bigger bass are exactly where the warm water says they should be: deep, and mostly nocturnal. Marine Parkway Bridge is still the spot, but you need to shift your clock. I fished the outgoing there two nights ago starting around 10 p.m., working bucktails — 1-ounce white with a white Gulp trailer — tight to the pilings and letting the current sweep them down along the structure. Two fish in the mid-20s, one push to 30, all on the same drift pattern, all in that first hour of hard outgoing water when the current stacks bait against the concrete. Daytime at that same spot has been dead for stripers. If you show up at noon expecting the same bite, you're wasting a tide.

Breezy Point surf has been quieter than I'd like for early August. A few schoolies mixed with bluefish on bucktails and swimming plugs right around first light, but nothing consistent enough to plan a trip around yet. That water's still waiting on real current and a break in this heat to wake up the way it can in September.

Weakfish have been the wildcard — not a target you can plan on, but real if you're fishing the channel edges through a tide change with the right presentation. I picked up two nice ones, one close to four pounds, working a small chartreuse twister tail on a light jighead along the drop-off near the Cross Bay Bridge on the tail end of the outgoing, right where the deeper channel meets the flat. That's a small window, maybe 20 minutes around the tide change, but it's real if you're there for it.

Porgies and blackfish are still mostly a September-October conversation in this bay — too warm right now, and the blackfish especially want that first real cool-down before they commit to structure. I wouldn't spend a trip chasing them yet.

Looking ahead, the tides start building toward the new moon over the next couple of days, and that's the thing that could actually change this pattern instead of just repeating it. Stronger current means more oxygen turning over in the bay, better bait movement, and — if we get any front activity mixed in, even a modest one — a real chance the bass shift back toward a daytime bite instead of hiding until dark. I wouldn't bank a whole trip on that shift happening by the weekend, but if I had one day to fish this week, I'd take the early morning outgoing at Marine Parkway and have a backup plan to slide into the bay for fluke if the bass don't show. That's the honest play right now — steady light tackle up top, patient nighttime work for the bigger fish, and an eye on the building tide for the first real sign this thing turns over.
