# Everybody's Drifting Fluke at Noon — The Real Bass Are Working the Rocks at Midnight
*Warm water pushed the stripers off the daytime pattern, but the jetty bite after dark is the best-kept secret at Jones right now.*

**JONES BEACH, NY — August 3** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Every boat ramp conversation this week starts the same way — guys asking where the fluke are biting, like that's the only game in town. Meanwhile I'm loading eels into a cooler at 9pm and heading for the west jetty rocks at the inlet, and nobody's asking me a thing. That's fine by me. Less company on the rocks.

Here's what nobody wants to admit: the ocean's sitting around 71 degrees off the beach right now, with some junky 4-foot swell riding in at short 5-second intervals — sloppy, uncomfortable, not the kind of water that gets stripers cruising the surf in daylight chasing bunker schools like they were in June. That surface warmth pushes bass deep and nocturnal. They don't shut off, they just clock out for the day shift and punch in after the sun goes down. I've fished this inlet long enough to know that pattern cold, and this week it held up exactly like I expected. We flagged this last week — said the night bass bite would be the better play for trophy-class fish while the daytime bite stayed fluke-and-blue driven — and that's exactly how it's playing out.

So here's where I've been: the rocks on the west side of Jones Inlet, fishing the last two hours of the outgoing into the start of the incoming, dead low around 1am this week with the tides we're running. Eels on a fish-finder rig, or bunker chunks fished on the bottom if the current's not ripping too hard. The current sweeps bait right off the tip of that jetty into the deep water, and that's where the bigger fish are sitting, waiting for it to come to them instead of chasing it down in the wash like they do in October. Landed a 34-inch fish Tuesday night on a chunk soaked in that deep slot off the rocks, and lost something bigger that just about ripped the rod out of the sand spike. That's the kind of fish that's around right now if you're willing to lose sleep for it.

During the day, the fluke bite is exactly what it's been all summer — steady, not spectacular. Sloop Channel and the drop-offs along the edges of the Jones Inlet channel are holding keepers mixed with a lot of shorts, which is about what I told you to expect last week too. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the sand-to-mud transitions in 15 to 25 feet, outgoing tide gives you the cleanest drift. I'm not seeing blitz-level fluke action anywhere in the bay, but if you cover ground and work the structure right, you'll put a few keepers to 4 pounds in the cooler. Don't expect miracles — this is a grinder's bite right now, not a walk-up-and-load-the-boat bite.

Bluefish are the one thing actually picking up steam. There's a wave of blues pushing down out of the Sound and along the Jersey coast right now, and I think it's starting to spill into our rips. I had three separate mornings this week working diamond jigs — half-ounce to one-ounce, no bucktail, just bare metal — through the rip line off the inlet on the last of the incoming, and connected on choppers to 6 pounds each time. Nothing giant, but steady, and getting more consistent by the day. If that northern push keeps rolling the way it has for the Jersey boats, I expect this bluefish bite at the inlet to get stronger before it gets weaker.

Now here's something worth paying attention to — the weakfish. They've been quietly building back into this bay for a couple seasons, and this week I finally started hearing about legit numbers again out of the deeper holes in South Oyster Bay, particularly around the channel bends off Freeport. Guys working small soft plastics — 3-inch chartreuse or pink shad-tail bodies on light jigheads, fished slow along bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing — are picking off weakfish in the 18 to 22-inch range in the evening. It's not a fleet-wide pattern yet, and I wouldn't build a whole trip around it, but if you're in that area at dusk it's worth ten casts.

Porgies are still holding tight to structure around the jetty rocks and the pilings under Wantagh Bridge — nothing fancy needed there, sandworms or clam on a hi-lo rig, small hooks, and you'll fill a bucket in an hour if you find the right piece of rock.

Looking ahead, we're building toward the new moon and the tides are only going to get stronger through the weekend, which should sharpen up both the bass bite at the rocks and get that bluefish push moving harder through the rip. If a front comes through and knocks that surface warmth down even a couple degrees, don't be surprised if the bass start showing up earlier in the evening instead of waiting for full dark — that's the tell I'd watch for. Until then, I'm sleeping in and fishing at night, and I'd suggest you do the same.
