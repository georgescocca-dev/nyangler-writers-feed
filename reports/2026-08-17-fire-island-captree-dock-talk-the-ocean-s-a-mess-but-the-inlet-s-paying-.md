# Captree dock talk: the ocean's a mess, but the inlet's paying rent
*With a beat-up ocean pushing everything inside, bass and blues are stacking on current seams while bay fluke stay stubbornly short.*

**CAPTREE, NY — August 17** — _by Mariana "Mari" Reyes, Zone Writer_

Guy at the fuel dock this morning asks me if it's worth running the beach at Democrat Point before work. I told him what I've been telling everybody all week — leave the waders in the truck and fish the inlet instead. There's a lumpy 4-footer rolling in off the bar right now, short-period junk, the kind of swell that makes standing on the sand feel like punishment more than fishing. Nobody's catching anything worth talking about out there. But run that same water fifteen minutes inside the rocks and it's a different world.

We flagged this a few weeks back — said the striper bite would stay the headline story through the middle of the month, and here we are. The bass haven't left, they've just gotten smart about where they sit. There's a real gap right now between what the open ocean's doing and what the bay and inlet are doing, and that gap is exactly why the fish are piling up on current structure instead of spreading out along the beach. Ocean water's sitting around 71 with that chop stacked on top of it, while the bay and back creeks are running noticeably warmer through the afternoons. Stripers don't love warm and they don't love junk water, so they're parking on the hard seams where moving current gives them relief from both — the rip off Democrat Point, the pilings under the Robert Moses bridges, the deeper cuts inside the inlet itself.

Best bite I've had this week has been the night tide at the inlet mouth, working the last two hours of the incoming into the top of high. Live bunker on a fish-finder rig, fished tight to the rock line where the current stacks up, has been drawing bass from 24 up to 34 inches, with the occasional better fish mixed in around the tide change. If you're bucktailing instead, stick with 1.5 to 2-ounce white or chartreuse, and let it swing down and through the seam rather than casting up-current — the fish are holding low and eating on the drop, not chasing anything across the top.

Bluefish have done exactly what we said they'd do back on the 14th. The snapper blues that were thick in the back bays a few weeks ago have grown into something with real shoulders. Patchogue River, the Swan River mouth, and the docks around Bellport are stuffed with fish in the one-to-three-pound class hammering small silver spoons and mini poppers off the bulkheads — a great, low-stakes evening with the kids if you've got them. Out at the inlet, adult blues in the three-to-five-pound range are mixing right into the bass schools on bunker chunks, and more than a few guys have had their bass rigs bit off clean by fish that size holding the same seam.

Fluke is the honest disappointment this week. The bay's full of short fish — Great South Bay flats off Sayville and Bay Shore are lousy with them, but you're picking through a dozen sublegal fish for every keeper. The better ones are stacking near the inlets themselves, where tidal flow keeps the water a few degrees cooler than the stagnant upper bay. Drifting 25 to 35 feet around the Robert Moses bridge pilings on the outgoing, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on a 3/4-ounce bucktail have put a handful of 4-to-6-pound fish in the boat for me, but it's not fast fishing — more a game of covering ground and being patient with the drift speed than working one spot hard.

Weakfish are still around in small numbers, mostly a bonus fish rather than a target — a few keepers have come on soft plastics worked slow along the channel edges off Sayville right at last light, but I wouldn't plan a trip around them right now. Porgies are steady if unglamorous on the south side of the bridges, bloodworms or clam on a hi-lo rig in 15 to 20 feet, good action if you want dinner without much drama.

Looking ahead, the tide's building back toward the full moon on the 28th, and that extra push should sharpen everything at the current-driven spots — the inlet rip, the bridge pilings, the deeper cuts by Democrat Point. If the ocean swell settles even a little, I'd expect the beach to open back up for surf guys working bunker chunks or big darters at dawn. Until then, my money stays inside the inlet on the incoming tide, and I'd rather work one current seam thoroughly than run all over the bay hoping to stumble into fish. That's been the difference this week between a slow trip and a good one.
