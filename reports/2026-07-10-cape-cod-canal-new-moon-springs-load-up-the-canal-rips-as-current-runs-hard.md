# New moon springs load up the Canal rips as current runs hard both ends
*Bigger water this week means bigger bait pinned against the rock — here's where the current seams are stacking bass right now.*

**SAGAMORE, MA — July 10** — _by Mike Devlin, Zone Writer_

Last week the Canal was still shaking off that stretch of soft, lazy tides we get around the quarter moons — current running but nothing with teeth to it, and the bite matched it. Schoolies mostly, a few mid-20s mixed in at the turn, but nothing that made you want to skip breakfast. That's changing fast. We're sliding into new moon, and the springs that come with it are building toward peak alignment right around July 11-13. If you've fished this ditch as long as I have, you know what that means: current velocity ramps up at both ends, the rip lines sharpen from mush into something you can actually read, and bait that's been scattered in slack water gets pinned hard against structure. This is the week the Canal remembers it's the best current-fishing on the East Coast.

Here's the mechanics of it, because it matters. On spring tides the flow through the ditch can push 5 knots or better at max current off the Bourne and Sagamore Bridge pilings, and that extra push is exactly what turns the East End rip — the water spilling off Sandwich side toward Cape Cod Bay — from a gentle seam into a defined, foamy edge you can cast a plug into with confidence. Same story at the West End, where the Buzzards Bay outflow past Mashnee and Hog Island stacks up against the current seam running off the rock jetty. Bigger tide, bigger differential between the fast lane and the eddy behind it, and that differential is where stripers stack up and let the current do the work for them. I don't fish the middle of the flood or ebb on a big tide — I fish the last ninety minutes into the turn, when the rip is still cranking but starting to organize, and the fish move up onto the structure to feed instead of holding deep and coasting.

Right now the East End is producing best on the tail of the east-to-west flow, the stretch where the water dumps out of Cape Cod Bay past the Sandwich Marina and Herring Run. I've had reports of stripers to 30-plus inches taking white and chartreuse Ava jigs with a red surgical tube trailer worked through that rip at first light, bounced just off bottom and let it swing with the current instead of ripping it against the flow. At night, live eels fished on a fish-finder rig off the rocks near the Herring Run outflow have been drawing the better class of fish — not a lot of them, but the ones that show are the 20-30 pound Canal fish everybody's after. That's the honest read: it's not a numbers game right now, it's a quality-over-quantity bite, and you need to put your time in during that narrow window when the tide's still moving hard but hasn't gone full washing-machine on you.

West End has been the more consistent producer for volume. The current seam running off Mashnee Island and down toward Phinneys Harbor has held bluefish mixed with schoolie bass most mornings, and topwater guys throwing pencil poppers and Super Strike Zig-Zags off the rocks near the Weight Handling Facility have been connecting at first light before the sun gets up over the trees. Once that sun's up and the tide's still running, switch to bucktails — half-ounce to one-ounce white or olive, tipped with a strip of pork rind or a swim shad — and work the current seam where the fast water meets the eddy behind Hog Island. That's a textbook Canal ambush point and it's fishing exactly like it should on this bigger water.

Fluke guys shouldn't ignore what's happening at the west end either. The sandy stretches off Mashnee flats and the deeper water in the mouth of Phinneys Harbor have been holding keeper fluke on the drift, four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on a half-ounce bucktail, working the last two hours of the outgoing when the current sweeps bait off the flats into the deeper channel. Nothing spectacular in numbers, but a few fish in the 18-20 inch range have come out of there in the past week, which for Canal-adjacent fluke water is a solid showing.

What I'm watching for the coming week: the bonito corridor down south has widened out of Block Island into the Newport-Point Judith stretch, with the old cold wall between the islands and Rhode Island Sound collapsing. That water's a few days from us at best, but if that warm plume keeps pushing north through Buzzards Bay, Wings Neck and the west end approaches are where I'd expect the first scout fish to show — false albacore usually beat bonito into this zone by a couple weeks most years, so keep half an eye on the bait schools working the rip off Wings Neck in the next two to three weeks. Too early to fish for them specifically, but not too early to start checking.

For this week specifically: if the wind stays out of the southwest and lays down through the evenings like it's supposed to, I'd fish the East End rip on the last of the ebb Friday and Saturday night, right into the new moon peak. Big tide, hard current, bait pinned against the rocks — that's the recipe that's produced the better fish all summer here. Backup plan if the East End's blown out or too strong to fish comfortably: West End seam off Mashnee on the bucktail bite, which has been steadier even if the average fish runs smaller. Either way, fish the turn, not the middle, and let the current tell you when to be there.
