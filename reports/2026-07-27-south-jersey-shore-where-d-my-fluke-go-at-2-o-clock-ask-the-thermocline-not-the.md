# Where'd my fluke go at 2 o'clock? Ask the thermocline, not the tide chart
*Shallow bay fluke have punched out for the season — the program now lives in the inlet channels, while bunker push a bluefish blitz toward Absecon.*

**OCEAN CITY, NJ — July 27** — _by Darnell Freeman, Zone Writer_

I got the same question three times this week, from three different guys at three different counters, so let's make it a lesson. "Darnell, I drifted the flats off Rainbow Channel all morning, marked bait, marked fish on the graph, and couldn't buy a bite after one o'clock. What happened?" What happened is the sun did its job. Those shallow sod bank flats — the ones that were stacked with keeper fluke back in June — are running warm enough now that the fish have simply left the classroom. When surface water in four and five feet of back bay sits in the high 70s all afternoon, summer flounder go looking for the nearest air conditioning, and around here that means the deeper bends and the inlet mouths where moving water keeps things cooler and oxygenated.

So here's where I've been putting my clients and myself the last two weeks, and where I'd keep sending you. Absecon Inlet, the stretch right along the Coast Guard station down to the Route 30 bridge, holding 18 to 25 feet on the channel edges — that's been the most dependable drift in my zone. Fish it on the last two hours of the outgoing, when the current is still strong enough to hold your bucktail down but has thinned the boat traffic. White Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, tipped with a fresh spearing strip, dragged slow along the drop where the sand gives way to mud — that combination has been outproducing bare bucktails two or three to one for me. Great Egg Inlet is running the same story. The deep bend by the Rainbow Channel range markers and the hole off Broad Thorofare, both holding fluke to 19 inches, some to 21, on the last of the outgoing into dead low. If you're marking bait balls of spearing or rain bait stacked on the ledge, slow your drift down — a fluke that's already got a belly full of forage isn't chasing a fast retrieve in July heat.

Now the bigger story this week, and one we saw building — the bluefish blitz that's been working its way down from the North Jersey beaches has arrived at our doorstep. We flagged this pattern last week, that stable southwest wind and warm water would keep pushing those bunker schools south, and that's exactly what's happening. Ocean City's beachfront, from the 34th Street jetty down past Corson's Inlet, has had bluefish blowing up on bunker pods right in the wash on the incoming tide, usually in that first hour after the tide turns when the bait gets pinned against the sandbar. Bring a metal — a 2-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — and don't overthink it, just get it past the boil and rip it back through. Sea Isle City's had the same show off the Promenade, and I expect it to keep marching toward Longport and the Absecon Inlet jetties over the next few days if this weather pattern holds. Watch the birds — when the gulls start working tight and diving instead of gliding, that's your signal to close the distance fast, because these blitzes don't sit still for long.

Stripers are a nighttime assignment now, full stop. Daytime surface temps have pushed the bass down onto structure and current seams where they're comfortable, and they're not coming up to feed until the sun's been off the water a while. My best producer has been the rock piles at the Absecon Inlet jetty, fished after dark on the incoming, bunker chunks on a fish-finder rig soaking bottom in 15 to 20 feet. A few guys working live eels along the Great Egg Inlet range structure have connected too, mostly on the top of the tide when current slackens enough for the eel to swim naturally instead of getting swept sideways. Don't expect numbers — this is a one or two fish a night proposition right now, but the fish that are coming have been quality, mid-20s to high-20-inch class with a few overslot mixed in.

Weakfish have shown some encouraging signs in the back channels — Risley Channel and the deeper cuts through Broad Thorofare have given up a few keeper-class weakfish on the evening incoming, mostly to guys drifting Bass Assassins in pink shad or a simple bucktail-and-bait combo along the mud bottom. It's not a program yet, more a signal worth watching, and if it holds through the next couple weeks it could turn into something real by late August.

Kingfish are still a solid bread-and-butter option if you want a bend in the rod without much fuss — Ocean City and Sea Isle beachfront, early morning before the crowds set up, bloodworms or Fishbites on a high-low rig in the third slough. And if you like a challenge, the triggerfish have been holding tight to the rock jetties at Absecon Inlet, taking small pieces of clam fished right against the structure on light tackle — frustrating, technical, but a fun change of pace on a slow tide.

Looking ahead, the moon's building toward full in early August, which should sharpen up that inlet current and make the fluke drifts even more productive on the stronger stages of the tide. If the southwest wind pattern holds through the weekend, I'd expect that bluefish blitz to keep pushing south past Ocean City toward Longport and possibly right up against the Absecon Inlet beachfront. Best case, we get a run of consecutive good mornings on the bunker schools before they slide further down toward Cape May. Worst case, a wind shift scatters the bait and you're back to hunting — either way, I'd have a bucktail rod and a metal rod both rigged before you leave the dock.
