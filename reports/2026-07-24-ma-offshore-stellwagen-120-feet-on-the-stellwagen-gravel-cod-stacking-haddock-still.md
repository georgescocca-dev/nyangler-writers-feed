# 120 feet on the Stellwagen gravel: cod stacking, haddock still on the mud edges
*Bluefin are pinning mackerel on top of the Bank while the bottom bite grinds along steady in July heat.*

**GLOUCESTER, MA — July 24** — _by Capt. Donna Shaw, Zone Writer_

120 feet of water on the southwest corner of the Bank, gravel bottom, and my sounder looked like static for a solid ten minutes this past Tuesday. That's cod marks, stacked tight to the structure, not spread out chasing bait like they do in the fall. This time of year they're holding right on the hard edges where the gravel gives way to broken bottom, and that's exactly where we found them — jigging diamond jigs and teasing them up with clam bellies on a standard cod rig, 6 ounces of lead to hold bottom in the current.

We said last week to expect stable conditions to hold through the stretch, and that's exactly what's played out. No fronts pushing through, no big blow to stir up the water column, just steady southwest breeze in the 10-15 knot range and a bank that's fishing the way it should in late July. The whales are thick out there right now too — humpbacks working bait balls on the surface most mornings, which tells you everything about how much forage is stacked up on this piece of real estate.

Haddock are still on the mud, like they've been all summer, sitting in that 130-140 foot range on the eastern side toward Tillies. Softer bottom, less current push, and the haddock like it that way — they're not fighting the tide like the cod are on the harder gravel. We've been finding them on standard haddock rigs, two hooks, squid strips or sandeel imitations, and the average fish has been a solid 3 to 5 pounds, with enough keepers mixed in to make it worth the run. It's not a bent-rod-every-drop fishery right now — you've got to work for it, moving the boat if the marks thin out, but a half day on the mud has been putting fish in the cooler consistently.

The real story this week has been the bluefin showing up on top of the Bank chasing mackerel schools. We've had fish busting bait in 90 to 100 feet of water right on the edge, and it's been a mixed bag — some days the slot fish are eating everything we throw at them, other days they show, blow up on the bait for twenty minutes, and vanish. Trolling small spreader bars and green machines around the bait schools has worked when the fish are up and moving, but when they go down, we've switched to jigging — Shimano butterfly jigs worked vertically through where the marks are holding, 80-150 feet, has drawn some strikes. This is post trophy-closure fishing now, so everything we're seeing is slot-class fish, and the recreational fishery around here is grinding along at a decent pace given that restriction. Nobody's filling the boat every trip, but there's enough action to keep you paying attention every time a bird starts working.

The mackerel themselves have been thick on the Bank, which is exactly why the bluefin and the whales are both camped out here. When you see the birds diving and the whales rolling through bait, that's your cue to slow down and watch the water — sometimes the tuna show right underneath that same bait ball fifteen minutes later. We've marked mackerel schools in 60 to 80 feet all along the western edge of Stellwagen, and jigging a few up for fresh bait has been worthwhile even if you're not planning to troll — a livelined mackerel drifted back near a bait ball has drawn a couple of solid takes from fish that wouldn't touch the trolled gear.

Pollock have been mixed in with the cod on the gravel edges, and a few nice ones in the 8-12 pound class have come over the rail as bycatch on the diamond jigs. Hake have been scattered in the deeper basin water, 180 feet plus, but we haven't spent much time targeting them specifically with the groundfish and tuna both producing shallower.

Looking at the week ahead, I don't see much reason for this pattern to break. The wind's supposed to stay manageable, no major system pushing through the Gulf of Maine, and that means the Bank should keep fishing the way it has — cod holding tight to the gravel in that 110-130 foot range, haddock working the softer mud toward Tillies, and bluefin opportunistic on the mackerel schools whenever the bait stacks up tight enough to trigger a feed. If you're planning a trip, I'd get out early before the wind builds in the afternoon, and I'd have both a jigging rod and a trolling spread rigged before you leave the dock — you want to be ready for whatever shows up on top when you're running out to the bottom spots. The one thing that could change things is if that mackerel bait starts thinning out or pushing off the Bank; when that happens the tuna go with it, and we'll need to start scouting Tillies and the northern edge to relocate them. For now, though, the Bank's holding everything it should for late July, and that's worth the run from either Plymouth or Gloucester.
