# Forget the bunker watch — the blues and blackbacks are paying the bills right now
*While everyone stares at the horizon waiting for the bass blitz, the real action at Jones Inlet is bluefish on the rocks and porgies stacking the channel edges.*

**FREEPORT, NY — August 31** — _by Sal Moretti, Zone Writer_

Half the guys at the bait shop this week are standing around talking about bunker schools that haven't shown up yet. I get it — everybody wants to be there when the blitz goes off. But if you're burning gas or shoe leather waiting on a striper blowup that isn't here yet, you're leaving fish on the table. Right now, at Jones Inlet, the bite that's actually feeding people is bluefish on the jetty rocks and porgies working the channel edges. That's not sexy, but it's real, and it's been steady for two weeks straight.

Here's the why. The ocean's been ugly — better than 3 to 4 feet of swell on a short period pushing onto the beach most of this week, which is enough to blow out the surf and shove bait and predators off the open front. Water out front is still holding in the low 70s, which is fine for feeding fish, but nobody wants to work a washing machine. So the activity has concentrated inside — the inlet channel, the rocks at the jetty, Short Beach flats, and up into the bay where the water's calmer and the current still does work. That's classic late-August Jones Inlet: when the ocean gets sloppy, the fish push into the structure, and the structure at this inlet is about as good as it gets on the south shore.

We flagged this pattern last week — said bluefish would keep being the most consistent producer while striper activity built slower behind it as bunker density increased. That's exactly what's playing out. The blues have been the one thing you can set a clock by. Guys working the north side rocks at the inlet and the tip of the jetty at Short Beach are getting into choppers in the 3 to 6 pound class pretty reliably on the outgoing, especially the first two hours of the ebb when bait gets pulled out of the bay and funnels past the rocks. Diamond jigs — the 2-ounce silver or gold — worked with a fast retrieve through the rip are doing the damage, and if you want to save your tackle, wire leader is not optional with these fish snapping bucktails in half.

Fluke has softened, the way it does every year right about now. It's still catchable, but it's not the June pattern anymore. Sloop Channel is still giving up keeper fish on the drift, mostly in that 25 to 32 foot stretch off the deeper bends, with 4-inch white Gulp swimming mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails working better than bare bait rigs this week. Warm bay water has fish looking for the cooler, better-oxygenated water near the inlet mouth and the deeper cuts, so if you're marking bait but not getting bit in the shallower flats, slide toward the channel edges closer to the inlet itself. The window for good fluke numbers is closing fast — figure you've got through most of September before the bulk of them slide out to the ocean structure for the fall run.

Porgies are doing what porgies do this time of year — showing up in real numbers on the rock piles and channel edges around the inlet. Bloodworms and clam on a simple hi-lo rig, fished on the bottom in 15 to 25 feet around the jetty rocks and the deeper holes off Jones Beach, is producing steady action, especially on the last two hours of the incoming when the current slows enough to hold bottom without getting swept into the rocks. Not glamorous, but it's a guaranteed bend in the rod for anybody bringing kids or somebody new to the game.

Weakfish are still the sleeper story in this bay. They're not showing in numbers yet that anybody's bragging about, but there's real chatter about fish being picked up on light jigs and soft plastics in the deeper holes off Freeport and up toward the Wantagh Bridge, mostly at last light. If that comeback keeps building the way it has the past couple seasons, September evenings on the bay side could be worth your time — small bucktails tipped with a strip of squid, worked slow along the bottom near deeper mud holes, is the play if you want to check it out.

Stripers are the story everybody's waiting on, and the pieces are starting to come together but the trigger hasn't been pulled. Bait balls are building near the inlet, and there've been scattered fish showing under the birds early and late, but it's not the concentrated blitz pattern yet. The moon's building toward full over the next several days, which means more tidal push, which means bait gets squeezed harder into the inlet mouth and the flats around Short Beach. That's usually when this fishery flips from scattered to serious. If you're going to gamble on stripers this week, first light at the inlet on the last of the outgoing, working a bunker spoon or a big topwater plug along the rip line, is the highest-percentage play — just don't expect a blitz yet.

Looking at the next few days: expect the bluefish to keep grinding along the rocks and inlet current, porgies to stay steady on bait rigs, and fluke to keep giving up fish to patient boats working the channel bends. The real change to watch for is the bait density spike as the moon builds — if bunker schools tighten up around the inlet mouth like they're starting to, the striper switch could flip fast, maybe within the next week to ten days. Keep an eye on the birds working the inlet mouth on the morning tide — that's usually the first tell before this whole thing turns on.
