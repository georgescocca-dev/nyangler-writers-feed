# Bunker Blew Through the Southwest Ledge — Bass Followed, Bonito's Next
*A week of bait-driven chaos around the island has stripers stacked tight to structure while everyone keeps one eye on the rips for the season's first bonito.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — August 14** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

I knew something had shifted before I ever put the boat in the water Tuesday morning. There's a smell that comes off Block Island Sound when the bunker are thick — oily, almost sweet — and it was in the air at the dock before sunrise. Out past the breakwater the gulls were already working, wheeling low and dropping fast, and that's a tell you learn to trust after enough summers out here. When the birds are nervous like that at 5:30 in the morning, the bunker are already under siege.

They were. We ran out toward the Southwest Ledge and found peanut bunker balled up so tight against the structure they looked like a shadow on the bottom machine, and stripers were slashing through them from underneath — that classic push-up-and-explode pattern where the whole surface goes white for ten seconds and then goes quiet again. We sat off the edge in about 35 feet, let the current walk us across the top of the ledge, and worked white and chartreuse bucktails, 1 to 1.5 ounces, right through the bait balls on the drift. Fish from 28 to 38 inches came off that structure all morning, with a couple that pushed past 20 pounds mixed in. Nothing that's going to make the cover of a magazine, but a strong, honest bite — the kind where you can put a dozen fish in the box in two hours if you're patient with your drifts and don't burn gas chasing every surface flurry you see.

The North Rip has been holding a similar pattern, though it's fishing better on the last two hours of the ebb than anywhere near the top of the tide. Current there does the work for you — bunker get funneled and squeezed through that rip line, and the bass sit underneath waiting for the current to disorganize the school. Live-lining bunker on a fish-finder rig with a 3/0 circle hook has been the most consistent producer, though guys throwing big topwater plugs — Doc's and Danny plugs mostly — have had some violent strikes right at first light. I pulled a 40-pounder out of that rip a couple weeks back and I keep going back because that fish is still down there somewhere, or one of his cousins is.

We flagged this pattern building last week — said the tidal push into the new moon would sharpen the bite at current-driven structure, and that's exactly what's happened. Two days past the new moon now, the flow through the rips and around the Ledge has real authority to it, and that's precisely what's stacking bait and holding stripers tight to structure instead of scattering everywhere. This is peak current-window fishing, and it won't last in this exact form much longer.

Bluefish have been riding right alongside the bass in the same bait schools, and they're not shy about it — 4 to 8 pound blues chopping bunker chunks to pieces on wire leader, mostly showing up mixed into the same blitzes off the Ledge and along the north side of the island toward Old Harbor. If you're fishing bait for stripers and getting bit off constantly, that's your answer — the blues are in the mix, not stealing your show, just crashing the party.

Fluke fishing has stayed modest here through August, which tracks with most of the region right now — the real fluke story this month is up around Nantucket Sound, not Block Island. We're still picking away at keepers drifting Gulp and bucktail combos off the west side in 25 to 40 feet on the last of the incoming, but it's a fish-here, fish-there proposition, not anything to build a whole trip around. Porgies have been steady and reliable on the Southwest Ledge structure itself — bloodworms and squid strips on a simple hi-lo rig will keep a rod bent for anyone who wants a break from bucktailing bass, and it's a good option if the blitz shuts down mid-tide and you need something to do with your hands.

The story everyone's actually talking about at the dock, though, is the hammerhead a surfcaster hooked into over on the south shore this week. That fish doesn't show up in cold, empty water — it shows up when the water's warm and the bait is thick, which tells you everything about what's going on around this island right now. That's also exactly the kind of signal that has me and every other captain I've talked to watching the rips hard for the first bonito or false albacore of the season. Conditions are lined up about as well as they get — warm water, heavy bait, strong current off the new moon. It hasn't happened yet, but I'd be surprised if we get through the next week without someone hooking into the first one, somewhere between here and Buzzards Bay.

Looking ahead, I don't expect this bunker-driven blitz pattern to slow down much through the coming week — the bait's not going anywhere and the current's still working in our favor. If I had one day to fish, I'd be at the Southwest Ledge on the last two hours of the ebb, working the edge where the current spills off the structure, bucktails ready, and one rod rigged light in case something with a sickle tail decides to show up in the wash. Worth being ready for it even if it doesn't happen on your trip.
