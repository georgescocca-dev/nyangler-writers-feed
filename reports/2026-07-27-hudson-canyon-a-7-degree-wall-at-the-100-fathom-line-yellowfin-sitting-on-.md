# A 7-Degree Wall at the 100-Fathom Line — Yellowfin Sitting on the Warm Side
*A Gulf Stream filament pushing 78°F water onto the canyon edge has drawn a hard line in the chart, and the tuna know exactly where it is.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 27** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

Seven degrees. That's the break I pulled off this week's SST composite, and it's the number that's been running the whole show out at Hudson. Shelf water sitting at 71.2°F — that's straight off the NY Bight buoy, and it lines up with what Montauk's reading at 69.1°F and what Kings Point's showing at 70.9°F closer to the beach — versus a filament of Gulf Stream water pushing 78 to 80°F onto the eastern wall of the canyon. When you get a spread that wide sitting right on top of the 100-fathom line, you don't need a forum post to tell you where the fish are. You need a chart and a compass.

I ran out of Manasquan Tuesday, 39°51'N, 72°40'W over the canyon head, then worked the wall down toward 39°38'N, 72°08'W — the Claw — where the warm filament was pinched up hard against the drop. That's where we found color change you could see from the tower, green shelf water bleeding into a cobalt blue tongue, and it's exactly where the yellowfin were sitting. Trolled a mixed spread — green machines and Joe Shute bars on the short riggers, a spreader bar with naked ballyhoo down the middle, cedar plugs way back — and picked at yellowfin in the 25 to 45 pound class through the morning. Nothing explosive, but steady enough that by 11 AM we had five in the box and moved into chunking mode on the drift once the sun got high and the trolling bite went quiet, which it always does once that filament water starts to heat up on top.

Chunked butterfish and bunker on the drift right on the break at the Claw, and that's where the bigger fish showed — a 68-pound yellowfin came up on a plain hook, no weight, drifted back 60 feet into the chum slick around 1 PM. The bluefin slot fishery is still doing exactly what it's been doing since the July 3rd trophy closure pushed everybody into that 27-to-73-inch window — scattered but consistent, mostly on the temp breaks rather than concentrated schools. We picked up two in the slot on cedar plugs pulled at trolling speed just inside the warm tongue, both around 60 pounds, and I heard similar counts from boats working the Dip a little to the south, 39°33'N, 72°16'W. If you're targeting bluefin specifically, I'd fish smaller profile baits — 6-inch cedar plugs, small Ilanders — over the bigger canyon spreads, because right now the slot fish are eating smaller forage than the yellowfin are.

Mahi were a mixed bag. We found one respectable weed line drifting on the warm side of the break, maybe half a mile of scattered sargassum with two lobster pot floats tangled into it, and pulled six dolphin off it in the 6-to-12 pound range on naked ballyhoo and small green-and-yellow Ilanders. That's about what I'd call a normal late-July mahi day out there right now — not a bailing frenzy, but worth a stop if you spot the structure. Wahoo were quieter this week. We had one hit-and-miss strike on a high-speed cedar plug at first light near the Claw, no hookup, and that's been the pattern — a few boats scoring one or two on the dawn troll right on the edge of the break, nothing consistent enough to build a whole trip around yet. White marlin showed twice on the long teasers but wouldn't commit to the pitch bait either time, which tells me they're around but not concentrated — probably still working bait pockets scattered along the shelf rather than sitting tight on the wall like the tuna are.

Sea state was workable all week — 3.9 feet at a 5-second period out of the south off the bight buoy, which means it's a little sloppy on top but nothing that should keep a canyon-capable boat home. High pressure has held the whole pattern together, and that's exactly what we flagged last week — said the bite would hold steady as long as the weather stayed put, and it has. No fronts pushing through to mix that filament out or scatter the break, which is the best thing that can happen for consistency out here.

For swordfish guys: daytime drops on the slope south of the Dip, 1,200 to 1,800 feet, have been producing — squid strips and glow gear, standard deep-drop rig, best window still first light through mid-morning before the sun gets high enough to push fish deeper on the thermocline.

Looking at the next several days, the moon's waxing toward full in early August, which should tighten current through the canyon and could sharpen up the vertical jig bite for bluefin as bait gets pushed harder against the wall. If that filament holds its position — and there's no reason in this pattern to think it won't move much in 72 hours — I'd keep working the Claw and the stretch just north of the Dip on the warm side of that break. If it slides east the way these tongues sometimes do late in the week, I'd follow it out past 72°W rather than keep grinding water that's gone cold on the chart. Either way, that 7-degree line is the only number that matters right now.
