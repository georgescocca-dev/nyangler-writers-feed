# Halfway Rock steady on the neap: mackerel thick, bass working the tide seams
*Cooler Gulf of Maine water is keeping Casco Bay's striper bite going through daylight while the rest of the coast goes nocturnal.*

**PORTLAND, ME — July 21** — _by Pashmi Cooper, Zone Writer_

I was out past Peaks Island Tuesday morning, engine idling, watching a raft of gulls work a patch of water no bigger than my old lecture hall, when the surface just erupted with mackerel. Hundreds of them, maybe more, driven up by something underneath — I never did see what, though I'd wager bluefish given how the school scattered and regrouped twice before I got a line in. That's the tell for this bay right now: the bait is here, it's dense, and it's drawing everything up through the water column instead of pinning fish to the bottom after dark, which is the story everywhere south of us.

The mackerel run has been the backbone of the week. Diamond jigs — the small ones, half-ounce to an ounce, in chrome or that green-and-yellow combination — are still filling coolers off Peaks, off the eastern side of Cushing, and out toward Halfway Rock itself. Mackerel trees work too if you want volume for bait, and that's really the point: everyone I've talked to at the co-op is jigging mackerel first thing not to eat them but to have fresh bait rigged for bass and cod by mid-morning. A lively mackerel on a stinger rig, drifted along the current seam off Halfway Rock in 40 to 60 feet, has been the most consistent producer for stripers this week — better than plugs, better than soft plastics, at least when the sun's up.

And that's the piece worth understanding. The Gulf of Maine's cooler water is doing us a favor. While the Sound and the water south of the Cape have pushed their keeper bass into a dawn-dusk-and-dark pattern because the shallows are too warm to hold fish comfortably in daylight, our water hasn't hit that threshold yet. Stripers are still willing to feed at ten in the morning on the ledges off Bailey Island and around Cape Small, provided there's current moving. That's the catch this week — provided there's current moving. We're coming off the first-quarter neap, so the swings are soft, and I found the bite noticeably slower through the dead stretch of slack water than it was on the push of the flood. If you're fishing the Bailey Island ledges or the drop-off south of Jewell, plan around the tide, not the clock. An hour either side of max flow has been outfishing anything at slack by a wide margin.

Bluefish have shown up in real numbers along the same lines as the mackerel — no surprise there, since they're feeding on the same bait. I had two respectable blitzes reported to me from the Peaks Island backside and one off Two Lights, blues in the three-to-five-pound class chopping through pogies on the surface. Poppers and metal — Kastmasters, Hopkins in the one-ounce range — have been the ticket when they're up top. We flagged this pattern building last week, that bluefish would keep following bunker and staying active as long as the weather held calm and the bait didn't scatter, and that's exactly what's played out here. The stable high pressure we've had has kept the bait pods tight instead of pushing them off the structure.

Groundfish have been steadier than showy, which is about right for this time of year. Cod and haddock are still coming off the deeper structure around Halfway Rock — 70 to 90 feet on the down-current side — on clam bait and diamond jigs worked slow off bottom. Nothing to write home about in terms of size; most of what's coming over the rail is in the 18 to 24-inch range, with the occasional better haddock mixed in. I'd call it a keep-two-for-dinner fishery right now rather than a fill-the-cooler one, and anyone telling you different this week probably isn't being straight with you. The soft neap current has actually helped the bottom bite some — easier to hold position and keep bait in the strike zone without a heavy sinker dragging you off the ledge.

What surprised me a little was how quiet Harpswell Sound itself has been compared to the open ledges. I made two passes through there hoping to find bass working the mussel beds on the last of the outgoing and came up with nothing but a single schoolie. The fish seem to want the harder structure and the current lines right now — Halfway Rock, the Bailey Island ledges, the mouth of Cape Small — rather than the softer bottom inside the sound. Worth remembering if you're planning a trip based on last month's pattern.

Looking ahead, if the mackerel schools hold through this week — and there's no reason yet to think they won't, the water's clean and the bait's thick — I'd expect the bass bite off Halfway Rock and Bailey Island to stay a daylight game a while longer. The moon moves toward full over the next week, which will build the tide swings back up and likely sharpen those current-seam bites considerably. If I had one morning to fish, I'd be jigging mackerel at first light off Peaks, then running that bait straight out to the Rock to fish the last two hours of the incoming. That's where I'll be.
