# September 4 and Washington Canyon Hasn't Slowed a Step Since August
*The filament that's carried this canyon's tuna and billfish bite all summer is still sitting right where it should be heading into Labor Day week.*

**WASHINGTON CANYON, NY — September 4** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Early September is when Washington usually separates itself from the rest of the canyon string, and this year is no different. Washington sits far enough west and shallow enough on the shelf break that it typically comes alive two to three weeks before its neighbors in June, and it typically holds that warm water regime two to three weeks longer into the fall. We're right in that back half of the window now, and the canyon is behaving exactly like it should for the calendar — which is to say, better than most people expect for a September that's already seen one round of tropical noise churn through the basin.

We flagged this pattern last week and the week before that — said to expect continuity with the stable warm water and the strong tuna and billfish activity carrying over, and that's precisely what's held. The filament pushing off the main Gulf Stream axis hasn't broken down, hasn't sheared off east the way it sometimes does once Labor Day hits. That's the single biggest driver of everything happening out here right now, and it's the thing to watch hardest over the next week to ten days, because if any tropical development spins up in the western Atlantic, that's what unravels this bite fast.

For now, the eddy structure on the canyon's western edge — the wall that typically holds the better water from June through September — is still doing its job. Boats working the 100-fathom curve down through the 600 to 900-foot range along that wall are finding a mixed bag that's about as good as early September gets around here: yellowfin in the 40 to 60-pound class, scattered bigeye on the night bite, and wahoo showing up in the spread with enough regularity that crews are starting to run a dedicated high-speed lure off the shotgun. White marlin activity has stayed consistent too — not a blitz, but enough encounters on the troll that boats running dedicated spreads for them are getting looks most trips out.

The morning troll bite has been the more reliable window. Ballyhoo behind Ilanders in blue/white and green/yellow combinations are still the bread-and-butter for the yellowfin, run at a modest 6 to 7 knots with the spread staggered — shotgun and long riggers pulled back into the second and third wake, short riggers tight. Where the water color has broken clean along the edge of that western wall, the bite has been better; where it's gone off-color or mixed with cooler shelf water pushing up from below, the tuna have pushed deeper and the troll bite has gone quiet until the sun's higher. Bigeye have been coming on the drop at night, chunking bunker or butterfish over a chum slick set up along the same wall structure, fished on circle hooks with just enough weight to get down through the thermocline without spooking the bite.

Mahi have been a bonus rather than a target this week — scattered fish holding on any floating structure or weed line crossing the canyon edge, and worth a few minutes with light spinning gear and small chunks of butterfish if the crew spots color or debris on the troll. Wahoo have been the more interesting story. They've shown up consistently enough on the high-speed gear — the deep-diving lures pulled at 10 to 12 knots off the corner riggers — that a few boats have started rigging a second wahoo bait specifically for that stretch of wall where the temperature break sets up tightest.

Tide and moon are cooperating without doing anything dramatic. We're building toward the full moon over the next several days, which means tidal flow on the surface will start tightening up bait concentrations, and that's generally a good thing for the troll bite because it keeps forage pinned against structure instead of scattered across open water. Wind has been the more manageable variable lately — nothing that's kept the fleet home, though anyone running out should still check the marine forecast morning of, because a building sea on top of a long run adds up fast this far out.

The honest read: this isn't a blowtorch bite where every spread gets crushed on the first pass. It's a solid, dependable September pattern — multiple hookups per trip on the better days, a mixed bag of species, and enough bigeye and wahoo mixed in with the yellowfin to make the run worthwhile. The days where the water color turns or the wind kicks up out of the wrong quadrant have been noticeably tougher, and crews that pushed through those stretches without adjusting the drift or the spread depth came home lighter.

Looking ahead, the next 48 to 72 hours should mirror what's already working — stable warm water along the western wall, a troll bite that turns on with the first real light and holds through mid-morning, and a night chunk bite for bigeye that's been worth the extra hours for boats willing to sit on the slick. The bigger question is Labor Day week itself and whatever's brewing in the tropics. If that filament holds, Washington keeps punching above its weight the way it always does this time of year. If a storm system starts organizing anywhere in the basin, that's the first sign the party's closer to ending than starting.
