# Cupsogue Bar Held Its New Shape — And the Fluke Found It
*The sand that piled up after last month's blow is still there, still funneling bait, and this week it started paying off on the flood.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — August 3** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

I ran the drift line off Cupsogue three times this week with a handheld and a piece of chalk in my head, tracing the edge that formed when that late-July blow pushed sand east into the throat of the inlet. Last week I told you that edge was worth working on the building flood. It held. The bar hasn't moved more than a few yards since, which for this inlet is practically frozen in place — Moriches likes to rearrange itself every time you turn your back, but this particular cut has stabilized into a real piece of structure, with a six-to-nine foot trough running parallel to the new shoal and a hard current seam right where the two depths meet.

That's where the fluke have been sitting. Not stacked, not a slaughter, but a real, repeatable bite on the last two hours of the incoming into the top of the tide, drifting Gulp Swimming Mullets in white and pink shad, 3/4-ounce bucktails tipped with squid strips, right along that seam in 7 to 10 feet of water. I had four keepers to 21 inches Tuesday morning working that exact stretch, and I watched two other boats do the same thing without anyone saying a word to each other — everybody's found the same seam because the bottom is telling everybody the same story right now. The mistake guys are making is drifting too fast on a hard flood. Slow the boat, use enough weight to feel bottom every third or fourth crank, and hold that bucktail right on the transition rather than letting the current sweep you off it into the flat sand where there's nothing.

Bass are a different animal right now, and the ocean side has a lot to do with it. Offshore buoy readings put the open Atlantic in the mid-70s with a leftover three-foot swell still running out of the southeast — enough chop that the ocean bar at Cupsogue has been unproductive for daytime casting, too roiled for the bass to hold comfortably against the beach in daylight. So they're doing what they do every August: sliding into the inlet's deeper cuts and the bay-side structure during the day, and coming up on the ebb after dark to work the bait that gets flushed out of the marsh creeks. I fished the night ebb Thursday off the rock jetty at the west end of the inlet with a black darter and a bone SP Minnow, and connected with three fish in the 26-to-29 inch range in under two hours, all on the second half of the drain when the current really gets moving through the throat. That's the pattern I'd lean on again this week — Tuthill Point's channel edge and the bridge structure up toward Westhampton are holding fish for the same reason, current compression plus low light. Bucktails with a curly-tail trailer, worked slow along bottom, have out-produced anything fished fast.

Bluefish have been the wildcard, exactly like I flagged. No sustained blitz yet inside the bay, but there's been bait around — bunker pods reforming tight to the beach in scattered pockets between Cupsogue and Smith Point, and when one of those pods gets pinned against the bar you get five minutes of total chaos and then it's over. I had one of those mornings this week, ran into breaking blues off the ocean side for about ten minutes on a topwater popper before they vanished back into deeper water. If you're going to chase that, keep a rod rigged with a heavier wire leader ready at all times and don't waste time on long casts — get tight to the boil.

Weakfish are still scattered rather than concentrated, mostly picked up as bycatch on the fluke drift over the channel edges inside the bay near Tuthill Point, a few keeper-sized fish mixed in with a lot of shorts. Porgies are steady but unglamorous over the hard bottom patches east of the Inlet — small pieces of clam on a hi-lo rig will keep a rod bent if the bigger stuff isn't cooperating, good option for a slow morning. Blackfish season's still off for most of you, but I'm seeing fish stacking on the deeper rock piles already, worth remembering for when the season opens.

Heading into next week, the tides are sliding toward the neap around August 5-6, and that changes the equation. Weaker current means the fluke bite on that Cupsogue edge will need a longer window — fish the peak of the flood rather than the whole cycle, because there won't be enough push to hold bait on the seam outside of that peak hour or two. For the bass, softer current usually means they tuck even tighter to the deepest, coolest structure — the Gut-adjacent holes and the bridge pilings become the most reliable bet, and I'd lighten up bucktails to 3/4 ounce so you can work them slower without losing bottom feel. If the ocean lays down at all behind this swell, the bar at Cupsogue is worth a dawn look for bass and blues working bait tight to the beach — that's always been this inlet's best-kept surprise, and it takes zero warning to happen.
