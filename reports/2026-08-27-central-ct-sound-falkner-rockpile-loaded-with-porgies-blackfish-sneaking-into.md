# Falkner Rockpile Loaded with Porgies, Blackfish Sneaking Into the Mix
*The bottom bite is carrying Central Sound through the August lull while stripers wait for dark and a cooler tide.*

**MILFORD, CT — August 27** — _by Lena Marchetti, Zone Writer_

Regulars working the outer New Haven breakwall Tuesday morning had green crab hi-lo rigs bouncing bottom in eighteen feet on the last of the flood and couldn't keep porgies off the hook long enough to check for sea bass — until a 15-inch black hit the same rig and settled the question. That's been the story on the rockpiles all week: a steady, unglamorous, very catchable bite that's carrying this stretch of Sound through a stretch of warm water that's put everything else on a slower clock.

The thermal picture explains a lot of what's going on right now. Kings Point is reading 70.9 degrees, New London down east is 69.4, and that west-to-east gradient means Central Sound is sitting in the warmest water on the beat. Warm surface water like that pushes daytime feeding down onto structure and shuts the door on much of the open-water action, which is exactly why the rockpiles at Falkner Island, the New Haven breakwalls, and Stratford Shoal have been the most consistent producers of the week while everything pelagic stays quiet until the sun goes down.

Striped bass are in their predictable late-August pattern — daytime fish holding tight to structure and river mouths, real activity waiting for dark. We flagged this a week ago: that the moon building toward full on August 28 would strengthen the current sweeping bait around the harder structure, and that's precisely what's setting up now. The tide swings at Charles Island and the bar off Milford Gulf have been building noticeably day over day, and the night crowd working bucktails and swim shads over the Charles Island sandbar on the last two hours of the ebb has been finding better numbers of schoolie-to-mid-30-inch bass than they were two weeks back. This isn't the fall run yet — that push doesn't really get going until nighttime water temps start dropping hard, likely mid-September — but the current is doing its job concentrating bait, and that's worth chasing after sundown.

On the rockpiles, porgies remain the easy money. Falkner Island's boulder fields in 25 to 35 feet are holding good numbers on green crab and squid strips fished on a two-hook bottom rig with just enough weight to hold in the current — an ounce and a half on the last of the ebb, up to three ounces once the tide really gets moving with this building moon. The New Haven breakwalls, both the outer and the shorter inner wall, are producing similar action in 15 to 20 feet, and they're an easier pull for anyone without a long ride in them. Sea bass are mixing in as a bonus rather than a target right now — legal fish to 17 inches coming up on the same rigs, with the better ones tending to show on the deeper edges of the rockpiles rather than the shallow tops.

Blackfish are just starting to trickle into the conversation, which is the first real sign the season is beginning to turn even if the water hasn't caught up yet. Early fish are coming off the rockier stretches around Stratford Shoal and the deeper structure off Falkner on green crab, mostly incidental catches while working porgy rigs, but it's enough that a few boats are starting to rig heavier tackle and fish crab exclusively on the last hour of the tide. Give it two to three weeks of cooling water before that becomes a real target fishery, but it's worth keeping a rod rigged for it now.

Bluefish are doing what bluefish do — showing up everywhere and complicating everyone's leaders. Snapper blues are thick in New Haven Harbor and around the Milford docks, small enough to be more nuisance than target but a good sign for what's coming. Cocktail-sized blues in the 2- to 4-pound class are mixing into the porgy and sea bass catches on the rockpiles and occasionally crashing bait on the surface off Hammonasset in the evening, worth a few casts with a small metal if you see them working.

Fluke are the softest part of the report right now. The warm, thin water has spread fish out across the flats rather than concentrating them, and catch rates off Hammonasset and the shallower stretches near Charles Island have been inconsistent at best. The better fish have been coming from channel edges and deeper structure — the drop-offs around Stratford Shoal and the deeper cuts near the Gulf — where a cooler bottom layer is holding fish tighter. White Gulp on a bucktail, dragged slow through those deeper edges on the outgoing tide, has been the more reliable approach than working the shallow flats.

Looking ahead, the moon goes full Wednesday and that should sharpen every tide-dependent bite window on the beat. Expect the current at Charles Island and the New Haven breakwalls to swing harder over the next few days, which should improve both the night striper bite and the rockpile bottom fishing as bait gets pushed and pinned against structure more aggressively. If overnight temperatures start easing even slightly by the weekend, look for the first hints of improved daytime striper activity — not a full turn, but an early crack in the summer pattern. Until then, the smart play is simple: porgies and sea bass on the rockpiles during the day, bass after dark on the structure, and a rod rigged for blackfish just in case the early ones keep showing up.
