# July 20 and Washington Is Already Fishing Like It's August
*The new moon spring current we flagged last week hit the shelf break right on schedule, and now the eddy's holding yellowfin and bigeye tighter to the canyon than they usually sit for another three weeks.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 20** — _by Capt. Chris "Wash" Morrison, Zone Writer_

Every year around this time I get the same question at the fuel dock: why does Washington always seem to turn on before Hudson, before Baltimore, before the boys up north even start talking tuna. It's not luck. It's geography. Washington sits closer to where the Gulf Stream throws its meanders inshore, and when one of those warm-water eddies breaks off and slides up over the canyon, we get first crack at it while everybody else is still waiting on their water to turn blue. This year the eddy showed up early and it showed up close — inside the 100-fathom curve on the north wall, not way out on the break like a normal mid-July setup. That's a two-to-three-week jump on the calendar, and it's why boats running out of Cape May and Ocean City have had the shortest steam to warm, clean water of any fleet on the coast right now.

We called this one. Said the new moon spring tide would push hard current across the shelf break and stack bait right where the temperature line sits, and that's exactly what happened. The tidal forcing peaked right on schedule and it bait on both walls — not just the usual squid and flying fish showing on the meter, but real concentrations of small mahi holding under the pods, which tells you the whole ecosystem out there woke up at once, not just the tuna.

The fishing itself has been solid, not spectacular — and I'll be straight with you, it's been a week where the boats that worked the temperature break instead of just running numbers put up better counts. My best afternoons have come working the color change along the north wall, roughly 39°18'N, 71°42'W out to the 39°12'N line, trolling a spread of medium ballyhoo behind Islanders and a couple of naked skirted baits down the middle. Yellowfin have been running 35 to 55 pounds, mixed in with a few bigeye pushing 100-plus on the long riggers fished deep behind the pattern. Wahoo have been the bonus fish — high-speed trolling a rigged Braid Runner or a Yo-Zuri bonito along the edge of the eddy where the water goes from green to that ink blue in about a hundred yards has produced fish in the 40 to 60 pound class, usually first thing at light or right at the temp break itself.

Night bite has been the more consistent producer if you want a sure thing over a home-run swing. Anchoring up on the south wall in 900 to 1200 feet, setting up a chunk line with butterflied bunker and a few chunks of squid mixed in, has been drawing steady yellowfin after dark, especially on the tide change. That's classic Washington behavior — the current on the spring tide runs hard enough to hold the chunk slick tight and keep bait in the zone instead of blowing it out into nothing. White marlin have shown up in the spread too, mostly on the troll during daylight hours working ballyhoo skipping just off the surface — nothing stacked, but enough action on the long and short riggers that I'd keep a pitch bait rigged and ready if you're running a spread out here this week.

I won't oversell it. Some boats have had quiet mornings where the bite just didn't show even with good-looking water — that's canyon fishing, and Washington isn't immune to it just because it runs early. The days that produced were the ones where guys worked the edges patiently instead of blasting through looking for birds. Bait has been thick enough that the fish don't have to move much, which means you have to go find them rather than wait for them to come find you.

Looking ahead, the first-quarter neap hits tomorrow, and that's going to soften the current across the shelf break for a few days. Historically that widens the bite window rather than shutting it down — less brutal drift speed means baits track cleaner and the fish don't have to fight as hard to feed, so I'd expect the yellowfin and bigeye numbers to keep building through the back half of the week rather than fade. If the eddy holds its position over the canyon like it has the last several days, I'd keep working that north wall temperature break early and slide to the chunk on the south side after dark. If it slides east the way these things sometimes do heading into August, I'll be adjusting toward the deeper break and you should too. Either way, Washington's running a full season ahead of pace, and for those of us who've fished this canyon long enough to know its rhythm, that's not a surprise — that's just what this piece of water does when the Stream decides to lean our way.
