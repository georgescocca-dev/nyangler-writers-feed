# New moon springs reopen the sunset flood bite from Stratford Shoal to Norwalk
*Big tides, warm bay water pushing fluke to the inlets, and the first whispers of bonito on the way — here's how Central Sound sets up this week.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 10** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

Last week the Sound was coasting toward the new moon, and you could feel the tides going slack and lazy the way they do in that dead zone between quarters. Bay water in the back harbors — Oyster Bay, Northport, the upper reaches off Port Jeff — pushed into the upper 70s, which is warm enough to shut down a lot of the shallow structure fishing and warm enough to send fluke looking for cooler water. That's exactly what happened. The fluke that were spread through the bays two weeks ago have been sliding toward the channels and the harbor mouths, stacking where the moving water stays a few degrees cooler than the flats around it. Stripers did the same dance in a different key — daytime bites got tougher and shorter, and the real activity slid into the last hour of light and the first hour of dark, which is where it's stayed.

Now the moon's gone new, and that means springs — the biggest tidal swings we'll see until the next new moon. Bigger swings mean more water moving through the pinch points that make this stretch of Sound what it is: Stratford Shoal, the Norwalk Islands gaps, the flow around Charles Island. More current means more bait getting pushed and concentrated, and more current means predictable current lines for bass and blues to sit in. The real prize this week is timing — high stand at the western end of the Sound is converging on sunset right now, and that alignment holds through about Sunday. That's the first evening flood-into-dark window we've had line up like this since the June full moon, and in my book it's the best few days of fishing we'll get before the dog days set in.

On the striper side, the Stratford Shoal rip has been the most consistent piece of water in my zone. Fish 18 to 34 feet on the drop-off edges, working bucktails — 1.5 to 2 ounce white or chartreuse, tipped with a strip of squid — down through the rip on the last two hours of the outgoing, then switching to live eels or bunker chunks as the tide turns and slows into the evening flood. That current seam where the shoal water meets the deeper channel is holding bait, and the bass using it have run mostly mid-20s to low 30-inch fish with a scattering of better ones mixed in — nothing extraordinary, but honest, repeatable action if you're willing to work the tide correctly instead of just anchoring and hoping.

The Norwalk Islands have been a mixed bag, and I'll be straight about that. Some evenings the rock piles around Sheffield and Cockenoe have been stacked with blues in the 3-5 pound class hammering topwater poppers and SP Minnows worked fast over the structure at dusk — that bite has been reliable in the last ninety minutes of light. Other evenings it's been dead water with nothing showing on the surface at all. The difference seems to be bait presence more than anything else — when there are peanut bunker pushed against the rocks, the blues show up on cue; when the bait's scattered, so are the fish. Bring bunker or peanut-imitating swimmers and be ready to move between spots rather than committing to one rock pile for the whole tide.

Fluke have been the more dependable story this week, if a less flashy one. With the bays running warm, the better drifts have been right at the mouths — the Port Jeff Harbor entrance, the Oyster Bay channel out past the Cold Spring Harbor split, and the deeper cuts around Charles Island. I've had my best results in 20 to 35 feet dragging 4-inch white or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, slow and tight to bottom on the last of the outgoing when the current is still moving enough to keep the bait tracking straight but not so hard you're bouncing bottom. Fish have run mostly 16 to 20 inches with the occasional four-pounder mixed in — solid eating-size fluke, not a doormat parade, but consistent enough to build a trip around.

Porgies remain the honest bread-and-butter fish right now — anchor up on any of the rock piles off Middle Ground Light or the Norwalk chain, drop small hooks baited with sandworm or clam on a high-low rig, and you'll load a cooler without much drama. It's not glamorous but it's real fishing, and on a slow bass evening it's what saves the trip.

On weakfish, I'm not going to pretend there's anything happening — I haven't heard a credible report out of the Sound in weeks, and the pattern down south has been the same story: warm water alone isn't bringing them back. That threshold theory needed more than temperature, and it hasn't delivered.

The thing I'm watching hardest right now is the pelagic push. The first Long Island bonito and Spanish mackerel reports of the season are trickling in from the ocean side around Moriches and Shinnecock, right on the calendar I'd expect. That's early — usually we don't see bonito pressure into the Sound proper until deeper into August, once the bait gets squeezed through the Race and Plum Gap. If that warm push holds and widens the way it has further east, I'd start paying attention to the eastern mouth of the Sound by late month. For now, my time this weekend goes to the sunset flood at Stratford Shoal while the tide alignment is right, with the Norwalk rock piles as my backup if the blues show. If the current setup holds through Monday, this is as good a stretch as we'll get until the next moon does the same trick in August.
