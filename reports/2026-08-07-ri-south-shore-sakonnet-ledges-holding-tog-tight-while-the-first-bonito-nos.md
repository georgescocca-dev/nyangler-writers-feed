# Sakonnet Ledges Holding Tog Tight While the First Bonito Nose Into Charlestown Breachway
*Green crabs are disappearing off the rocks on the slack, and the bait pushing through Charlestown says the run might start early this year.*

**NEWPORT, RI — August 8** — _by Billy Tanaka, Zone Writer_

Walked the base of the cliffs below Second Beach Tuesday morning and the water was full of silversides, tight against the rock, moving in that nervous little shimmer they get when something's under them. Couldn't tell you what — the water's murky enough this week that I never got a visual — but that kind of bait behavior along the wash usually means bass are working underneath, and by the second cast on a black Slug-Go I had my answer. Nothing big, mid-20s mostly, but they were there, tucked against the base of the rock where the wash meets the ledge.

That's been the story all along this stretch since the middle of July — stripers holding deep and tight to structure, feeding at night or right at the edges of light, not showing themselves in the middle of the day. We flagged this pattern a week back and said it'd hold until either the water cooled or the new moon tide stirred things up. Well, the new moon's Wednesday, and you can already feel the tidal range building. Second Beach, the rocks off Gooseberry, the ledges under Sachuest — all of it's pushing more current on this week's tides than it was ten days ago, and the fish know it before we do.

Tog fishing has been the most reliable thing going. I've been working the ledges off Jamestown — the rockpile off Fort Wetherill, the drop just outside Mackerel Cove — dropping green crabs on a simple two-hook rig, 2 to 3 ounces depending on the drift, right into the cracks on the last hour of the ebb into dead slack. That slack window is everything with tog. Too much current and you're just getting your rig swept off the structure; give me that quiet 20-30 minutes either side of the turn and I'm picking fish out of the same three or four cracks every trip. Nothing huge yet — mostly 14 to 17 inches, a few pushing 20 — but they're eating well and they're not hard to find if you know which rock holds them. The ledge just north of the Jamestown Bridge on the east side has been quietly good, deeper water, maybe 18 to 25 feet, less crowded than the usual spots.

Now for the part everybody's been asking about. Charlestown Breachway has bait pushing through it on every tide right now — bunker peanuts and some early silversides getting funneled through that cut, and Tuesday evening I watched something come through and slash at the surface right at the mouth on the last of the outgoing. Didn't hook up, didn't even get a real look, but that kind of surface commotion in early August at a breachway mouth has one likely explanation this time of year, and the intel I'm hearing out of the Buzzards Bay side backs it up — bonito have already crossed over and they're pushing this way on the warm water riding in with these building tides. I wouldn't call it a run yet. One push of bait, one flash of something working it, that's not a green light — but it's exactly the kind of signal that tells you to start carrying a bonito rod in the truck instead of leaving it home.

If you want to be there when it happens, Watch Hill and Napatree are the two spots I'd have eyes on this week. Napatree because the current wraps the point hard on the ebb and pushes bait right along that outer bar, and Watch Hill because the reef off the point holds structure that current-run bait always finds. Small metal — half-ounce to an ounce, Deadly Dicks or Kastmasters in that silver/chartreuse pattern — is what you want rigged and ready. Cast into the froth, don't overthink the retrieve, just get it moving.

Bluefish have been showing some life again after being quiet most of July, which tracks with what's been happening on deeper structure elsewhere in the region. I picked up a few choppers to 6 pounds off the Sakonnet rocks near the Point, working poppers on the last hour of daylight into dusk. Not blitz conditions, just steady enough that if you're out there for stripers or albies you should have a bluefish plug tied on as backup, because they'll eat before the bass show and save an otherwise slow hour.

Fluke has been the disappointment this week. I've made a couple of drifts off Point Judith and through the deeper water outside Sakonnet Point and it's mostly shorts, a few keepers mixed in but nothing to write home about. Water's warm enough that the bigger fish may have pushed to cooler, deeper structure than I was fishing. Scup fishing, on the other hand, has been a nice consolation prize — drop a piece of clam or squid strip around any of the Jamestown or Newport bridge structure and you'll have a bucket of dinner fish in under an hour, which isn't glamorous but it's honest fishing when everything else is being stubborn.

Looking at the week ahead — the new moon Wednesday is going to push the tidal range hard, and that's the ingredient this whole zone has been waiting on. The tog bite should stay steady through the stronger currents as long as you're patient for that slack window. The striper pattern should sharpen at current-driven spots like the Jamestown Bridge rips and the Sakonnet Point rocks as the flow builds. And if that bonito push we saw a hint of Tuesday is real, the next four or five days around the new moon tides are exactly when it should show itself hard at Charlestown, Napatree, and Watch Hill. I'd have a bonito outfit rigged before I'd bother chasing fluke this week — the odds are better, and the payoff's a lot more fun.
