# Fort Trumbull current sharpens ahead of Thursday's full moon — bass working the seams after dark
*Tide swings are widening through New London Harbor, and the bite windows at the bridge and the fort are getting tighter and better defined.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 27** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

The current off Fort Trumbull has a different feel to it this week. Stand on the seawall at the state park on a strong ebb and you can watch the rip line stretch further out into the channel than it did two weeks ago — the moon's building toward full on Thursday, and that means bigger tidal swings pushing more water through the harbor mouth. Anyone who's spent time on this river knows what that does: it tightens up the seams, concentrates the bait, and turns lazy structure fish into predictable current feeders. We flagged this pattern last week — said the zone would see a bump in activity as the moon built toward full and the current strengthened — and that's exactly what's setting up over the next several tides.

Water on this end of the Sound is running cooler than the western reaches, New London reading in the high 60s, which keeps this fishery in its typical late-August holding pattern rather than anything migratory. That's an important distinction for anyone expecting a fall-run bite already — it isn't here yet. The stripers working the Thames right now are residents and holdovers keying on current and bait concentration, not fish staging for a push south. That push doesn't usually start in earnest until the nighttime air really starts dropping, which for this river means mid-to-late September. Until then, the game is reading the tide and fishing the structure that funnels bait past a fixed point.

The most consistent word off the docks this week has been snapper blues — thick in the upper harbor around the marinas, off City Pier, and up into the mouth of the river past the Gold Star Bridge. These aren't the cocktail blues that show up on the troll offshore; they're the summer snappers that pile into New London Harbor every August, chasing peanut bunker and silversides in tight against pilings and floating docks. Small silver spoons, quarter-ounce Kastmasters, or a sliver of mackerel on a long-shank hook under a bobber will connect all day, but the best window is the last two hours of the incoming when the bait gets pinned against the bulkheads.

Porgies are holding to form on the deeper structure — the rock piles off Pleasure Beach, the drop-off along the Ocean Beach jetty, and the mussel-covered rip-rap under the Gold Star Bridge pilings in 15 to 25 feet. Sandworms or clam strips on a high-low rig, fished on the bottom during the slower stages of the tide, are still the go-to. The bite's been steady rather than spectacular — a mixed bag of keeper-class porgies with a few throwbacks mixed in, best worked slow with light line so you can feel the subtle pickup before they spit the bait.

Striped bass are the story worth watching closest right now, and they're doing what this river's bass do every summer when the current sharpens: sliding into the seams after dark. The Fort Trumbull rip on the back half of the ebb has been holding fish in 18 to 30 feet, stacked up where the fresh water dumping out of the Thames meets the harder salt current sweeping past the point. Bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig, or live eels drifted slow through the seam, have been the producers, mostly from an hour before high tide through the first three hours of the ebb. Under the Gold Star Bridge, the pilings themselves are worth working on the same stage of tide — bass tuck in tight to the structure waiting on baitfish getting swept through, and a bucktail bounced along the base of the piling on a slow retrieve, or a swimming plug worked the seam line where lit water meets shadow, has been drawing strikes after dark.

Hickory shad have been scarce this late in the season, which tracks — that's more of a spring pattern up this river, and by late August they've largely thinned out. Not worth planning a trip around right now.

Looking at the next several days, the widening tide swings heading into Thursday's full moon should keep sharpening these bite windows rather than flattening them out. If there's only one evening available this week, the smart play is the Fort Trumbull rip starting an hour before the top of the tide and fishing through the first real push of the ebb — that's when the current does the work and stacks bait against the point. Keep a bunker chunk rig as Plan A and a bucktail as Plan B in case the fish are holding tighter to structure than the open seam. Wind's been cooperative and shouldn't be the deciding factor; it's the tide and the moon doing the heavy lifting here. Once nighttime temperatures start easing off in a few weeks, expect this holding pattern to give way to the real fall movement — but for now, work the current, work it after dark, and don't expect the fishing to hand itself to you in daylight.
