# Valiant Rock at 4.1 knots: bass still biting daylight while the rest of the Sound goes nocturnal
*Hard current keeps The Race and Plum Gut cool and honest even as Kings Point pushes past 70 — plus the first real bonito chatter building toward the Aug 12 new moon.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 3** — _by Capt. Sabrina Cole, Zone Writer_

4.1 knots. That's what the ebb was doing off Valiant Rock on Tuesday afternoon, and it's the number that tells you everything about why this rip is still fishing during the day while the rest of the Sound has gone to bed. Slack this week is running roughly twenty minutes either side of the tide charts for New London — call it a ten-minute true window if you want clean bottom contact with wire. Miss it by much and you're bouncing bucktail off rock at an angle that'll cost you gear and fish both.

Here's the thing everybody west of us is dealing with right now: Kings Point is reading 70.9, and stripers in that kind of water go deep and nocturnal because daytime surface temps in the low-to-mid 70s just aren't comfortable for them to feed up top. Back in the bays and harbors, guys are switching to night trips because that's the only way to connect. We don't have that problem the same way out here, and it's not luck — it's mechanics. The Race and Plum Gut are running 4, sometimes 5 knots through a rock garden that drops to 100-plus feet in places. That kind of current doesn't let the water column sit still long enough to stratify. New London proper is reading 69.4, a degree and a half cooler than Kings Point, and down in the guts of the rip itself, on the bottom where the wire line lives, it's colder still. The turbulence is basically doing the bass a favor — keeping the whole column mixed and feedable around the clock, which is exactly why daytime bass fishing on wire and bucktails still works here when it's shutting down everywhere else in the Sound.

We're running 20 to 28-ounce wire drops off Valiant Rock and Race Rock on the last two hours of the ebb, bucktails in white and chartreuse, 1.5 to 2 ounces, tipped with a sassy shad or a strip of squid when the current's really honking. Depth is 40 to 70 feet depending on where you sit relative to the rockpile, and you want to feel that jig ticking bottom, not swinging free — if it's swinging free you're too shallow in the water column for this current speed and you'll skip right over the fish. Sizes have been solid, most fish 28 to 36 inches, with a few better ones mixed in on the shift from ebb to slack when bait gets pinned against the rock. Bluefish are riding shotgun on almost every drift right now — there's a real wave of them pushing down from the north this year, and Plum Gut and the Sluiceway have both been getting worked over pretty good on top when the tide's ripping. If you want to save your bucktails, throw a heavier metal — something like a diamond jig or a Deadly Dick — into the boils and let the blues have that instead.

Fluke have been the honest bite this week — not a blitz, just steady. We flagged this pattern already: keepers mixed with shorts on structure drifts, and that's exactly what Valiant Rock and the sandy edges off Great Gull Island have been giving up. Four-inch Gulp in pink shad or white on a half-ounce to one-ounce bucktail, dragged slow across 25 to 35 feet of sand adjacent to the rock, has been putting keeper fluke in the boat at a rate of maybe one in four or five fish. Not a limit trip, but a dependable one if you're willing to grind through the shorts. Sea bass are the bonus fish on the same drifts — drop a Gulp or a squid strip tight to the structure itself, right on the drop-off where rock meets sand, and you'll pick off a few 15 to 17-inch fish without even trying.

Now the one everybody's been asking about: bonito. We said last week that the water at Montauk, Block Island, and the Vineyard corridor had already crossed the seasonal threshold and it was just a matter of the tides building enough current to trigger the first real push. That's exactly the setup we're in now — Montauk's holding at 69.1, and with the new moon on August 12 stacking the tides day by day, current strength through Plum Gut and the Race approaches is only going to increase between now and then. I haven't had a rod bend on bonito yet this week, but the bait's showing on the meter around Little Gull and Great Gull in tighter, nervier schools than we saw two weeks ago, and that's usually the tell. I'd have an epoxy jig rig ready — half-ounce to three-quarter-ounce, fished on light fluoro, worked fast on a rip or slack edge — and I'd be checking the Gut every session from here through the new moon.

Look-ahead: if the tides build the way they should into next week, expect the current through The Race and Plum Gut to strengthen noticeably, which should tighten up the slack windows even further — plan your bass trips around ten-minute pockets, not twenty. The bass and blue bite should hold steady on wire and bucktails as long as that mixing keeps the rip cooler than the rest of the Sound. And if the bonito or albies are going to show anywhere first, my money's on the Gut and the water around Great Gull sometime in the next seven to ten days, as that building current finally gives them a reason to commit.
