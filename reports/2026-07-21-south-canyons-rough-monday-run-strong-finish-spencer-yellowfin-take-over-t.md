# Rough Monday Run, Strong Finish: Spencer Yellowfin Take Over the Troll by Thursday
*A sloppy start off Cape May gave way to a building bigeye and yellowfin bite once the swell laid down and the warm water held its ground on the edge.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — July 21** — _by Capt. Ray Lugo, Zone Writer_

If I had to grade this week, I'd call it a C-minus Monday and an A-minus Thursday through Sunday. That's the canyon game for you — you run four different pieces of bottom out of Cape May and Ocean City, and the one that's dead on Tuesday is stacked by Friday. Early in the week we were fighting a leftover groundswell, 3 to 4 feet at a short, chunky 5-second period out past the Bight — the kind of sea state that makes a 60-mile run to Norfolk feel like a chore and turns the ride to Spencer into a rodeo. I had two boats bail on Poor Man's Tuesday morning because the ride out was uglier than the fishing was going to be worth. But the water underneath that chop never went anywhere, and once the wind backed off midweek, the bite caught up fast.

We flagged this last week — said the bite would hold as long as the offshore water stayed put — and that's exactly what happened. The warm push riding up out of the Stream never backed off, and once the sea state settled, boats running out to the 50 and 60 fathom curve off Spencer found bait stacked and fish willing. That's the canyon that carried the week. Daytime trolling around the edge — I'm talking the drop from about 38°32'N, 73°50'W down along the break — produced yellowfin in the 35 to 55 pound class on a mixed spread: naked green machines and cedar plugs up front, a couple of small Islander/ballyhoo combos in the shotgun, running 6 to 8 knots right on the color change where the green Jersey water meets that cleaner blue. When you see birds working bait balls on top and the water temp needle jump a degree and a half in a mile, that's your green light to put the spread in.

Night chunking stayed the bigeye play, same as it always is down here. Anchoring or drifting on the northeast side of the canyon with butterfish chunks and a couple of whole squid mixed in, running long fluorocarbon leaders — 100 to 130 pound — down to a 6/0 circle hook, glow stick clipped a rod length up. The bite windows were tight, mostly that first hour after dark and again right before first light, but the fish that came aboard were quality, several pushing well past 150 pounds. Patience wins that game — you can burn through a slow four hours before the current line sets up right and the fish start feeding under the boat.

Bluefin mixed in right on top of the yellowfin grounds at Spencer and pushed a little into Lindenkohl too, mostly slot-class fish in that 27 to 47 inch window everybody's still working with since the trophy fish closed out July 3rd. Nothing wrong with that — a two-fish limit on 40-pound bluefin eating the same trolled ballyhoo as the yellowfin is a good problem to have, and several boats filled out mixed bags without ever switching gear.

Mahi were the wildcard, same as they always are in late July. Found some solid chicken dolphin and a handful of slammers pushing 15 pounds around any bit of floating structure — lobster pot buoys, weed lines, a broken-up patch of sargassum drifting off the edge at Poor Man's. Small chartreuse feathers and a naked ballyhoo worked when we slowed the spread down to check structure, and it's worth having a rod rigged and ready any time you spot color change or floating junk, because those fish show up fast and disappear just as quick.

Wahoo came mostly on the run — high-speed trolling with black and purple Ilanders and cedar plugs while transiting between grounds, especially crossing structure edges at Lindenkohl and the deeper contour lines south toward Norfolk. Nobody's targeting them exclusively down here yet, but enough came over the rail on the troll-through that it's worth a couple of extra rods rigged heavy while you're running.

White marlin are the story building underneath all of it. We called this one too — said the billfish migration was building through July, and Poor Man's threw a few flags this week, along with some raised fish at Norfolk that never committed. Switch-bait tactics with dink ballyhoo and a naked circle hook teaser worked better than straight dead-bait spreads; these early-run fish are still a little green and want to be teased before they eat.

Looking ahead, if the high pressure holds through the coming week like it has, I'd keep leaning on Spencer for the bulk of the day-trip yellowfin and bigeye work, with Poor Man's as the marlin play if you've got the extra fuel and daylight. Norfolk's worth the long run if Spencer gets crowded or the fish slide south with the bait — that's happened before in late July. Watch the wind forecast closer than the tide chart this week; we're sliding into a neap cycle that'll soften current on the inside stuff, but out here it's the sea state, not the moon, that decides whether you're fishing or just riding.
