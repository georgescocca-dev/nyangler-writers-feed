# Marsh Grass Turning, Bunker Sliding Downriver — Stripers Stacking Off Great Island
*The peanut bunker exodus is on, water's holding in the high 60s, and the first real push of fall bass is showing at the river mouth.*

**OLD SAYBROOK, CT — September 14** — _by Walt Jensen, Zone Writer_

The cordgrass out on Great Island is turning that first dull bronze it gets before it goes full gold, and that's always been my cue that the river's flipping seasons on us. Spring shad run is a memory now — the herring and shad pushed through back in April and May, and what we're watching this week is the other half of the calendar: bait pulling out of the marsh creeks and sliding down toward the mouth, with striped bass starting to organize behind it the way they do every September.

We flagged this last week — said the staging would intensify as bunker schools tightened up ahead of the fall run, and that's exactly what the last several days have shown. Peanut bunker have been evacuating the coves off Old Lyme and the flats behind Calves Island in tight, nervous pods, and there are stripers shadowing them now instead of just picking off stragglers the way they were in late August. Water temperature at the mouth is sitting in that comfortable high-60s range — nothing dramatic, but cool enough that bass are getting serious about feeding instead of just surviving the summer heat. That's the shift. It's not a blitz yet. It's a build.

The better fish this week have come from the classic fall spots: the rock pile off Saybrook Point on the last two hours of the ebb, the deep bend by the Amtrak bridge in Old Lyme, and the channel edge running out past DEP Marina toward the outer bar. Nothing huge — most of what's coming over the rail is 18 to 26 inches, with a scattered slot fish mixed in for anglers working the deeper cuts at first light. Bucktails in white or chartreuse, half-ounce to an ounce depending on current, dragged slow along bottom on the last of the outgoing, have been the most consistent producer. A few boats working live-lined peanut bunker off Great Island on a fish-finder rig have done better on numbers, especially right where the marsh creeks dump into the river channel — that's where the bait is getting squeezed and the bass know it.

Snapper blues have been thick in the coves around Essex and up into the mouth of the Lieutenant River, which is normal for mid-September and worth mentioning because they'll eat almost anything small and shiny — a piece of Kastmaster or a tiny bucktail teaser under a popping cork will keep kids and light-tackle guys busy for hours. The adult bluefish haven't shown in real numbers yet this far up the estuary; that push tends to lag a couple weeks behind the striper staging, and with the bigger blue activity concentrated more toward the open Sound and Montauk right now, I wouldn't expect a serious cocktail blue run inside the river mouth until the water eases another few degrees.

White perch fishing has been steady but unspectacular — small jigs and grass shrimp working the deeper holes up around Essex and the Selden Neck stretch, best on the last of the flood when the current slows enough for fish to hold. It's not the story of the week, but it's a dependable option on days when the tide or the wind doesn't favor the mouth.

Tide-wise, the last two hours of the outgoing have been the window that matters most right now. As bait clears out of the back creeks on the ebb, it funnels past the rock structure at Saybrook Point and along the edges of the Great Island flats, and that's where the bass are waiting rather than chasing bait out into open water. Wind has been the wildcard — a stiff southwest breeze the last few afternoons has put some chop on the outer bar and made the mouth sloppy for smaller boats, so the morning tides have fished cleaner than the evening ones.

Looking ahead, the pattern should keep building rather than breaking. If the nights keep cooling the way they have, water temperature at the mouth should ease down another degree or two over the coming week, which typically tightens the bunker schools further and pulls more bass out of the upper river and into the staging grounds around Great Island and the outer flats. I've watched this same sequence play out for fifty years of falls on this river — the bait gets nervous first, then the bass commit, and by early October the mouth usually turns into the best striper water in the state for about a month. We're not there yet, but the ingredients are lining up.

For the next few days, the smart play is working the ebb at Saybrook Point and the DEP Marina channel edge early, before the wind builds, with bucktails or live bunker fished tight to the current seams. If the wind lays down out of the southwest, the outer bar off the river mouth is worth a look on the last of the falling tide — that's usually the first place the bigger fall fish show when they finally push in from the Sound.
