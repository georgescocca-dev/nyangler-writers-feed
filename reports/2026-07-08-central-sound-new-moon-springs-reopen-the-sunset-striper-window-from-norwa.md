# New Moon Springs Reopen the Sunset Striper Window from Norwalk to Charles Island
*Bigger tides and bunker pods are reshaping the Central Sound bite as fluke bail out of warming harbors for the cooler edges of Stratford Shoal.*

**PORT JEFFERSON, NY — July 8** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

We're coming off a stretch where the harbors got soft and the Sound stayed honest. Oyster Bay, Northport, Port Jeff Harbor — all of them have been running warm in the back corners, warm enough that the fluke that were happy sitting in eight feet of water on a sand bottom two weeks ago have picked up and moved. That's not a guess, that's what I'm seeing on the drift — empty water where there used to be marks, and marks now stacking on the hard edges where warm harbor water bumps into the cooler main-Sound flow. Stratford Shoal is the textbook example of that right now. The thermal break sitting off the western drop is doing exactly what a thermocline is supposed to do: holding bait, holding fluke, holding porgies, and making the shoal fish tighter to specific depth bands instead of spread all over the plateau.

The bigger story this week is the moon. We're building into new moon springs, with the tightest alignment landing Friday through Monday, July 10–13. Bigger tidal range means stronger current through the pinch points — Stratford Shoal rip, the gaps between the Norwalk Islands, the tip of Charles Island — and stronger current means bait gets pushed hard into the eddies where predators don't have to fight for it. The piece that's got my attention specifically is the PM flood lining up with sunset on those spring tide days. That's the first evening high-water-into-dark window we've had since the June full moon, and in the western half of my beat — Oyster Bay out through the Norwalk chain and down to Charles Island — that combination has produced some of the better bass nights of the last two summers. If you can only fish one window this week, that's the one I'd take.

Striper fishing itself has been honest but not easy. Guys working the Norwalk Islands humps on the last two hours of the incoming into dusk are finding schoolies mixed with better fish — bass to low 30-inch class — on white and chartreuse bucktails, one to two ounces, worked slow along the rock edges, and on soft-plastic swimbaits skipped over the same structure once the light drops. Nothing has been a blitz. It's been pick-your-spots fishing: find where the current is breaking around a specific rock or point, work it thoroughly, move on if nothing happens in twenty minutes. There have also been quieter reports of some genuinely large fish mixed into the bunker schools working Port Jefferson and around Middle Ground Light — not a pattern you can plan a whole trip around yet, but worth having a bigger rod rigged if you're marking bait balls getting nervous on the surface. When those peanut bunker and adult bunker schools get pushed tight by blues on top, that's when the bigger bass show up underneath to clean up what gets dropped.

Bluefish have been the most reliable thing in the middle Sound the last ten days. Port Jeff, Middle Ground, and the water off Charles Island have all had bunker getting worked over hard, blues in the 3 to 6 pound range mostly, some better mixed in. Diamond jigs — half-ounce to one-ounce, bare or with a tube — cast into the surface commotion is about as complicated as it needs to be right now. If the blues get finicky, dropping a bunker chunk on a fish-finder rig behind the melee usually pulls a few more.

Fluke has been the honest disappointment of the last two weeks for guys fishing the traditional harbor spots, but the Stratford Shoal push has been a bright spot if you know where to look. Four-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on three-quarter-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the western edge in 25 to 35 feet on the last of the outgoing, have been putting keeper-class fish in the boat — nothing huge, mostly 18 to 21 inches, but steady enough to call it a pattern. Spearing tipped on the same bucktail has been the better producer when the current slacks and the drift gets lazy. Middle Ground Light has fished similarly on the smaller tides but got noticeably better once the bigger water started moving through this week.

Porgies remain the most dependable thing I can point you to right now. Stratford Shoal and Middle Ground Light on the anchor, high-low rigs with sandworm or clam, and it's a numbers game — good for keeping a rod bent when the bass and blues aren't cooperating. Blackfish, honestly, is a non-story this time of year — that bite belongs to October and November, not July, and I wouldn't waste a trip on it now. Weakfish continue to be more theory than reality in this zone; I haven't heard a credible report worth passing along, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

Looking ahead, the July 10–13 spring tide window with that PM flood-sunset convergence is where I'd put my money if I only had one shot this week — Norwalk Islands and Charles Island rock structure, working the last two hours before dark into the first hour of true dark. If the wind stays reasonable out of the south or southwest, that setup should hold. Plan B if the bass don't show is the Stratford Shoal fluke drift, which has been the more consistent producer regardless of moon phase. As for bonito — it's too early for Central Sound yet, that's an August pattern once the water pushes past 68 degrees through the narrows, but the pelagic corridor east of us is already stirring, and it's worth watching how fast that warmth works its way down the Sound this month.
