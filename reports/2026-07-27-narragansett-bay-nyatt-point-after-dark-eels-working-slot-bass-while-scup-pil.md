# Nyatt Point after dark: eels working slot bass while scup pile the Rocky Point ledges
*The Providence River's gone quiet on daytime bass, but the night bite and the bottom bite are carrying the bay through the heat.*

**PROVIDENCE, RI — July 27** — _by Vinny Ciervo, Zone Writer_

There's a guy who ties off at the Fields Point pier most mornings, retired dockworker, fishes for an hour before the heat gets stupid, and he's been telling me the same thing for two weeks straight: 'Nothing's moving till the sun's gone.' He's not wrong. That's the whole story of the upper bay right now.

Back in May and June, Buckeye Brook was doing what it does — herring stacking up in that run, bass following the smell of them right up into the Warwick coves, and for about six weeks it was as easy as this fishery gets. That window's closed. The herring are long gone, the water in the upper bay has crept into the mid-70s on the surface, and the bass that were slashing bait in broad daylight off Conimicut in June have gone nocturnal on us. It's not that they left. It's that they got smart.

I've been fishing Nyatt Point and the flats off Rocky Point starting an hour before sundown through the first couple hours of dark, and that's where the slot fish are showing. Live eels on a 3/0 circle hook, no weight if the current allows it, drifted right along the drop where the rocky bottom falls into deeper water on the outgoing tide. Had a couple of fish push 30 inches doing exactly that last Tuesday, both right at the top of the ebb when the current was still moving hard enough to keep the eel swimming natural instead of just sitting there. If you're set up for chunking, fresh bunker chunks fished on the bottom in that same stretch after full dark has been drawing bluefish and the occasional bigger bass mixed in — bring more chunk than you think you need, because the blues will pick a chunk bait apart in about four casts once they find it.

Daytime, honestly, the upper bay bass bite has been tough. I won't sugarcoat it. If you're out there at noon working topwater over the flats like it's May, you're going to get skunked and you're going to be miserable doing it in this heat. Save your gas and fish the tide change at dusk instead.

Fluke are the one that's actually moved on us, and not in the direction guys want to hear. The shallow stuff — the flats off Warwick, the inside edges of Greenwich Bay — has gone soft. Too warm, not enough current exchange to keep it fresh. The fish that are still biting have pushed down toward the deeper channel edges around Prudence Island and the drop-offs on the west side of the bay where the water's still moving and holding a couple degrees cooler. White Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails, dragged slow along the channel break in 25 to 35 feet, on the last two hours of the incoming — that's produced the better fish, a few keepers to 21 inches mixed into a lot of short fish you've got to sort through. It's not a banner fluke summer in the bay right now, but it's fishable if you go where the current still runs.

What's carrying the daytime action, no question, is the bottom bite. Scup have piled onto every rocky ledge from Rocky Point down through the east passage, and that fishery hasn't slowed a bit with the heat — if anything it's gotten better as the scup pack tighter around structure to escape the warm surface layer. Simple rig: two hooks, size 6 or 8, squid strips or sandworm, small enough sinker to feel bottom without dragging, anchored right up on the rock piles. You can limit out in an hour some mornings if you find the right ledge and the tide's got some push to it — I like the last of the incoming into the top of the tide, before it goes dead slack and the bite shuts off for a while. Black sea bass are mixed in on the same structure a little deeper, and they've been a reliable bonus fish all month on the same rigs, just fished a touch heavier to hold bottom in the current seams.

Bluefish are around, riding whatever bait pods are getting pushed up the bay on the tide, and they'll show up wherever the bunker are thick — I had a stretch off Conimicut two weeks back where they blew up a school right at the surface for about fifteen minutes and then vanished like they were never there. That's the pattern with them right now: not a sustained blitz, more of a hit-and-run. Keep a rod rigged with a wire leader and a Kastmaster or a metal-lip swimmer ready, because when they show, you've got a short window before they push off again chasing the bait.

We flagged it last week and it's held — stable, hot, calm pattern, and that's exactly what's kept the bass on the night shift and the bunker-chasing blues moving around unpredictably instead of settling into a real blitz. I don't see that changing in the next few days unless we get a real wind event to mix the water column and knock the surface temp down. Until then, my plan stays the same: eels at dusk off Nyatt and Rocky Point, scup rigs on the ledges in the morning before it gets brutal, and fluke gear pointed at the deeper edges around Prudence where the current's still doing some work. If a front comes through and drops the surface temp a few degrees, watch for the bass to come back up shallow during the day — that's the tell I'd be looking for heading into August.
