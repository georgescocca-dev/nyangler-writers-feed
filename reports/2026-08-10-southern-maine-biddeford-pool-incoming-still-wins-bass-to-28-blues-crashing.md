# Biddeford Pool Incoming Still Wins: Bass to 28, Blues Crashing Peanut Bunker at Pine Point
*The Pool did what the Pool does at first light, and the bunker run finally dragged the blues into the back creeks like we said it would.*

**BIDDEFORD POOL, ME — August 10** — _by Dutch Osterberg, Zone Writer_

Six-fifteen Tuesday morning, incoming water just starting to push through the Pool channel, and I put a 9-inch black Danny Plug across the current seam off the eastern shore. Second cast, fish nearly ripped the rod out of my hand. Twenty-eight inches, fat, full of herring, came off the outer bar where the tide funnels bait past those rocks like a conveyor belt. That's the Pool for you — best hour in Maine striper fishing, and it doesn't care what the calendar says. Water's been running cooler up here than anywhere south of us, and that's exactly why this pattern holds through August when guys in Massachusetts are stuck fishing the dead of night.

That's the story of the whole zone right now. Gulf of Maine water stays honest — not warm-bath stuff like the Sound or the Vineyard — so our bass haven't gone nocturnal on us the way they do south of the Cape. You can still find them working daylight in the river mouths and around current-swept structure, especially the first two hours of an incoming tide when the bait gets pushed hard against the rocks. Biddeford Pool remains the textbook version of that pattern — fish staged on the flood, working the mouth where the river dumps into the Pool, then sliding back out as the tide slacks. I've had my best luck an hour before high, working topwater over the flats before the sun gets too high, then switching to a swimming plug worked slow along the channel edge once the light comes up.

Pine Point and the Scarborough marsh system have turned into the other story this week, and it's one we saw building. Peanut bunker have been thickening up in those back creeks for two weeks now, and the cocktail blues finally caught up to them — smaller fish, one to three pounds, but stacked tight and mean, blowing up on the surface right at the mouth of the marsh on the outgoing. We flagged this pattern last week — said the bunker would pull the blues into the back bays — and here it is, right on schedule. Small metal — half-ounce Deadly Dicks, or a topwater popper if you want the show — thrown right into the boil gets bit instantly. Snapper blues are also thick right in Wells Harbor around the docks, which is good news if you've got a kid with a spinning rod and some worm chunks. Easy, steady action, nothing that'll bend a rod, but it keeps them interested.

Higgins Beach had one real blitz this week, Thursday evening, southwest wind pushing bait up against the beach and the blues came in hot for about forty minutes right at the base of the rocks toward the Cape Elizabeth end. Guys throwing poppers and Hopkins spoons got into fish in the three-to-five-pound range mixed with schoolie bass working the same push of bait. That window closed fast — by dark it was over, and Friday and Saturday it never repeated. That's the honest read on Higgins right now: when the wind sets up right and the bait's pinned against the beach, it can go off in a hurry, but it's not an every-evening thing. I'd check it at dusk on any day with a light southwest breeze and moving bait, but don't plan your whole week around it.

Prouts Neck has been fishing the way it always does on a southwest wind — the ledges on the ocean side load up with bait and bass tuck in behind the rocks working the current seams. I worked the outer ledges Sunday morning with a bucktail, three-quarter ounce with a white curly tail trailer, working it slow along bottom in eighteen to twenty-two feet, and picked up two schoolies and one 24-inch fish that ate right at the base of the rocks where the wash meets the calmer water. Nothing to write home about volume-wise, but quality water, and worth the walk out if the wind cooperates.

Mackerel schools are still thick along the whole stretch from Ogunquit down through Cape Elizabeth, and that's been the difference-maker for anyone fishing bait instead of plugs. Snag a few mackerel off the rocks with a Sabiki rig, then drift one live or cut fresh under a slip float around structure — that's produced some of the better bass this month, including a couple of overslot fish reported out of the Kennebunk River mouth on the tail of the outgoing.

Flounder fishing has been steady but unspectacular in Wells Harbor and up in the Kennebunk River — bloodworms and clam strips on a simple two-hook flounder rig, fished the last two hours of the outgoing where the current slows over the mud. Nothing huge, but a limit of dinner flounder isn't hard to come by if you're patient. Cod remain a boat game — the deeper ledges off Cape Elizabeth are still holding fish for guys jigging diamond jigs in sixty to eighty feet, though the bite's been more consistent early in the morning before the boat traffic picks up.

Tides have been running big all week off that moon, which sharpened the bite windows at the Pool and at Prouts but also made some of the beach fishing tougher — current ripping too hard through the middle two hours to hold bottom comfortably. That should ease up some over the next several days as we move off the peak lunar push, settling things into a more standard rhythm. If I had one morning to fish this week, I'd be at Biddeford Pool on the incoming, first two hours of daylight, throwing a swimming plug along that channel edge. If the southwest wind holds through the weekend, Prouts Neck deserves a look too — that pattern rarely lets you down when the wind sets up right.
