# Fort Trumbull Rip Sharpens as the New Moon Tide Builds
*Bass are holding tight to the sub base bait schools while snapper blues rule the shallows off Ocean Beach.*

**NEW LONDON, CT — August 8** — _by Marcus Webb, Zone Writer_

Standing on the Fort Trumbull wall Tuesday evening, I watched the ebb line pull harder than it had in three weeks — the seam where the river current meets the harbor swell was throwing a visible wake off the point, something you only see when the tidal range starts stretching toward a new moon. That's exactly what's happening. We're four days out from the August 12 new moon and the Thames is starting to feel it, the current running with more authority through the narrows off the sub base and around the Gold Star Bridge pilings. We flagged this last week — said building tidal flow into the new moon would sharpen the bite at current-driven structure, and that's precisely what's unfolding on the river this week.

The striper pattern hasn't changed much from late July, but it's gotten more reliable as the current has firmed up. Fish are stacked around the bait schools off the submarine base, holding in 15 to 20 feet of water where the river channel drops off the flats. This is warm-summer behavior — bass parking on structure with quick access to deep water, not chasing bait around open water in the heat. New London's reading around 69 degrees at the surface, which is comfortable enough that the fish aren't forced deep, but they're not roaming far from cover either. The best window has been the last two hours of the ebb into dead low, when bait gets funneled down through the channel and the bass set up on the downstream side of the pilings to ambush it. Bunker chunks on a fishfinder rig, 3 to 4 ounces of lead depending on how hard the current's running, fished tight to the base of the Gold Star Bridge pilings has been the most consistent producer — mostly schoolies and low-slot fish in the 24 to 28 inch range, with the occasional better fish mixed in on the deeper drifts past Fort Trumbull.

Bucktails are working too, especially on the top of the flood when bait pushes up into the harbor. A 1.5-ounce white or chartreuse bucktail, tipped with a strip of squid or a 4-inch white Gulp swimming mullet, cast up-current and swept back through the rip off Fort Trumbull has drawn strikes from fish holding on the current seam itself — you can see them boil there some evenings when the light gets low. It's not fast fishing. You might make thirty casts to draw three or four hits, but the fish that come are solid, and the rip concentrates them enough that it's worth the repetition.

Bluefish are the real story right now, and they're following the same regional trend we've seen building elsewhere in the Sound — a resurgence after a flat July. The shallows off Ocean Beach and Pleasure Beach are thick with snapper blues, small enough to be a kids' fishery but numerous enough to keep a rod bent all afternoon on tiny Kastmasters or a bit of bait on a size 6 hook under a bobber. What's more interesting is the adult blues showing up mixed in with the bass around the sub base bait schools — I had a 4-pounder hit a bucktail meant for stripers off the bridge pilings Wednesday, and heard of a few similar fish taken on Ava jigs worked through the deeper water off Fort Trumbull on the outgoing tide. If that pattern holds and more of those bigger blues push up-river behind the bait, it could turn into a legitimate mixed bag fishery through the rest of August.

Porgies are still around if you want a change of pace — working bloodworms or clam on the bottom near the State Pier moorings and around any rock piles in the harbor in 10 to 15 feet of water has been steady, nothing spectacular but good for a bucket of dinner fish on a slow tide. I haven't put much time into blackfish yet; that pattern doesn't really turn on for us until the water starts cooling toward fall, and we're not there.

What's driving all of this is fairly straightforward. The building tidal range ahead of the new moon is pushing more water through the narrows at the sub base and past Fort Trumbull, which stirs bait out of the shallows and forces it to run the gauntlet past waiting stripers and blues. The wider the swing between high and low, the more pronounced that effect gets — and we're only going to see the range increase through the weekend and into early next week as the new moon approaches on the 12th.

Looking ahead, I'd expect the pattern to keep sharpening rather than change. If the current keeps building the way it has the last few days, the last two hours of the ebb at Fort Trumbull and the top of the flood through the bridge pilings should keep producing bass and the occasional bigger blue right through the new moon. I'd fish the rip with bucktails on the flood and switch to chunk bait on the pilings once the tide turns hard toward low. It's not blow-up fishing, but it's honest, and the structure here doesn't lie — when the current runs like this, the fish show up where it funnels the bait, same as it's always been on this river.
