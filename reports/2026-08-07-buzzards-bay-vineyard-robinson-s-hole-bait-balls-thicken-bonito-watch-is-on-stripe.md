# Robinson's Hole bait balls thicken — bonito watch is on, stripers cashing in first
*Silversides and squid are stacking the rips between the Elizabeths and the Vineyard, and the water's finally warm enough to make the albie question a matter of days, not weeks.*

**WOODS HOLE, MA — August 7** — _by Maria Fonseca, Zone Writer_

The bait tells you everything before the rod does. Right now silversides are balling up tight against the rocks at Robinson's Hole on the last two hours of the ebb, getting pinched by current into nervous, flickering sheets that any predator with eyes can see from thirty feet down. Squid are showing too — small ones, an inch or two, working the deeper water off Quick's Hole where the current runs hardest between Pasque and Nashawena. That combination, silversides tight to structure and squid stacking in the deeper cuts, is exactly the setup that precedes the first albie and bonito showing of the season in this corridor. We flagged this corridor last week as one to watch for the turnover, and the bait is doing its part. What hasn't happened yet is the actual sighting — no confirmed albie or bonito reports have come out of Robinson's, Quick's, or the Menemsha rips as of this week. The water's in the window, the bait's in the window, but the fish haven't punched their ticket. That's an honest read, not a hedge.

Regional water temps back up the timing. Numbers from Long Island Sound and Montauk this week are sitting in the mid-70s — Kings Point at 74.8, New London running warmer at 77.4, Montauk holding 74.5. That's the same thermal band that typically flips the switch on this stretch of Buzzards Bay and the Vineyard side. If the corridor here is tracking similarly, and there's no reason to think it isn't given the bait behavior, the window for the first false albacore push runs from right now through the next week to ten days. I'd rather tell you to watch the birds and the nervous water than promise you a hookup that hasn't happened.

In the meantime the striped bass have been the honest workers of this bite. Robinson's Hole on the last hour of the outgoing has been giving up bass in the 26 to 32-inch range to anglers working white bucktails, half-ounce to three-quarter depending on the current speed, tipped with a strip of squid or a chunk of mackerel belly. The fish are sitting on the downcurrent edge of the rip where the white water folds back into slower water — that seam is doing the work, not the middle of the rip itself. Quick's Hole has been similar but slightly better on bluefish, with choppers in the 3 to 6-pound class hammering topwater poppers worked fast across the rip face on the early morning flood, before the sun gets high and pushes them deeper. If you're throwing metal, a 1-ounce Deadly Dick or similar tin worked with a jigging retrieat through the deeper pockets off Cuttyhunk's west end has also been producing mixed bass and blues on the last two hours of the incoming.

Fluke fishing off Woods Hole and around the Elizabeth Islands has been steady rather than spectacular — this is the honest part of the report. Drifting 20 to 30 feet of water off Naushon and Nonamesset on the slower stages of the tide, white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails have accounted for keepers to 4 pounds, but the counts are thin compared to peak fluke weeks. Scup fishing around the Menemsha jetty and the Oak Bluffs breakwater has been the more reliable inshore action for anyone wanting a bent rod without covering much water — small hooks, sandworms or squid strips, fished right on bottom around the rocks on either tide stage, though the bite slows hard at dead high and dead low.

The moon and tide picture matters here more than people give it credit for. Weaker tidal exchange around the neap stretches tends to spread bait out and soften the rip action at Robinson's and Quick's — when the current isn't pushing hard, the bait doesn't get pinned against structure the same way, and the bass and blues scatter to feed on their own schedule instead of stacking on the seam. Watch for the stronger push tides coming — that's when the rips really load up and when, if the albies and bonito are going to show, they're most likely to show first.

Looking ahead, this is the week to be on the water early and watching, not necessarily the week to expect a hookup on the first cast. If the bait keeps stacking at Robinson's and the water holds in the mid-70s, the first confirmed bonito or albie of the season out of this corridor could come any day now — that's not hype, that's the pattern doing what it's done in past Augusts. Best case, a warm stretch with clean tides holds through the coming week and the first fish show at Robinson's or off Menemsha on an outgoing tide with bait pinned tight. If a cold snap or a blow scatters the bait instead, I'd shift attention back to the striper and bluefish bite in the rips, which has been the more dependable producer all along.
