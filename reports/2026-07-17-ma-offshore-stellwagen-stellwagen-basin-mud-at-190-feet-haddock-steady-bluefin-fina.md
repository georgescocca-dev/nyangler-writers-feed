# Stellwagen Basin mud at 190 feet: haddock steady, bluefin finally chasing mack on the northern edge
*Cod have slid deep off the gravel with the summer warm-up, but the mud bite holds and the tuna bite is starting to build on the Bank.*

**GLOUCESTER, MA — July 17** — _by Capt. Donna Shaw, Zone Writer_

Sixty-eight degrees on the surface over the Stellwagen Basin Tuesday morning, and 190 feet on the sounder before the mud showed up clean on the screen — soft, rolling bottom with just enough life on it to tell you the haddock are home. That's been the read most of this week: surface water pushing warm, mid-70s in spots on the shallower flats, but the deep basin between Stellwagen and Tillies still holds that cold cushion the groundfish want in July.

Cod have done what they always do this time of year — they've backed off the classic gravel patches on the western edge and slid into deeper, cooler pockets. I'm not finding much shallower than 140 feet right now with any consistency. The 39-42 fathom line off the northwest corner, where the gravel breaks up into scattered boulder and shell hash, gave up a handful of cod to 15 pounds on Thursday, but you had to work for every one of them. Jigging diamond jigs with a green tube teaser, tipped light with clam, is still the go-to when the current's moving. When it slacks off, switch to bait — whole clam on a high-low rig fished right on bottom, patient, letting it sit. The cod bite isn't dead, it's just spread thin and deep, and anybody telling you it's stacked up shallow this week is selling something.

Haddock are the more honest fish right now. The mud bottom in the basin, running 180 to 220 feet, has been holding solid schools — we've had double and triple headers on some drifts with fish running 2 to 5 pounds, a few pushing 7. Standard haddock rig, two hooks, red or chartreuse beads, clam or squid strips, bounced just off bottom on the drift. The current has to be moving enough to keep the bait presenting naturally — dead slack and they go quiet on you. Tillies Bank has been the more consistent producer of the two basins this week over the classic mud south of the humps, though Stellwagen's own basin ground isn't far behind.

Now for the fish everybody's been asking about at the dock. The bluefin are showing, and it's the mackerel that brought them. We've had good pods of mackerel stacked up over the northern part of the Bank, in that 60 to 90 foot range, and where you find bait balled up tight and diving birds working it, you're starting to see fish push up under it — boils, sometimes whole schools of small mackerel getting shredded from below. Nothing like the giant class fish you'll see out here come September, these are mostly school-size to mid-60-inch fish so far, but a few boats have hooked up trolling small spreader bars and green machines right through the bait schools in that stretch running from the northeast edge down toward Cape Cod Tails. If you can find fresh mackerel yourself, jig up a dozen, keep a couple live-lined off a balloon or kite rig near the surface, and drift through the bait — that's produced better than blind trolling this week.

The pattern makes sense when you think about it. Mackerel like that cooler, well-oxygenated water on top of the Bank, and where the mackerel go, the tuna follow. It's early yet for the real giant class fish that'll show later in the summer chasing sand eels and herring, but this early mackerel-driven bite is a good sign the Bank's setting up the way it should for late July and into August. Pollock have been mixed in around the wrecks and the steeper drop-offs on the eastern side of Stellwagen, taking diamond jigs meant for cod, and there's been a scattering of hake showing on the softer mud edges at night for anyone willing to soak bait after dark.

Tide's been the deciding factor more than wind lately — we've had a stretch of manageable southwest breeze, nothing over 15 knots most days, so it's really about picking your drift around the moving water rather than fighting sea state. We're coming off the new moon now, tides building toward the next push, and that stronger current should help both the haddock bite on the mud and get the cod moving again on whatever gravel they're holding to.

Looking ahead, if the mackerel schools hold their position on the northern Bank through this week, I'd expect the bluefin activity to build rather than fade — that's usually how it goes once the bait sets up. I'd spend my first hour scouting for bird activity and bait balls before committing to a troll pattern. For groundfish, I'm staying deep and patient — the basin mud over gravel structure at 180 feet-plus is the safer bet through the heat of summer, and I wouldn't waste a tide working shallow gravel hoping the cod haven't left yet. They have. Give the deep water a shot, keep an eye out for diving terns on the surface, and don't be surprised if this mackerel-tuna pattern turns into something worth planning a whole trip around by early August.
