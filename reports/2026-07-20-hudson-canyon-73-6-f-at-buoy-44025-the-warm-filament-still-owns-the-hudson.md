# 73.6°F at Buoy 44025 — the Warm Filament Still Owns the Hudson Wall
*A stable eddy is holding bait right on the 100-fathom break, and yellowfin, bigeye, and slot bluefin are all working it — but you have to find the right water first.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 20** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

73.6°F. That's the read off Buoy 44025 out in the Bight as of Saturday afternoon, and by itself it doesn't tell you much — but stack it against Kings Point at 71.6 and New London at 67.6 and you've got a picture of a coastal shelf that's still cool relative to what's sitting out past the edge. That gradient matters because it's exactly the kind of setup that pushes warm-core ring water tight against the canyon wall instead of letting it slide offshore. I've watched this pattern build for ten days now under a stubborn high pressure ridge, and it's done what I told you it would do two weeks back — the filament held, and the bite kept building instead of fading. We called that. Now it's paying off.

The run out is a hair over 100 nautical miles from Manasquan, closer to 96 out of Shinnecock, and either way you're pointed at the same target: the 100-fathom line running north-south through 72°30'W, with the heaviest concentration of life stacking up around the Dip near 39°51'N/72°32'W and pushing south to the Claw at 39°44'N/72°28'W. Boats working that stretch this week found green-blue water, a hard temp break, and bait balls thick enough to show up as a solid red smear on the sounder in 900 to 1,100 feet. That's your daytime yellowfin and bigeye zone — troll ballyhoo behind Ilanders and small spreader bars through the morning, then anchor up or drift the edge after dark and chunk butterfish once the sun's off the water. Both methods have been putting fish on the deck, which tells you the tuna aren't just reacting to one trigger — they've got bait to eat and they're eating around the clock.

The daytime troll bite has been mostly yellowfin in the 30 to 60-pound class, with bigeye mixed in on the deeper drops along the west wall where the bottom falls from 90 to 120 fathoms fast. That's classic bigeye structure — steep relief pushes bait up the water column and gives them a wall to work against. At night, the chunk bite has produced better averages on bigeye, some pushing toward the 100-pound mark, and that's not surprising given the timing — we're coming into the July 21 first-quarter neap, which softens current speed across the whole Bight. Less current means a slower drift on anchor, which means your chunk baits stay in the strike zone longer instead of getting swept out in ten seconds. If you've been frustrated by baits getting hammered on the surface before they ever sink, this is the week that problem eases up.

Mahi have been mixed in on the troll wherever there's floating structure — weed lines, a stray lobster ball, anything holding shade. Nothing giant, mostly schoolie-class fish in the 3 to 8-pound range, but they'll eat a small spreader bar or a naked ballyhoo just as fast as a yellowfin will, so don't be shocked when one shows up in the spread. Wahoo have been scarce this week — a couple of cutoffs reported near the temp break but nothing confirmed boatside — which tells me the high-speed bite hasn't fully turned on yet. If you want a shot at one, run a lure at 12 to 15 knots right on the color change itself, not fifty yards off it. White marlin have made a light appearance around the Claw, mostly on naked ballyhoo pulled slow behind the dredge, but it's a bonus fish right now, not a target.

On the bluefin side, the recreational slot fishery — 27 inches up to just under 47, two-fish limit — has held remarkably steady since the July 3rd trophy-category closure. Most of that action has concentrated closer to Block Island and the eastern canyons, but I'm seeing enough slot-class bluefin mixed into the spreader bar spread at the Dip to mention it here. If you're already running ballyhoo for yellowfin, don't be surprised if a 35-pound bluefin eats first.

Swordfish guys have been quiet on the report front this week, but the daytime drop conditions haven't changed — 1,200 to 1,800 feet off the edge near the Claw, squid strips and glow gear, same program that's worked all July. I'd expect that to pick back up once boats start committing full trips to it instead of splitting time with the tuna bite, which right now is the better bet per hour on the water.

Looking ahead: if this high-pressure pattern holds through the weekend — and right now there's no reason to think it won't — I expect the bite to keep strengthening rather than plateau. The neap tide softens current for a few more days, which favors the night chunk crowd, and the warm filament shows no sign of sliding off the wall yet. Best case, this stays a stable, repeatable Hudson pattern through the end of the month. If the eddy shifts east like these things sometimes do, I'd be ready to run a little further south toward Wilmington to stay on the warm side of the break. For now, though, the Dip and the Claw are the two spots I'd fish if I only had one trip this week.
