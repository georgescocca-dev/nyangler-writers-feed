# Great Point still empty of albies — but the bass under the birds say patience
*No bonito, no albies yet off Nantucket, but the bluefish and stripers working the rips are keeping rods bent while we wait on the real show.*

**NANTUCKET, MA — July 24** — _by Charlie St. Pierre, Zone Writer_

Stood on the point at Great Point Wednesday morning with the light just coming up pink over the rip, glassing for that first nervous flash of bait getting pushed to the surface — the kind of thing that means albies or bonito showed. Didn't see it. Saw gannets working way out past the bar, saw terns dipping over a bluefish blitz closer in, but the specific, frantic, small-bait-getting-shredded-from-underneath pattern that means tuna-family fish arrived — not yet. Two weeks now I've been watching for it, same as every July, and the water's finally sitting in that window where it should happen any day.

Here's the thing though — the rip itself has been plenty alive without them. Fishing the ebb off the Point Tuesday, we had bluefish stacked from the surface down to maybe fifteen feet, blitzing on sand eels getting swept off the bar. Poppers got bit instantly — Doc's and Gibbs pencils, white and chartreuse, worked with a stumbling retrieve right through the froth. Fish running four to eight pounds, mean as always, cutting leaders if you're not running at least 40-pound fluoro bite tippet. That blitz has been the most reliable thing on this end of the Sound for going on ten days, and it fits exactly what we've been seeing spread up the coast — bunker and sand eel schools pinned tight against beaches with blues working them relentlessly. We said last week that pattern would keep spreading as long as the bait stayed concentrated, and out here it's held true — the Point blitz hasn't broken up once.

Under that blitz, though, is where it's gotten interesting. Guys jigging deeper through the bluefish with 2-ounce Kastmasters and Ava jigs have been picking off striped bass in the 28 to 34-inch range, mostly on the last two hours of the ebb when the rip is really cranking and that current seam forms hard off the northeast corner of the bar. Stripers are doing the typical mid-summer thing here too — daytime bites have softened with the water sitting warm, so first light and last light are your windows, same as everywhere south of the Cape right now. Fish are holding tighter to structure and the thermocline break, then sliding up onto the rip edges to feed when the light's low and the current's pushing hard.

Sankaty's been quieter than Great Point but not dead — a scattering of keeper bass off the bluff on bucktails worked slow along bottom during the last hour of the outgoing, water depth there running maybe 20 to 30 feet close to the rip line. Nothing to write home about, but if you want to avoid the bluefish circus at the Point and just soak a bucktail for bass, that's where I'd point the boat.

Down at Monomoy, the southwest wind we've had on and off has been setting up the rips the way it usually does — pushing bait and current together off the south end near the Powder Hole and out toward the elbow. Fluke fishing there has been decent but not great, which tracks with what's happening everywhere south of us this month — the shallow water's warm enough that the better fish have slid into deeper channel edges and humps, leaving mostly shorts up top. Working 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with Gulp swimming mullet along the drop-offs in 15 to 25 feet on the stronger stages of the tide has produced fluke to 4 pounds, but you're culling through a lot of dinks to get there. Bring patience, not just tackle.

Horseshoe Shoal has stayed a bluefish and scup program mostly, steady but unspectacular, which is about what you'd expect with the Sound sitting in this stable mid-summer holding pattern — warm, settled, no real disruption from wind or a big tide swing since we're coming off the neap. That should start to change some as the tide rebuilds strength over the next few days, which typically sharpens up structure fishing around the shoals and rips generally.

As for the bonito and albies — every signal says they're close. Water temps pushing up through the corridor from Buzzards Bay through the Vineyard and out toward us have crossed into that range where scout schools historically show, and there's chatter of squid showing thick inshore, which is exactly the kind of forage that pulls albies onto the Great Point rip once they arrive. I don't have a confirmed fish yet to report, and I won't pretend I do. But if I had one morning to burn this week, I'd be back on that point at first light with an epoxy jig tied on and a popper rod rigged and ready, glassing hard for that first nervous bait push. When it happens here, it happens fast — one morning there's nothing, and two days later the whole rip's snapping with three-pound footballs ripping drag on 10-pound tippet.

For now: fish the blues while they're thick, work the bass on the low-light tides, and keep one eye on the horizon. The real show at Great Point is close. It's just not here yet.
