# Ran the long haul to Wilmington, trolled the break for six hours — three yellowfin, a mahi, and a bigeye after dark
*The warm eddy is still parked over the canyon, and running the extra fuel to fish it paid off — barely — this week.*

**CAPE MAY, NJ — August 14** — _by Capt. Dana Cooper, Zone Writer_

The decision this week was simple math. I could've stayed closer, worked the inshore lumps, saved four hours of diesel — or I could run the extra distance out to Wilmington and fish a break that's actually holding structure instead of guessing at scattered bait. I ran the long way. Three trips out, and I'd do it again, though I won't pretend all three days paid the fuel bill the same.

The warm-water pool that's been sitting east of the canyon system all month hasn't moved much, and that's the whole story right now. It's been parked over the edge, feeding a clean break where the green inshore water gives way to that blue, current-streaked water everybody's chasing. When that edge holds still like this, it does the work for you — bait stacks up on it, tuna sit under it, and you're not burning half a day searching. When it wanders, you're back to running the ledge blind. This week it mostly held, which is why the fishing was decent instead of desperate.

We trolled the break early each morning — spreader bars with cedar plugs and Green Machines in the middle, naked ballyhoo and small Ilanders in blue-white and green-yellow out the long riggers, a bird teaser up top to draw eyes. First morning out, we picked off two yellowfin in the 25-30 pound class right on the color change, both on the short rigger ballyhoo, and had a mahi come up on the long bird and eat a naked bally — that fish went about 12 pounds, nothing to write home about but it filled the fish box while we reset. Second trip was slower — one yellowfin, a couple of chances that didn't stick, and a wahoo that came screaming through the pattern and cut a bird bar clean off the wire. That's the wahoo tax out here. You troll fast enough to interest tuna, you're going to feed one to a wahoo eventually and lose gear doing it.

White marlin showed exactly the way we figured they would this stretch — a bonus, not a target. We had one pop the long rigger ballyhoo on day one, pitched a naked bally back at him on spin gear, and he ate it and came unbuttoned boatside. That's about the going rate right now — a swipe or two a day if you're trolling the right water, not stacked fish. Anybody planning a trip around marlin numbers specifically is going to be disappointed; anybody treating it as a bonus while targeting yellowfin will be pleasantly surprised once or twice a trip.

The better story has been the overnight chunk bite. We anchored up on the edge of the canyon proper the second night, set a slow chunk line of butterflied bunker with circle hooks fished way back with no added weight, and let it soak through the dark hours. That's where the bigeye showed. One fish, but a good one — pushing 140 pounds, came up on the third or fourth chunk after midnight and took nearly forty minutes to get boatside on 50-wide gear. That's the trade-off with the overnight chunk approach: you're not going to load the boat, but when a bigeye finds the slick, it's a different class of fish than anything you're pulling off the troll. If you've got the crew and the patience to sit through a slow midnight drift with butterfish and bunker chunks going out steady, it's worth the time right now while that thermal edge is sitting where it is.

I'll be honest about the days that didn't work. The third trip out, the wind swung around and put a little chop on the edge, and the bite went quiet for almost four hours in the middle of the day — no marks, no birds working, nothing showing on the surface. We picked up one small mahi under a weed patch and called it. Not every run out there this week was a paycheck. The canyon's producing, but it's not stacked wall-to-wall the way it can be when everything lines up — moon, tide, bait, and edge position all at once.

Looking ahead, the read is straightforward: as long as that warm pool holds its position over the canyon edge, I'd expect the troll bite to stay steady on yellowfin and mahi, with wahoo as an occasional bycatch tax, and the overnight chunk to keep producing a bigeye shot for anybody willing to sit through the dark hours. If that thermal boundary starts sliding — east, west, doesn't matter which direction — the whole pattern resets and you're back to running around looking for where the bait went. I've seen that happen fast out here in mid-August before. For now, if I had one more trip to plan this week, I'd run out early, work the break hard through the morning hours while the water's calmest, and if the numbers are thin by early afternoon, I'd rather anchor up and let a chunk line soak into the evening than keep burning fuel trolling dead water.
