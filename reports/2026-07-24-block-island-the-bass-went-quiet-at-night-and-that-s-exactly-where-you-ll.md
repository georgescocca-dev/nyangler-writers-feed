# The bass went quiet at night — and that's exactly where you'll find them now
*Block Island's stripers have slid deep and nocturnal, but the first bonito scouts may be closer than anyone thinks.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — July 24** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

There's a smell to the air out here in late July, a kind of stillness that settles over the island around 8 PM when the wind drops and the day-trippers head for the ferry. I noticed it three nights running this week — the kind of calm that makes you look twice at the water off the bluffs, because you know something's about to change. And it has. The bass have gone underground on us, the way they do every summer once the surface water settles into that warm, glassy mid-70s feel. If you want stripers right now, you fish the dark.

I worked the North Rip Tuesday night on the tail of the flood, anchored up in eighteen feet with the current pushing hard against the ledge, and it was a different fishery than the one we had in June. No surface commotion, no birds, just that steady mechanical thump of a fish taking a bait off bottom. Eels did the work — plain and simple, an 8-inch live eel on a fish-finder rig, enough weight to hold bottom in that rip current, and a long enough leader to let it swim natural. We put four bass in the boat between 10 PM and 1 AM, biggest one 34 pounds, and every single one came on that slow incoming push when the water stacks up against the ledge and the current really starts to sing.

Southwest Ledge has been more of a dawn program lately. I like being anchored up there by 4:45, right as the sky starts to gray out, working bucktails — the 2-ounce white ones with a chunk of mackerel belly hooked through the skirt — bounced right along that rocky bottom in 30 to 40 feet. The fish are down deep chasing bait that's stacked up on the thermocline, and you need to get down to them fast and stay there. We're not seeing the daytime blitzes we had a month ago. That's just where we are in the calendar. The water up top is too warm and too bright, so the fish do their feeding in the low-light windows and then drop back down and go quiet through the heat of the day.

The bluefish, on the other hand, haven't gotten the memo about subtlety. We flagged this pattern building last week, and it's still the most reliable action going — aggressive blues chewing through bunker schools anywhere there's bait pinned against structure. Off Sandy Point and down toward Charlestown Breachway, we've had fish blitzing bunker in broad daylight, and it hasn't slowed a step. Metal — a 1-ounce Kastmaster or a Hopkins — thrown right into the surface chaos will get bit on almost every cast right now. Bluefish to 10 pounds, sometimes bigger, and they're not picky about presentation. If you want fast action and don't mind retying leaders all afternoon, that's your play.

Fluke have been a grind, honestly. The shorts are everywhere — inside the Great Salt Pond, along the sand edges off Old Harbor — but keeper-sized fish want depth and current right now. I've had better luck working the drift along the deeper edges off Charlestown, 35 to 45 feet, dragging a white Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, tipped with squid strip, moving slow on the last two hours of the outgoing. You have to cover water and pick through a lot of dinks to find a 20-incher, but they're there if you're patient.

Porgies have been steady and mostly ignored, which is a shame because they're good eating and easy fishing right now — bottom rigs with squid or clam off the Coast Guard station in 20 to 30 feet will keep a rod bent most of an afternoon. Blackfish season's closed, so that's a non-issue till fall, but I mention it because guys keep asking.

Now here's the piece everyone on the dock wants to talk about — bonito and albies. Water temps around the island and up through the Buzzards Bay corridor have crossed into that zone where the first scout schools typically show, and I've got a gut feeling we're closer than people think. No confirmed catch yet as of this week, but the bait's right, the water's right, and every August for the last several years the first fish have shown up right around this stretch of the calendar. If I had one afternoon to burn looking for them, I'd be running light spinning gear with small Deadly Dicks or Albie Snax off the Southeast Light rips, working the moving water where bait gets funneled and squeezed. It's a scouting mission more than a sure thing, but that's the fun of it this time of year.

Tide-wise, we're coming off a moon that's kept the current honest — good push through the rips, which the bass have been using to their advantage at night. Wind's been cooperative, mostly light out of the southwest, which keeps the water clean around the Ledge and the North Rip. If that holds through the weekend, I'd keep the program the same: eels and bucktails after dark for bass, metal on the blues whenever you see working birds, and a scouting run out toward the lighthouse rips if you've got the itch for something new on the line. The water's telling us summer's fully set in — deep bass, hungry blues, and a bonito arrival that feels like it's just days away, not weeks.
