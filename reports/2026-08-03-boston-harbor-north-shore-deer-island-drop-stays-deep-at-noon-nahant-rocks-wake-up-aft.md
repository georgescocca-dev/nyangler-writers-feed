# Deer Island Drop Stays Deep at Noon, Nahant Rocks Wake Up After Dark
*Harbor bass are feeding on schedule while the North Shore rockpile bite has shifted almost entirely to the low-light hours.*

**BOSTON HARBOR, MA — August 3** — _by Sara Whitfield, Zone Writer_

I made the call Monday to work the harbor islands first this week instead of running straight out to Nahant, and it was the right one. With the surface running warm everywhere south of the Cape, the fish up here haven't gone fully nocturnal — but they have gotten smart about the sun. Deer Island Light and the drop-off along President Roads are still giving up bass in daylight, just not in the top ten feet anymore. You want the edge where the flat drops from twenty into thirty-five feet on the outgoing tide, working a live mackerel on a three-way rig with just enough lead to hold bottom without hanging up on the rubble. That current seam off the Deer Island cut has been holding stripers from 28 to 38 inches all week, mixed with a scattering of overslot fish that come up once, thump the mackerel, and go straight back down into the ledge.

Spectacle Island's north side has been the sneaky producer — the drop into the shipping channel gives bass a highway to move bait through on the tide change, and a slow-trolled umbrella rig with mackerel-colored tubes has been drawing strikes in that 25 to 30 foot band starting an hour before the top of the flood. It's not fast fishing. You might make eight or ten passes before you connect, but the fish that come are quality, and there's enough bait stacked in the harbor right now — mackerel schools have been thick from Long Island Head clear down past Georges Island — that the bass have every reason to stay put instead of pushing out to open water.

Nahant is a different story right now, and it's worth being honest about it. Egg Rock and the Bass Point ledges were money at first light two weeks ago; this week the noon bite there has gone dead flat. The rocks are holding fish, but they've pulled into the deeper pockets and won't touch a topwater plug until the sun drops. If you want that classic North Shore surface bite — Rebel Jumping Minnows and Guppy poppers working the white water off the boulders — you're fishing the last hour of daylight into dark, or picking it up again at first light before the sun hits the water. Bluefish have moved into that same window, and they're not shy about it. A fresh wave has clearly pushed down out of the Gulf of Maine over the last week, because the blues showing up at Nahant and off Gloucester's back shore are bigger than what we had in June — three to five pounds, blitzing bait right up against the rocks at Bass Point and off Brace Cove. Poppers, metal, anything fast and loud will draw them up.

We flagged this pattern last week — said to expect steady conditions with no real disruption coming — and that's exactly what's played out. No fronts, no big temperature swing, just the harbor and North Shore settling into their August rhythm: bass deep and structure-bound at midday, bluefish and topwater bass at the margins of the day.

Up around Cape Ann, Halibut Point and the ledges off Rockport are holding the same mackerel-and-bass pattern as Nahant, just a few degrees cooler, which is buying an extra hour or two of daylight activity on the outer ledges. If you've got a boat that can handle the run out past the Dry Salvages, there are still schoolie-to-mid-30s bass working the edges where the bottom breaks from 40 to 60 feet, though you're better off jigging a diamond jig tipped with a strip of mackerel belly than trying to troll through that rocky bottom.

South of the harbor, Duxbury Bay and the Plymouth flats are running their own program entirely — flounder fishing has actually picked up nicely on the last two hours of the incoming tide. Sandworms and skimmer clams fished on a simple spreader rig off the bottom in eight to twelve feet inside Duxbury Bay have been steady, nothing huge, but a solid box of flounder for anyone willing to sit on the anchor through a tide change. Striper action in Duxbury and Kingston Bay has been quieter, mostly small fish working bait pods on the last of the ebb near the channel markers.

Tide-wise, we're building toward the new moon, and that's going to matter. Stronger current through the Deer Island cut and around President Roads should tighten up that drop-off bite and might finally push some of those Nahant rock fish back into a daytime pattern if the extra push stirs the bait. If I had one trip to make this coming week, I'd take the last two hours of an outgoing tide at Spectacle Island with live mackerel, then swing over to Bass Point right at last light with a popper rod rigged and ready. Watch the sky — any real cooling snap would only help, resetting some of that midday lockjaw we're seeing on the rocks. Until then, fish the tide changes and don't waste daylight where the fish have already told you they won't show.
