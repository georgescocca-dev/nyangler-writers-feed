# Race Point at first light: bass on the mackerel, cod holding deep on Peaked Hill
*The big fish slid off the flats and into the rips this week — you just have to be there before the sun clears the dunes.*

**PROVINCETOWN, MA — August 7** — _by Donny Costa, Zone Writer_

We were anchored up on the inside edge of the Race Point rip Tuesday morning, maybe 4:45, still dark enough that the loom of the light was doing more work than the sun. My sternman, a kid named Petey who's fished with me three summers now, dropped a live mackerel down on a fish-finder rig and didn't even get it to bottom before the rod buried. Thirty-four inch bass, fat as a football, came up thrashing in that gray light with the current just starting to run. That's been the story all week out there — you want to be on the water before the bay wakes up, because once the sun gets up over the dunes and that surface water warms into the mid-70s, the bigger fish drop off the structure and go quiet till dusk.

Thirty years drifting this bay and the pattern doesn't change much once we hit August — it just gets more particular about when. The bass haven't left, they've compressed. Race Point rip, the seam off Peaked Hill Bars, the drop-off on the northeast corner of Billingsgate — that's where the current concentrates the bait and the fish know it. We've been marking good pods of mackerel and sand eels stacking right on those rips at first light and again as the light fails in the evening, and the bass are using the moving water to pin bait against the structure rather than chasing it around open flats where it's too warm to bother.

Tide's been building back out of that soft neap stretch we had last week, and you can feel it — the rip off Race Point is starting to rip again instead of just nudging along. That's exactly what you want this time of year. A hard-running tide on a rip like that turns bait into confetti and gives the bass a reason to show themselves instead of holding deep and sulking. Best window has been the two hours either side of the top of the tide change, whether that's the last of the incoming building into slack or the first hour of the ebb pulling off Peaked Hill. Live mackerel on a three-way rig, fished just off bottom in eighteen to twenty-five feet, has been the ticket for the better fish. If you can't get mackerel, a big white or chartreuse tube-and-worm trolled slow through the same water has been drawing strikes too, though nothing beats a livie when they're up and feeding hard.

Bluefish have been mixed in with that same bait and honestly stealing a fair share of our mackerel before the bass get to them. We had a stretch Wednesday off the Race where the blues were slashing bait on the surface for a good twenty minutes — nothing giant, three to six pounds, but they hit like a freight train on anything shiny. Poppers and metal, Kastmasters and Deadly Dicks, worked fine when we could get in front of a surface show. We flagged a couple weeks back that peanut bunker moving into the shallows would pull cocktail blues into the back bay creeks, and that's exactly what's happening now — Barnstable Harbor and the marsh creeks behind Sandy Neck have snapper blues thick enough that kids on the docks are catching them on bare hooks. If you want light-tackle action with the family, that's your spot, incoming tide, small white bucktails or Kastmasters.

Fluke have mostly pushed off the shallow flats — too warm now on the inside of Barnstable Harbor and the flats around Sandy Neck during the day — and settled onto the deeper sand and gravel bottom out toward Billingsgate and the channel edges off Wellfleet. We've been picking at them drifting three-inch white Gulp Swimming Mullets on half-ounce bucktails in twenty-two to thirty feet, tipping with a strip of squid when the current's really moving. Nothing huge, a few keepers to five pounds mixed with a lot of shorts, but steady enough to fill a cooler if you're patient and cover ground.

Cod are a slower conversation right now — it's early yet for the real push, that usually doesn't build until the water cools off in the fall — but we have been marking fish holding tight to the deeper structure on Peaked Hill Bars, the humps out past forty feet where the bottom breaks up. Jigging diamond jigs and running clams on high-low rigs has produced a few keeper cod mixed with dogfish, which tells you the bigger schools haven't fully staged yet, but the structure's starting to hold life again.

Looking ahead, if this tide keeps building the way it has the past few days, I'd expect the rip bite at Race Point and Peaked Hill to sharpen up through the weekend — moving water is the whole engine of this fishery, and right now it's turning back on. I'd be out there at first light with live mackerel if you can get them, working the slack-to-flood transition hard, then shifting to the flats or Billingsgate for fluke once the sun's up and the rip bite shuts down. Keep an eye on the bunker pushing into the harbors too — wherever those pods thicken up, the blues won't be far behind, and that's usually good for an hour of chaos right off the dock.
