# Bay water pushing 79°F shoves the fluke into Shinnecock Inlet as spring tides build
*New moon currents are loading up this weekend — the outgoing at the inlet is where the smart money's fishing right now.*

**SHINNECOCK INLET, NY — July 8** — _by Charlie "Night Owl" Ortiz, Zone Writer_

I've been out here past midnight three of the last five nights and I can tell you what the thermometer's been telling everybody all week — the bay's cooked. Shinnecock Bay is sitting up near 78-79 degrees in the shallows, and that's too warm for a fluke to sit still in. They know it before we do. Every fluke that was spread out over the flats off Tiana and the flats behind Hampton Bays two weeks ago has pulled off into the channels, and a good chunk of them have already run for the inlet itself, where the ocean's pumping in water in the low 70s on the incoming. That's a real thermal wall — bay water baking, ocean water upwelled and cool — and it's stacking bait and fluke right where the two meet. That's the story of the week, and it's not subtle if you're paying attention to where the boats are anchoring up.

Tide-wise, we're building into a new moon — peak spring alignment lands right around July 11-13 — and that means bigger swings, faster water, and a harder pull through the inlet on the outgoing than we've seen since the June full moon. For me that's the whole ballgame. Bigger tides mean the current lane through the inlet gets stronger and longer, which pins bait against the jetty rocks and gives the bass and blues a reason to stack up instead of scattering. If you fish here regularly you know the pattern — neap tides make the bite lazy and spread out, springs make it violent and concentrated. This week's leaning toward violent. Wind's been mild out of the south-southwest most nights, which has kept the inlet clean, and that's a gift — dirty water on a spring tide is a mess to fish, clean water on a spring tide is when you load the cooler.

Striped bass have been the most consistent thing I can put my name behind this week, and it's strictly a night game right now. I've been running the outgoing starting about an hour before peak ebb, working bucktails tipped with pork rind and swimming plugs — SP Minnows and Danny-style swimmers — tight against the north jetty rocks where the current curls into an eddy. Fish have been mostly schoolies to mid-20s, but there've been a few nights with a couple of fish pushing 30-plus inches mixed in, especially in that dead-black hour after the tide really starts ripping. Live eels drifted on a fish-finder rig through the main channel have out-produced the plugs on the slower nights — if the current's not moving much yet, an eel swims itself into a strike zone better than anything I can cast.

Fluke is where the volume's been, and the inlet mouth and the deeper edges of the channel running out past the Coast Guard station are the spots right now. Boats drifting the outgoing with 3/4-ounce chartreuse bucktails tipped with white Gulp Swimming Mullet, working 15 to 25 feet of water, have been putting decent numbers in the box — nothing epic, but steady keeper-class fish in the 18-20 inch range with the occasional 4-pounder mixed in. The bay drift off Tiana still has fish on it if you want to stay shallow, but it's thinned out noticeably from three weeks ago, and I'd rather burn the gas to fish the inlet where the water's actually comfortable for them.

Weakfish at Ponquogue Bridge have been a mixed bag, and I want to be straight with you about that instead of blowing smoke. The bite's real but it's thin — a handful of solid fish, including at least one better than 7 pounds weighed in locally this month, but for every good report I hear about three quiet nights. I've had luck working the up-current side of the pilings on the last two hours of the outgoing with bucktails tipped with squid strips, fished slow and bounced off bottom, but you need to put in the time. This isn't the kind of bridge bite where you show up and it happens for you in twenty minutes. Treat it as a bonus fish while you're really there for bass or fluke.

Bluefish have shown up in flurries at the inlet on the last of the incoming, chopping up spearing and rainfish pushed against the rocks — poppers and small metal have worked when they're up, but it's been on-again-off-again, not a sustained blitz. Porgies and blackfish are around the usual structure — the rocks off the inlet and the reefs out toward Shinnecock proper — standard clam baits on a high-low rig, and that bite's been reliable if unremarkable, good for a mixed bag when the marquee species aren't cooperating.

One more thing worth watching — with the pelagic window still open through this weekend, there's been chatter about early bonito and Spanish mackerel showing on the ocean side, working the clean incoming water off the inlet and toward Moriches. I haven't connected myself yet, but the conditions line up — warm plume pushing in, clean water, bait present. If I had a morning free this week I'd have a small tin rigged and ready just in case they show inside casting range of the jetty.

Looking ahead: if the wind stays out of the south and doesn't kick up chop through the inlet, I like the outgoing tide Friday and Saturday night as the window to be there — the new moon spring should have that current ripping harder than it has all month, and that's when the bigger bass show themselves. Plan B if the bass don't cooperate is sliding into the fluke drift right at the inlet mouth on the same tide — you're already there, and the fish have already told us that's where they want to be.
