# Sandy Hook's Rip at First Light: Bass on Bunker, Fluke Sharpening on the Channel Edges
*The full moon is stacking current through the Hook this week, and that's the difference between a dead tide and a window worth setting an alarm for.*

**PERTH AMBOY, NJ — August 10** — _by Marisol Duarte, Zone Writer_

Here's the call I made this week and I'm sticking with it: dawn at the Rip, then run the channel edges on the back half of the tide. That's the whole game plan right now, and if you try to do both at the wrong hour you'll end up with nothing in the box.

The bay's been sitting mid-to-upper 70s through the day, and that's enough to shut the bass down once the sun gets up. What's kept them honest is the bunker. Pods have been holding tight along the Hook's rips — that seam where the ocean water meets the bay current — and the stripers are using them the same way they do every August: hit hard at first light while there's still low light and a temperature break, then drop back down and go quiet once the heat sets in. I've had two mornings this week where the bunker balled up tight off the Hook right at the top of the tide change, birds working, and it turned into a real window — bass to 34 inches on live bunker under a balloon, and a few more on bunker chunks fished on a fish-finder rig right on bottom in 15 to 20 feet. Once the sun climbed, it shut off like someone flipped a switch. If you're going, be there before the light, not after.

The full moon is doing exactly what it should — pushing more water and more current through that rip than we've seen in weeks. That's a double-edged thing. More current means more bait gets pinned and stacked, which is good, but it also means the window is shorter and sharper. I'd rather fish 45 minutes of hard current with fish stacked than three hours of slack water with nothing showing.

Fluke are where I'd point anybody who wants a more dependable day. The channel edges around Flynn's Knoll and down toward Romer Shoal have been the most consistent producers, and that full-moon tide swing is sharpening the drift bite right at the turns. I've been dragging 3/4-ounce white bucktails tipped with 4-inch chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullets along the drop-offs in 25 to 35 feet, slow and right on bottom, and getting quality fish — nothing enormous, but a steady stream of keepers in the 18 to 21-inch range with the occasional better one mixed in. The edges hold up because the water there stays a few degrees cooler than the flats around them, and this time of year that's the whole story with fluke — they're not spread out anymore, they're pinned to structure where it's comfortable. Keyport Flats has some fluke on it too but it's spottier; I'd treat it as a second stop, not the main event.

Inside the bay, porgies and blowfish are doing the heavy lifting on the slower days, and there's no shame in that — it's meat in the cooler while you wait for the tide to set up for bass or fluke. Bloodworms and clam on high-low rigs over the structure off Old Orchard Shoal and around the bridge pilings near Ward's Point has been steady, nothing spectacular but reliable, which is more than I can say for some of the other stuff this week.

We flagged the peanut bunker push into the back bays a couple weeks back, and it's showing up now — cocktail blues have gotten thick in the Navesink and Shrewsbury, chasing those peanuts around the mouths of the creeks and under the bridges. Small metal — half-ounce Deadly Dicks, Kastmasters — worked fast on the retrieve is all you need. It's light-tackle fun and a good option for an evening trip when the main bay bite is dead flat from the heat.

Weakfish, I'll be straight with you, haven't shown much in the bay proper this year. I keep hearing they should be tracking that same peanut bunker push, and maybe they will, but I haven't seen the numbers to back it up around here yet. If you're targeting them specifically, I wouldn't build a whole trip around it — treat any weakfish as a bonus while you're fishing for something else.

Looking ahead, the moon's still carrying extra push through the middle of this week, so I'd keep working the Hook at first light while that current stays strong — if the bunker schools hold their position out there instead of scattering, that early window should keep producing. Once we get a few days past full, the tide swings will settle back down and those bite windows will shrink back to normal length, so if you've got a morning free, I wouldn't wait on it. Keep an eye on the wind too — anything building out of the southwest midweek will put a chop on the Hook's outer edge and make that rip fishing miserable even if the bass are there. Inside the bay stays fishable in almost anything, which is exactly why the channel-edge fluke program is the safer bet if the weather turns.
