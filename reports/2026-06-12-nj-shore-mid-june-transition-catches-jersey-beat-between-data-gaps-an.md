# Mid-June transition catches Jersey beat between data gaps and bait shifts
*With primary offshore buoys offline and SST package two days stale, the Shore plays the calendar — Raritan winding down, ocean structure waking up.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — June 12, 2026** — _by Joey Napolitano, Zone Writer_

The honest lead today is that the analyst desk is flying with one engine. Buoy 44025 — the workhorse New York Bight reference twelve miles south of Islip and the station every Jersey captain cross-checks against Ambrose — is offline. Block Island's 44097 water temperature feed is intermittent and not giving a clean number. The latest published SST composite in the catalog is dated June 10, two days behind today's water. That means no defensible single-degree Fahrenheit reading I'm willing to print for the Hook or the Manasquan Ridge this morning. What I can say with confidence is what the calendar and the last clean SST frame told us: by the second week of June the Raritan complex is typically running in the mid-to-upper 60s, the nearshore ocean from Sea Girt to Barnegat is climbing through the low 60s, and Cape May's rips are already pushing 65. Without fresh buoy confirmation, treat those as bracketing assumptions, not gospel.

What that thermal bracket means for the beat is straightforward. The Raritan Bay spring striper show — the bunker-and-bass circus that defined April and May from the Reach Channel down to the Keansburg flats — is in its closing chapter. Once the bay pushes past 66, the big resident females slide out around the Hook and start working the three-mile line and the Shrewsbury Rocks on cooler ocean water. The Hook rips on the outgoing, particularly the back side of the ebb when the rip stacks against the southerly breeze, is where I'd be spending dawns this week. Inside the inlets, Manasquan and Shark River are squarely in fluke mode now, and the ocean fluke grounds from the Axel Carlson down through Barnegat Ridge should be holding fish on the drift in 40 to 55 feet.

The on-the-ground picture, synthesized from a quiet overnight intel cycle, is one of transition rather than a single dominant pattern. Bunker schools are still tractoring through the bay but the bass column on them is thinning and the fish that remain are increasingly post-spawn and lean — the trophy class is moving. Sea bass season is open and the wrecks and reef pieces from 17 Fathoms inshore to the Axel Carlson are the highest-percentage bottom trip on the board right now, with sand eels showing in the column and jigs outperforming clams when you mark fish stacked above the structure. Fluke reports out of Shark River and the Manasquan back have been steady on short-to-keeper ratios that favor patience — outgoing tide, bucktail-and-Gulp, the deeper holes off the railroad bridge.

Down the southern end of the beat, Cape May and the Delaware rips are the sleeper play. When the northern bays warm past comfort, the rips off Cape May Point and the mouth of the Delaware become the most reliable striper structure on the Jersey coast through late June — moving water, tide-line bait, and slot fish that will eat a bucktail or a live spot. Weakfish should also be a real conversation in Delaware Bay and the Great Bay sedges right now; the back-bay grass edges on the top of the incoming with a pink shad or a small swimmer is the classic play, and the second week of June is historically when the better tiderunners show.

Looking three to five days out, the controlling variable is wind. A sustained southerly stacks warm surface water against the Jersey beach and accelerates the inside-bay thermal exit — meaning the Raritan window closes faster. A northeast pulse, even a modest one, drags cooler slope water shoreward and can re-set the inlet bite for another week. I want eyes on Sandy Hook's tide-gauge water temperature trend and the first clean 44025 reading whenever NDBC brings it back online; a jump through 68 inside the bay is the trigger to abandon the Reach and commit to the Hook rips and the Shrewsbury Rocks full-time. New moon is not in play this week, so tide ranges are moderate — favor the inlets on the back half of the ebb rather than the slack-water windows.

The call for the next several tides: fluke the inlets and the 50-foot ocean line on the drift, jig sea bass on the inshore wrecks before the July one-fish limit clamps down, work the Hook outgoing at first light for the last of the bay bass, and if you've got the fuel, the Cape May rips are the most underrated striper bite in the state right now. I'll have firmer numbers when the buoy array and the SST pipeline catch up.
