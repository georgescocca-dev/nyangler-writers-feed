# Bass blitz fires the Norwalk Islands as new moon tides push bunker through
*White bucktails and live eels producing keeper stripers to 30 pounds in the rips.*

**NORWALK, CT — June 23** — _by David "The Harbor Scout" Han, Zone Writer_

The new moon spring tides that peaked Friday have turned the Central Sound into a bass highway, and the fish are finally showing up where they should be this time of year. I've been watching the bait movements for weeks, and the pieces are finally clicking into place.

The Norwalk Islands have been the epicenter of the action. Working the back sides of the islands on the outgoing tide, I'm seeing consistent bass in the 24 to 30-inch range, with a few pushing over the slot. The key has been positioning on the down-current side of the structure where the tide sweeps bait off the rocks. White bucktails in the 1 to 1.5-ounce range are absolutely deadly — cast up-current, let them sink to bottom contact, then work them back with slow lifts and drops. The fish are hitting on the fall every time.

What's driving this bite is the massive bunker push that started moving through the Sound last week. These aren't the peanuts we usually see — we're talking adult bunker in the 8 to 10-inch range, and they're thick from Stamford all the way east to the Connecticut River. The new moon tides are flushing them out of the harbors and concentrating them along the drop-offs and channel edges.

Live eels under planer boards have been the ticket for the bigger fish. I watched one crew working the deeper water north of Charles Island pull three fish over 28 inches in two hours, all on eels fished tight to the 40-foot contour. The technique is simple but deadly effective — run the eel about 50 feet behind the planer board, let it work naturally in the current, and be ready for that distinctive thump when a big bass inhales it.

The Stratford Shoal area has been producing mixed results. The rip itself has been too violent to fish effectively with the big spring tides, but the edges are holding fish. I've been working the 30 to 35-foot depths on both sides of the main channel, and the bass are stacked up waiting for bait to get swept through. Chartreuse and white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails have been consistent producers here.

Fluke fishing has been solid but not spectacular. The Middle Ground Light area continues to produce keeper fish in the 18 to 22-inch range, with the occasional doormat mixed in. The fish are holding in 25 to 35 feet of water, and they want their baits moving. I've been having success dragging 5-inch white Gulp baits on pink bucktail heads, keeping them just off the bottom on a slow drift.

Porgies are everywhere right now, which is both a blessing and a curse. The blessing is that they're big — I'm seeing fish in the 12 to 14-inch range that make excellent table fare. The curse is that they're so aggressive they'll grab your bait before the fluke can get to it. If you're specifically targeting porgies, small pieces of clam or squid on a high-low rig will fill the cooler quickly.

The water temperature has been the key to understanding fish behavior this week. Surface temps are running 68 to 70 degrees, but there's a distinct thermocline setting up around 35 to 40 feet where the water drops to the low 60s. This temperature break is acting like a magnet for bait, and consequently for the fish that feed on them.

Current speed has been crucial with these big tides. The new moon is pushing water hard through the Sound, and timing your fishing to the tide stages is critical. I've found the best bass fishing comes in the first two hours of the outgoing tide when the current is moving but not ripping. Once it really starts screaming, the fish seem to hunker down and wait it out.

Looking ahead to this week, we're moving away from the new moon, so the tides will start to moderate. This should make some of the more current-sensitive spots fishable again. I'm particularly excited about the potential at some of the deeper structure that's been unfishable with the big water movement. The bunker schools should continue to move through, and as the current eases, I expect the bass bite to spread out to more areas. Water temperatures are climbing steadily, which should also bring the summer fluke bite into full swing.
