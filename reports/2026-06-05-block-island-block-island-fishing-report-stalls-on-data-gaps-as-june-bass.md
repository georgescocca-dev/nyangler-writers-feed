# Block Island fishing report stalls on data gaps as June bass migration peaks
*Analyst pipeline is short on live buoy and SST inputs today, but seasonal timing puts the heart of the striped bass push squarely on the island's rips.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — June 5** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

The oceanographic desk is running thin today. The analyst package arrived without live buoy readings for the Block Island corner — no current surface temperature off Southwest Ledge, no fresh number from the Newport side of Narragansett Bay, no updated wind or sea-state snapshot for the southeast approaches. The SST imagery catalog tops out at June 3, meaning the past 48 hours of thermal evolution across the island's southwest quadrant is not verified. Tide station data is similarly thin for this run. That is the honest dateline: we are reasoning from seasonal mechanism and recent pattern rather than from this morning's instruments.

What the calendar tells us is unambiguous. Early June is when the island earns its reputation. The migratory body of striped bass that staged through Montauk and the Rhode Island south shore in mid-to-late May is now arriving on the island's structure in numbers, drawn by the same physics every year — cold, oxygen-rich water spilling around the rips, abundant sand eels on the southwest grounds, and squid still hanging in the deeper edges. Without a confirmed SST, I would estimate surface temperatures around the island sitting in the high fifties to very low sixties based on the June 3 imagery and typical early-June warming, which is exactly the band that concentrates overslot fish on the Ledge before they push deeper for the summer thermocline pattern.

The structure to fish right now is the Southwest Ledge on the dropping tide, and that is not a guess — it is what the water column does in the first week of June every year the bait shows up on time. The west rip forms hardest about two hours into the ebb, and that is the window when wire-line trollers running tube-and-worm or big single-hook bucktails along the 60-to-90-foot break tend to find the heaviest class of fish. Live eels drifted along the same edge after dark have been the trophy method here for decades and there is no reason to think this week breaks the pattern. The North Rip is the morning play on the flood, particularly the last two hours before slack, with bucktails and soft plastics worked through the standing waves where the bait stacks.

Inside the bay and around Newport, the story is different. Background reports through last summer and fall confirmed what is already setting up now — the blackfish closure is in effect, but sea bass have opened and the structure from the Newport Bridge south through the lower bay is holding fish on the ledges and wrecks. Porgies should be thick on the Brenton Reef line and the rockpiles off Point Judith by this point in June, and fluke drifting on the sand south of Point Judith and along the Watch Hill reefs is in the productive window, though the better grade of summer fluke typically does not consolidate on the southwest grounds of the island until later in the month. Bluefish reports have been scattered along the Rhode Island coast; when they show on the island it tends to be in waves keyed to bait, and surface activity at the rips is the tell.

Looking ahead three to five days, the variable that matters most is wind. The island fishes hard south and southwest in June, and any sustained southwest blow over 15 knots will both stack bait against the Ledge and make the rips dangerous to work — that is the trade. A backing northwest wind cleans the water and flattens the southwest corner, which is when the wire-line fleet does its damage. The new moon is past us and we are building toward the full moon mid-month, which means tides will strengthen through the period and the rips will run harder each day. Bonito are still two to four weeks out under any normal year, so anyone running offshore for them is early. I would be watching for the first credible reports of squid thinning on the southeast side, because that is the signal the bass shift fully to sand eels and the daytime jig bite on the Ledge turns on in earnest.

The instruments are quiet today but the calendar is loud. The fish are here or arriving, the Ledge is the address, and the dropping tide is the appointment. Anyone with a seaworthy boat and a bucket of eels should be making the run.
