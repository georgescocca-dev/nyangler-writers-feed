# 71.2°F at the Bight buoy, but the canyon edge is running 6-8 degrees warmer — that's your yellowfin water
*A stable high has held the Hudson's warm-core structure in place for a full week, and the tuna numbers reflect it.*

**MANASQUAN, NJ — July 24** — _by Capt. Tony Rosetti, Zone Writer_

71.2 degrees. That's what 44025 was reading out in the Bight earlier this week, which tells you almost nothing about what's happening on the canyon edge — and everything about why I run 100-plus miles instead of fishing the inshore lumps. The Hudson wall itself has been holding surface temps in the upper 70s to right around 80 along the break, which means we're carrying a 7-to-9 degree gradient from the Bight buoy out to the 100-fathom line. That's the number that matters this week. When you see a spread that wide with settled high pressure sitting on top of it, you get warm-core water pinned against the canyon structure instead of getting pushed around by wind-driven mixing, and that's exactly the setup that's been parked over the Hudson for going on ten days now.

We called this direction last week — said the stable pattern was quietly building one of the better yellowfin and bigeye setups of the season, and that's precisely what showed up. I ran out of Manasquan Tuesday, put the spread in the water at the 90-fathom curve on the northeast side of the canyon, around 39°32' N, 72°38' W, and had color change and a hard temp break within the first half mile of trolling — you could see it in the water itself, that blue-to-green transition with a visible slick line of foam and debris riding the edge. Yellowfin were stacked on the warm side of that break, 68 to 76 degrees on the surface reading, and we boated six inside of three hours on a standard spread: Green Machines and small Islanders in the shotgun and long rigger, ballyhoo naked and rigged behind a bird on the short corner. Fish ran 20 to 35 pounds, nothing giant, but steady enough that we never had more than a 20-minute lull between strikes.

The better fish came off the chunk once the sun got high and the troll bite went quiet, which is the pattern I've seen play out at the Hudson for fifteen years now — troll early and late, chunk through the heat of the day. We anchored up on the edge of the Dip in 700 feet, butterflied fresh bunker on 60-pound fluoro leader, circle hooks, and let it soak with the chunks drifting on a slow current. That's where we picked up a 45-pound bigeye around 1:30 in the afternoon, deep bite, rod bent double on 50-pound stand-up gear. Mahi showed up opportunistically around a scattered weed line south of the Claw — nothing major, a half dozen schoolies in the 5-to-10 pound class, but they'll eat a small skirted lure or a chunk bait if you're marking floating structure and want to burn twenty minutes on the way out or back.

White marlin have been the wildcard. We raised two on Tuesday's run, neither one committed, both came up on the long rigger ballyhoo and just looked at it before sliding off. That's typical for this stage of the season — the fish are around, the water's right, but they're not eating with any consistency yet. If you want a shot at one, I'd downsize the leader to 20-pound fluoro and pull naked ballyhoo rather than rigged, and have a pitch bait ready the moment you see one show color behind the spread.

Wahoo have been scarcer than I'd like given the water temps, but one of the other boats working the 100-fathom line south of us picked off a decent one, mid-30s, on a high-speed lure pulled at 12 knots through a temp seam near the Texas Tower drop-off. That's a program I'd try again if I had a dedicated wahoo day — those fish like a hard break and a fast presentation, and the Tower area has been holding the kind of structure and current edge that sets that up.

Bluefin have been more of a Block Island and Massachusetts story than a Hudson story this week — the slot fishery up there is grinding steady post-closure, but down here they've been incidental, a couple jumped off the troll spread and one 60-pounder gaffed on the chunk Tuesday afternoon. Not the primary target, but worth having a heavier stand-up rod rigged in case one shows.

Here's my honest read: this is a good bite, not a great one. Six yellowfin and a bigeye in a full day is solid work for late July, but it's not the kind of numbers day where you're boxing fish every fifteen minutes. The fish are there because the water's set up right — that wide temp gradient between the coast and the canyon is doing exactly what it should, holding bait and tuna against structure instead of letting it scatter. But you still have to find the exact edge, and it's moved a few miles east since last week as the eddy shifts with the Gulf Stream's meander.

Looking ahead, if this high pressure holds through the weekend — and right now there's no reason to think it won't — I'd expect the yellowfin bite to hold or even improve slightly as more bait gets pushed up against that warm-core structure. I'd run straight to the northeast side of the canyon first, check your surface temp against that 76-78 degree window, and if you're not seeing bait or birds within the first hour, slide south toward the Claw where the bottom contour tends to funnel current and hold structure tighter. Bring gear for both troll and chunk — you'll want both before the day's out.
