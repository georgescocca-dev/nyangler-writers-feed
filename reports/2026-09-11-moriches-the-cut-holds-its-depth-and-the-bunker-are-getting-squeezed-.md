# The Cut Holds Its Depth — and the Bunker Are Getting Squeezed Against It
*New-moon tides tightened bait against the inlet's east wall this week, and the first real striper commitments of the fall are showing up right on the seam.*

**MORICHES INLET, NY — September 11** — _by Pete Gallo, Zone Writer_

The east cut at Moriches Inlet has held its depth through the summer better than it has in a few years — no major shoaling event since the spring nor'easters reworked the bar, and that stability is exactly why this week's bunker push had somewhere to go. When the new moon on the 10th brought the strongest tidal exchange of the early fall, the bait didn't scatter into open water like it does over a flatter, less defined channel. It got pinned against that east wall, funneled by the same deep groove that's been carrying the ebb current since the last big cut reshaped the inlet's throat. We flagged this pattern last week — said the building moon would tighten bunker schools through the system — and that's exactly what's playing out now.

The result is the first legitimate blitz activity of the fall run showing up on the ocean side of Cupsogue and along the bar off Tuthill Point. It's not a five-day-a-week event yet, but the tide stages that matter are consistent: the last two hours of the ebb, when the bay is emptying hard through the inlet and bunker get squeezed into the throat, and the first hour of the incoming, when predators push back in on the new water. Boats working the inlet mouth and surfcasters walking the ocean beach at Cupsogue are both reporting the same window — fish showing on top for twenty, thirty minutes, then going quiet until the tide turns again.

Striped bass are the story, and they're mostly slot-class and up, not the shorts that dominated earlier in the season. That tracks with what's been reported from Montauk on down — this fall run is arriving heavy on mature fish, light on juveniles, which means fewer fish overall but better average size. Bucktails in the 1.5 to 2-ounce range, white or bunker-colored, worked with a slow bottom retrieve along the current seam on the ocean side of the inlet, have been the most consistent producer. When bass are visibly working bunker on the surface, a straight-retrieve bunker spoon or a topwater popper thrown into the boil outproduces anything fished blind. The bite dies fast once the surface activity stops, so the read is: fish the visible sign hard, don't waste time working dead water waiting for it to start again.

Bluefish are backing up the bass story from underneath. Snapper blues are thick in the back bay — East Moriches and around Tuthill Point, small enough that they're mostly a kids-with-bobbers fishery right now, but their density is the leading indicator for adult blues showing up in bigger numbers as the water keeps cooling. That progression already happened at Montauk, where a nearly 18.5-pound bluefish took a derby prize this week, and the same cooling trend is working its way down the South Shore. Expect adult blues to start mixing into the bass blitzes at the inlet within the next week to ten days, likely first showing on bunker spoons and diamond jigs fished on the drop right in the inlet throat where the current is running hardest.

Fluke is the one piece of this report that isn't trending up. The keeper-grade bite has largely pulled out of the bay entirely — bigger fish staged toward deeper, cooler structure well before this, and what's left inside Moriches Bay and off Cupsogue is mostly undersized fish holding in the shallows longer than the fish that already made the move offshore. That's the same pattern showing up across the mid-Atlantic bays right now, and there's no reason to expect it to reverse. If fluke is the target, the smart money is on a short window before the last of the keepers slide out, working white Gulp Swimming Mullets on 3/4-ounce bucktails along the channel edges near the inlet on the last of the outgoing, where current still concentrates bait tight to the bottom contour.

Weakfish have been quiet — no strong signal either way this week, which for early September isn't unusual as that fishery has thinned out generally on the South Shore. Porgies are still biting steady on the deeper bay structure around Tuthill Point and the channel edges toward Smith Point, bloodworms and clam on standard bottom rigs, a reliable if unglamorous producer while the bigger story plays out at the inlet. Blackfish season hasn't kicked in yet — too early, water's still carrying enough warmth that the wrecks and structure bite won't be worth chasing for a few more weeks.

Looking at the next 48 to 72 hours: the tidal push from the new moon is still working through the system, and that means the bait-squeeze pattern at the inlet should hold or even intensify before it eases. Offshore sea state has been rough — better than 3.5 feet of swell pushing against the ocean beaches — which is actually helping the inside pattern by keeping bait from spreading out along the open coast and forcing it to concentrate at the protected structure around the inlet mouth. If the ocean lays down even slightly, watch the Cupsogue rip and the bar off Tuthill Point for the blitz window to extend past that thirty-minute burst into something more sustained. If it stays rough, stick to the inlet throat itself on the last two hours of the ebb — that's where the depth and current are doing the work regardless of what the open ocean is doing outside.
