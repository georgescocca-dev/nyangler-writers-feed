# Peconic porgies stay thick on the reefs while bass slide into the night shift
*Warm bay water has scup piled up around Shelter Island and pushed the stripers onto a dawn-and-dark schedule — here's where to find both.*

**GREENPORT, NY — July 24** — _by Bill Hubbard, Zone Writer_

Late July on the Peconic follows a clock I've watched turn over for going on three decades now, and this year it's right on schedule. The bay warmed early back in the spring like it always does, got the jump on the Sound, brought the bass in ahead of everybody else — and now, in the thick of summer, it's paying us back with the flip side of that coin. Surface water sitting in the mid-70s across the open bay means the stripers have gone the way they do every year about now: they've slid off the flats and pushed down onto structure, feeding hardest in the dark hours and in that gray window right around first light. If you're used to daytime bass fishing in the Peconic, this is the week to adjust the alarm clock instead.

The porgy fishing, though, hasn't skipped a beat, and honestly it's the most dependable thing going on the whole bay right now. Shelter Island remains the epicenter — the reef off Cornelius Point, the drop-off outside Coecles Harbor, and the rock piles between there and Jessups Neck are holding scup in the kind of numbers that make you forget how slow other things can get in mid-summer heat. Anchoring up in 20 to 35 feet on the last two hours of the incoming or the first two of the outgoing has been the move — you want a little current moving bait around, not a dead slack. Small hooks, size 6 to 4, tipped with sandworm or squid strips, fished right on bottom with just enough weight to hold. Fish are running a good average size this year, plenty in the 12 to 14 inch class mixed with the usual pan-sized scup that make for a great fry.

Fluke are the more frustrating story, and I'll be straight about it — the shorts are everywhere and the keepers are making you work. That's classic mid-summer behavior once the shallow water gets uncomfortable for them. The better fish have moved into the deeper channel edges — the cut off Nassau Point, the deeper stretch of Noyack Bay, and the channel running out toward Jessups Neck all held quality fish this week when the drift lined up right. White or chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullet on a 3/4-ounce bucktail, dragged slow along bottom on the stronger half of the tide, has been out-producing bait-only rigs when the current's really moving. We're coming off the first-quarter neap that hit around July 21, and I flagged this last week — said that softer current through the bay's connecting cuts would be a good window to slow presentations down for bonus fluke and blackfish, and that's exactly how it played out. Guys working the channels slow and patient through the neap picked off some nicer fish than the tide strength alone would suggest.

Blackfish season's still a ways off officially opening back up for most of you, but the tautog are around the same structure holding those bonus fluke — Orient Point rock piles, the rips off Plum Gut, anywhere there's a mussel-covered ledge in 15 to 25 feet. Worth remembering when the season does turn over.

As for the bass, your best bet right now is fishing the edges of darkness rather than the middle of the day. Plum Gut at first light, working bucktails and swim shad down deep through the rip as the tide starts to turn, has produced some solid fish in that 28 to 34 inch range — not the numbers you'd get in the spring run, but quality single fish worth the early wakeup. Three Mile Harbor and the mouth of Sag Harbor Cove have both had night action on live eels and bunker chunks fished on the bottom, with the better bite coming on the last two hours of the outgoing when bait gets pulled out of the shallows and stripers position themselves right at the mouth to ambush it. Bluefish have been mixed in wherever there's current and bait — nothing like the blitz activity happening out west along the ocean beaches, but enough choppers around the Gut and Orient Point to keep things interesting if you're bouncing metal or diamond jigs through moving water.

Looking ahead, I want to flag something we've been watching for — warm water continuing to push in from the ocean side through Plum Gut and the Race should start bringing the first bonito and albie sign into the bay's eastern approaches over the next week or two. We called that shift coming, and with water temps holding steady in the upper 60s to low 70s pushing in from Block Island Sound, Orient Point and the outer edges near Plum Gut are exactly where I'd keep an eye out first. Nothing confirmed yet close to home, but the ingredients are lining up.

For the coming week, I'd split my time between early-morning bass at Plum Gut and Three Mile Harbor before the sun gets high, then switch over to porgies around Shelter Island through the middle of the day when that bite is most dependable regardless of what the bass are doing. If the wind stays cooperative — and this week it looks like it will — the deeper channels off Nassau Point and Noyack are worth another look for fluke on the stronger tide stages. Patience with the fluke, persistence with the bass after dark, and the porgies will bail you out most afternoons if nothing else is cooperating.
