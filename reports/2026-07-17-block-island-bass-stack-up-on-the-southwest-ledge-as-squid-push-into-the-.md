# Bass Stack Up on the Southwest Ledge as Squid Push Into the Rips
*Warm water off the Ledge and a strengthening fluke bite give Block Island two good reasons to make the run this week.*

**BLOCK ISLAND, RI — July 17** — _by Jack Sullivan, Zone Writer_

Last week the water around the island did something I like to see in mid-July — it held steady instead of crashing on us. We didn't get any big blow-through fronts, just a run of southwest breeze in the 10 to 15 knot range that kept the surface a little bumpy on the Southwest Ledge but never shut down the drift. Montauk was reading around 70 degrees, and the island's water tracks close to that number this time of year, maybe a touch warmer in the shallower rips where the sun's had all day to work on it. That's comfortable water for stripers, not the kind of heat that pushes them deep and sour. The bigger story last week was squid. Reports out of the Rhode Island and Nantucket Sound side have those concentrations building, and when squid show up in numbers around here, the rips start percolating — bass and blues key on them hard, and it's usually the first real signal that the summer pattern is locking in around the island.

This week I'm watching the tide swings more than the sky. We're coming off a moon phase that's had the currents running with authority through the North Rip and off the Ledge, and that kind of push is exactly what stacks bait against structure instead of letting it spread out over open water. As the moon eases toward the next quarter, expect the current to soften some days out and firm back up on others — when it slackens, don't stop fishing, just shift your approach. Slower current means working structure closer, not blind-casting the middle of the rip. Wind's forecast to stay in the southwest quadrant most of the week, which is livable for boat control off the Ledge but will put a chop on the surface during afternoon sea breeze — mornings and evenings are your window if you want it glass-calm.

The stripers have been on the Southwest Ledge in respectable numbers, holding in that 25 to 40-foot zone where the ledge drops off into the deeper water. I had a 40-pounder off the North Rip two weeks back on a live eel worked slow along bottom on the last two hours of the outgoing tide, and that pattern's held — eels drifted on a fish-finder rig or a three-way with just enough weight to stay down without dragging bottom too hard. If you're not an eel guy, umbrella rigs trolled slow through 30 to 50 feet of water off the Ledge have been putting fish in the box too, especially right at first light before the boat traffic picks up. The bite isn't a guarantee every trip — I've had mornings out there where three boats worked the same water and only one connected — but when the tide's moving right and there's bait showing on the machine, it's some of the best striper water on the whole coast right now.

Fluke have strengthened noticeably, which lines up with what I'm hearing from other boats working the drift between the Southwest Ledge and the inside grounds off Old Harbor. Four and five-pound fish aren't uncommon, and I heard of a couple pushing toward seven off a drift in 35 feet with a chartreuse Gulp Swimming Mullet on a bucktail, dragged slow with the drift rather than worked hard. Squid strips on a standard fluke rig are still producing too, especially in that push of squid that's moved into the area — matching the hatch matters right now. If you're marking bait on structure but not connecting, slow the drift down. These fish are eating, but they're not chasing far for it with the water this comfortable.

Bluefish have been mixed — some days the harbor mouth and the rips off the north end have choppers in the 3 to 6-pound range slashing bait on top, other days they're absent entirely and it's all bass and fluke. When they're around, don't overthink it — a diamond jig worked with a fast retrieve or poppers at first light will draw strikes. Porgies remain a reliable backup if the bigger stuff isn't cooperating — anchor up on the rocky bottom off Southwest Point or inside the harbor breachway, small hooks, sea worms or clam, and you'll fill a cooler even on a slow bass day.

Looking ahead, I want to see how that squid push develops. If it keeps building through this week the way the regional signs suggest, the rips off the island should only get better — squid draw bait, bait draws bass, and bass on squid tend to feed hard and predictably on the tide change. My plan is to be out on the Ledge during the last two hours of the outgoing on the days the wind lets me, working eels and umbrella rigs, then shifting to the fluke drift midday when the sun's high and the bass go quiet. If the bass bite gets tougher with the softening current later in the week, I wouldn't be surprised to see more boats concentrate right on the North Rip where the depth change forces bait to hold regardless of tide strength. That's always been the fallback out here, and it rarely lets you down completely — even on a slow day, the Rip usually gives you something worth the ride.
